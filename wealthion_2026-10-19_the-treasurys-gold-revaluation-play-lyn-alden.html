Fiscal dominance: deficits now drive money creation, so Fed tools miss

Annual deficits now exceed net new bank loans plus corporate bonds, so rate policy is solving a different problem.

  • Fed tools (rates, balance sheet) work through the bank-lending channel, which drove money growth in Paul Volcker's era but is now smaller than the deficit channel.
  • Net new Treasuries issued over 12 months are bigger than the sum of net new bank loans and net new corporate bond issuance.
  • Higher rates also raise the deficit, and cash-rich holders (money market funds, locked-in mortgages, her own company's capital) get 'a raise' when the Fed hikes.
  • At roughly 100% debt-to-GDP, the question is how the Fed communicates it isn't its purview; central banks are built around the idea that they can solve this.
  • Energy matters: a war ending and refineries no longer being hit would mildly lower inflation, but the K-shaped economy and above-target inflation predate the war.
  • AI power and resource demand also crowd out homebuilding, keeping home prices high despite higher rates.
  • 'When does the debt matter?' She says it has been mattering: the cost lands on non-recipients like a family buying a first home.

Revaluing Treasury gold is a 'break the glass' lever

Marking the official gold stock to market would fill the Treasury General Account with spendable cash without issuing debt.

  • The host cites Bessent's recent counselors (including Judy Shelton, a known sound-money figure) and Hank Paulson calling this a 'break the glass' moment as fuel for speculation.
  • Mechanism: official books carry gold at 40-something dollars an ounce; revaluing to roughly $4,000+ an ounce creates cash the Treasury can spend until it's used up.
  • It's mostly an accounting trick at first, but spending the cash is pro-liquidity and inflationary, comparable to post-COVID stimulus depending on magnitude.
  • Winners: gold, equity and Bitcoin holders (scarce assets); losers: wage earners years later, unless offset by fiscal policy beneficial to them.
  • Trade-off: it weakens the dollar (if that's the intent) but also hits wages; every lever helps one group and hurts another, so officials may kick it to the next team.

AI buildout: valid red flags, but the reckoning is later

Lease obligations and circular financing are real risks; she doesn't put them in subprime territory yet.

  • Hyperscalers are taking on increasing long-term lease obligations; chipmakers help raise funds and backstop data-center buildouts (vendor financing, a circular structure).
  • Frontier labs are chased by open-weight models: 'very hard to justify a trillion or two trillion dollar valuation' while aggressively unprofitable and only months ahead of copiers.
  • Things outside the banking system tend to be less catastrophic than subprime; she expects pretty big drawdowns at certain points.
  • Her indicator: are businesses spending more or less on AI than months ago; headlines of pullbacks keep appearing while aggregate spend keeps rising.
  • Studies show higher AI adopters win share and often increase headcount; open question is useful life of a leading-edge GPU.
  • Open source: she says trying to ban open-weight competitors through regulation is 'whack-a-mole' and a losing game; open models let more than a few companies control the tech.

Demographics, a K-shaped economy and algorithmic trust erosion

These problems come in clusters; the two-tier economy can last longer than people think but risks breaking toward the extremes.

  • Demographics: systems assume each generation is bigger; six workers per retiree vs three; Social Security, Medicare, Japan, Europe and China are all exposed.
  • Geopolitics shifting from unipolar back toward multipolar, with breakdowns in international law.
  • Record high stock prices alongside record low consumer sentiment: a divide between asset holders and deficit recipients versus non-asset holders and non-recipients.
  • The aristocracy sustained such divides for a very long time, but the risk is horseshoe politics; the host notes the midterms as a test.
  • A handful of big platforms tune algorithms for profit or other aims; she and Jack Dorsey discussed choosing your own algorithm via decentralized social media.
  • Video as proof and trust in institutions have fallen, so people shrink their world back to local; her sci-fi imagines a centralized censored internet plus edges.
  • AI as equalizer: it's never been easier to start a small business, if you avoid 'AI brain'.

Orange Juice: a Berkshire-style permanent-capital buyer with a Bitcoin treasury

It buys small and mid-size businesses to hold indefinitely, pitched as the alternative to private equity's flip-it playbook.

  • She says it's not a Bitcoin treasury company but a company that has a Bitcoin treasury as part of its strategy.
  • Private equity critique: buy, leverage on cheap debt, cut staff, flip in a few years; a 10-year fund mandate pulls profits forward and it ends with a larger-than-average failure rate.
  • Permanent capital (Berkshire Hathaway is the example, funded by insurance) is geared to decades of cash flows; Bitcoin on the balance sheet can be 'rocket fuel' if managed appropriately.
  • Sellers: owners who like Bitcoin and want a legacy, and owners who want liquidity but keep working; hundreds of companies have reached out.
  • A new CTO hired from Nvidia advises acquired firms on AI to reduce busy work, not cut back-end staff.
  • Counter-cyclical edge: pure Bitcoin-treasury companies like Strategy tend to buy Bitcoin at highs and can't raise capital in bear markets; Orange Juice can lean on its acquisition funnel instead.
