Affirm: pay over time with no late fees, no revolving debt, no fine print

Affirm is a third way between the debit card and the revolving credit card: every purchase becomes a fixed, pre-priced plan with nothing that compounds or surprises.

  • Example: a baby pram paid at $100 a month for six months, then done. Interest, if any, is fixed in dollars up front, and late payers just may not get credit next time.
  • Bankers laughed: 'half the profit comes from late fees', and you want borrowers making minimum payments forever. VCs asked why customers don't just use a platinum Amex.
  • Now at three-quarters of US and Canadian e-commerce checkouts, expanding to the UK and several European countries. Almost $50B in volume this year; publicly traded, profitable, and 30%+ year-over-year growth for the last 10 quarters.
  • Never a penny of late fees or revolving interest. Its share of the ~$1.3T in US credit card debt is still 'a footnote'.
  • Origin story: right after PayPal's IPO, he and Luke Nosek went to buy matching Mercedes hardtop convertibles in LA. Nosek got a loan; Levchin's credit 'sucks' — he'd missed three payments at 18 — so he had to wire the full price.
  • He had arrived with $600 for a family of five and was independently wealthy within about seven years, yet his credit score saw none of it. So he built a better score with a U of I friend, and had to start lending to prove it.
  • Card traps he found: a $1,000 draw becoming $3,000 of debt, and '0%' loans that retroactively compound if you're a dollar short or a minute late.

Two lucky bets, consistent losses, and AI agents that remove the friction

Affirm's edge is underwriting, and agentic commerce is about to erase the one cost of its model: the extra friction compared with tap-and-go.

  • Luck one: a study of ~10,000 millennials found ~70-78% hate banks. His read: they were early teens in 2008, watching banks take their family's house, so they'd try anything else.
  • Luck two: brilliant mathematicians avoid lending because it's embarrassing at cocktail parties ('I make my profits on late fees'). Strip out the gunk and they come — some Affirm underwriters have stayed 10-12 years.
  • Underwriting means pricing risk in real time, not saying yes or no. The average loan half-life is ~5 months, and Affirm's loss and delinquency numbers are 'really, really consistent'.
  • Cards are 'the single best financial interface ever created'. BNPL adds friction, offering certainty and control instead — people no longer trust what happens after they tap.
  • Agents will remove that friction. Instacart and DoorDash orders are already agentic for low-stakes buys, and an Indian service delivers in 6 minutes. But for $500-$10,000 purchases, normal people want to deliberate.
  • His near future: tell your chatbot 'a beautiful Italian bike with Shimano components, no late fees, ideally no interest'. Agents comparison-shop, and merchants fund 0% plans through Affirm.
  • The job apocalypse is 'so goofy': there's endless software to build. The Claude Code moment last December was the latest 'whoa'. Next he wants ultrasound brain-computer interfaces that read your mind and output code.

Personal story SUMMER 1998cryptofinance

DigiCash died of bad UI; PayPal was built while reading Cryptonomicon

Levchin's cryptography obsession met Peter Thiel the same summer DigiCash went bankrupt, and he took away one lesson the crypto crowd rejected: the interface matters.

  • Luke Nosek was a year ahead of him at the University of Illinois (Levchin graduated in 1997); they did failed startups together. Levchin studied David Chaum's work; DigiCash, 'the granddaddy of all cryptocurrencies', went bankrupt in summer 1998.
  • At DigiCash's wake on Stanford's picnic grounds, the crowd said the world wasn't ready. He said the user interface sucks — nobody waits for a slow RSA signature to buy coffee — and got called a heathen.
  • Days later he met Peter Thiel at a Stanford lecture, with Nosek as the connection. At the Financial Cryptography conference in Anguilla, he's '99% confident' Satoshi was attending too.
  • Years into PayPal he presented 'I was there when DigiCash failed, it was the UI'; the audience booed.
  • Six people, three coding 24/7, breaking only to read Neal Stephenson's Cryptonomicon as its first 100 pages appeared online. It described digital cash with bad UI — 'am I in a dream?'
  • PayPal fundraising was brutal, with 'no' at every turn (Tim cites Reid Hoffman). After the eBay acquisition Levchin hit a low; his wife Nelly later told him fintech was 'what you're meant for', and Affirm followed.

Socialism's brochure is great; the redistributors always keep the food

Levchin, who spent 16 years in the USSR, is 'pretty worried' about socialism's appeal in the US. He'd fix capitalism's casualties with philanthropy and pro-social products, not central planning.

  • Defining image: in every state store, the clerks behind the counter were fat while everyone you knew was skinny. Whoever redistributes gets to keep a lot, however honest they start.
  • Central planning removes the pressure to cut costs, so it stagnates: the same government-mandated prices for decades, and rotary phones that looked like the 1950s. The Soviet collapse 'surprised exactly zero people'.
  • He quotes the current New York mayor's 'collectivist paradise' line. The appeal is real: capitalism can be profoundly unfair to individuals, and layoffs can be catastrophic for families.
  • Answers: a safety net, philanthropy — more important the more disruption there is, and religions encode thousands of years of thought on it — and pro-social products.
  • Affirm as proof: accept 'a little bit less money' to build something more beneficial to society, and retention follows.
  • Book context: Bulgakov's The Master and Margarita (the devil wrecks 1925 socialist Moscow) is the earliest brilliant portrait of homo sovieticus. He gives it to new friends and keeps copies on his desk.

Principle STILL TRYING TO IMPRESS NELLYrelationshipsmindsetcareer

Any doubt means no doubt; marriages and co-founders work when both feel they married up

Two lines run Levchin's decisions: Ronin's rule on doubt for hires and co-founders, and the daily effort to impress his wife and co-investor, Nelly.

  • 'Whenever there is any doubt, there is no doubt' (Ronin): you know the answer, make the decision, and don't delay the unpleasant part. If you doubt a key employee or co-founder, odds are low your mind changes for the better. He also draws leadership lessons from Seven Samurai.
  • The secret of his marriage: 'I'm still trying to impress this girl'. The best couples and co-founders both secretly think they lucked out, so each keeps growing.
  • He met Nelly in mid-1999 — they bonded over a Master and Margarita quote spoken in Russian — and they've been together 27 years with two children. He credits her with calling his BS and with Affirm.
  • Working together at their family-office VC (SciFi VC) works because their strengths don't overlap — he's technical, she has a deep finance background and is 'an incredible empath'. She doesn't ask about his token budget; he doesn't second-guess her hiring.
  • Family motto: 'Don't go to bed angry. Stay up and fight.' When work and family overlap, unspoken frustrations compound faster than in most marriages.

Personal story HRV AND RESTING HEART RATEhealthmindset

From tracking fingernail clippings to two metrics and a bike

Age replaced raw compute with pattern recognition: Levchin now tracks the few things that matter and saves his obsession for cycling.

  • A 2018 Men's Journal profile: he photographed every meal, found his minimum sleep by cutting 5 minutes a night, and graded every meeting 1-10. He even counted fingernail trims — 'no value'.
  • Now his anchors are resting heart rate and HRV, plus enough time in bed. Diet relaxed once travel taught him gut health is fine with any fermented food.
  • He was a sickly child whose parents were told he might not live past childhood. He took up clarinet to build lung capacity, measured it with a blow-tube, and crashed into a splinter on Kyiv's outdoor velodrome.
  • After the eBay deal he hit a low. Nelly pointed at the bike on the wall; on his first group ride on a college beater, he dropped everyone on the first hill. He's a 'hammerhead' who still hunts watts through crank-length tweaks and now adds weights.
  • Cycling is low-impact (6 hours without wrecking your body), the only time his head fully clears, and a CEO social scene — finishing with the coffee-shop ride. Sami Inkinen warns he goes all-out for the first 90 minutes.
  • Jens Voigt, whom he has ridden with: 'Shut up legs, do what I tell you' and 'If it hurts me, it must hurt the other ones twice as much'.

For better espresso, buy the grinder before the machine

Levchin's order: a real burr grinder, then free YouTube skills, then the espresso machine. He's not paid by any of these brands.

  • Grinder tiers: a Niche-class grinder (~$600-700); a Kaya Orbit-type flat-burr grinder with swappable burrs (~$1,600-2,000); Weber Workshops if no price is too high.
  • Then watch the many YouTube espresso tutorials before buying anything else.
  • Machine: he prefers the classic 58mm portafilter. La Marzocco is 'the granddaddy of them all' on his counter; Decent Espresso is 'an Android tablet attached to an espresso machine'; Breville is surprisingly good.
  • Roasts: medium-dark for honeyed, thick espresso; light roast in a Chemex for high clarity. French press for campfires; for AeroPress, 'just get an espresso machine'.
  • Budget: a manual Lido grinder (~$273, per Tim) makes beautiful coffee at low RPM, but takes ~10 minutes a cup. He grew up on instant coffee and Soviet chicory 'coffee' with no coffee in it.

Recommendation SCI-FI AND DISTILLED BUSINESS ESSAYScareermindset

Sci-fi for the future, short distilled books for leadership

Levchin has 'zero to negative' respect for most business books. He prefers first-hand anecdotes, essays distilled into short books, biographies, and science fiction as philosophy.

  • He arrived in Chicago on July 16, 1991, six weeks before the Soviet Union fell and left him stateless. New nerd friends handed him Neuromancer and a VHS of Akira, and they still talk daily 30 years later.
  • Neal Stephenson: Snow Crash shaped his software life (it coined 'metaverse'), followed by Cryptonomicon. William Gibson, per Tim: 'the future is already here — it's just not evenly distributed'.
  • Leadership picks: Hamilton Helmer's 7 Powers (short, foreword by Reed Hastings; why network businesses last and what brand means), A Failure of Nerve (the differentiated leader who survives unpopular calls), and Cialdini's Influence.
  • Biographies: Ron Chernow's (Titan on Rockefeller is the most business-like) and A Mind at Play on Claude Shannon, a playful genius like Feynman. He suspects Claude Code is named after Shannon, though he didn't hear that from Anthropic.
  • After Tim's Ed Thorp episode he put Thorp on his list of people to meet. Tim recommends the anime Paprika (2006), with Kyoto researchers already decoding dreams via fMRI.

Names and terms at a glance

Glossary & Entities

BNPL — Buy now, pay later: splitting a purchase into a fixed schedule of payments at checkout.
Revolving debt — Credit-card style balance that rolls over month to month with compounding interest.
Underwriting — Pricing the risk of a loan — expected loss — before deciding to lend and at what rate.
Merchant-funded 0% — An interest-free plan where the retailer pays the lender's financing cost to win the sale.
HRV — Heart rate variability, a recovery marker that rises as resting heart rate falls.
Hammerhead — Cycling slang for a rider who can't help going hard on every road.
Homo sovieticus — Term for the mindset shaped by life under Soviet socialism.
