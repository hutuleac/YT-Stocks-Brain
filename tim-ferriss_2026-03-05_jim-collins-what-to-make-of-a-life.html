The one-paragraph takeaway

Video Snapshot

  1. Jim Collins's third Tim Ferriss conversation and the first on his new book, What to Make of a Life (subtitle: Cliffs, Fog, Fire, and the Self-Knowledge Imperative), 12 years in the making.
  2. Method: matched pairs of people who hit the same cliff event, studied across whole lives. It started as a study of self-renewal and became a study of what to make of a life.
  3. Core idea: encodings, durable capacities waiting to be discovered by life. Trusting them matters more than finding them (Jim puts 70 of 100 points on trust).
  4. Fog is universal: every person in the study had fog phases, sometimes losing a decade, and fog almost always follows a big cliff.
  5. Return on luck, not luck itself, separated 10x companies from their comparisons. Three kinds: what luck, who luck and zeit luck.
  6. Jim at 68 says he has more energy than at 37: the fire changed from red-hot insecurity to a sustained glow.
  7. Most people in the study did their best work after 50, 60 or 70. Jim reads early peaks as being out of frame, not running out of energy.
  8. Tim is in a project fog: an 850-page draft on saying no, the podcast in a saturated field, and a newer writing project pulling at him.
Personal story JIM, 68 — MORE ENERGY THAN AT 37mindsethealth

More energy at 68 than at 37: two mornings, one coffee, a changed fire

Jim credits loving the work itself, always having a big project, and a fire that stopped running on insecurity.

  • Up at 4:00 a.m. for 3-4 hours of his most intense creative work; a nap gives him a "second morning". He once slept on a couch backstage minutes before speaking to 3,000 people.
  • One cup of coffee a day, made himself: he travels with Peet's Arabian Mocha Java, a cone filter and a water boiler (except in Italy), as a "boot up sequence" no hotel can break.
  • Breakfast with Joanne, who reads out stories she's picked from the Wall Street Journal; the two now cycle Dolomites passes together, heart rate 160-170s for hours.
  • Tim's parallel: Marcelo Garcia, nine-time BJJ world champion, slept under the bleachers before a final, then went from 1 to 10. Josh Waitzkin calls this avoiding "the simmering six".
  • Shared activities later in a marriage: Kelly Starrett and his wife Juliet switched to mountain biking together.
  • Jim says he's not disciplined but compelled: when you love the doing itself, preparing isn't discipline.
  • His fire went from red, molten "channeled rage" to a green-yellow sustained glow; he credits years living alongside the lives he studied for the shift.
  • Everyone has an energy set point; the goal is to stay on the positive side of it, not lose 20-30 years below it.

Framework THE BOOK'S METHODmindsetcareer

Cliffs and fog: how one 1985 Ironman win became a 12-year study

Studying matched pairs through the same cliff turned a question about self-renewal into the bigger one of what to make of a life.

  • Joanne won the 1985 Ironman world championship in Hawaii despite a chronic hamstring, losing a minute a mile from a 10-minute lead and winning by about 90 seconds.
  • When the injury ended her career at its peak she told Jim "I feel like I'm dying". Combined with John W. Gardner's work on self-renewal at Stanford, that seeded the study.
  • A cliff event is a before-and-after moment, chosen or not. Jim studied pairs who were alike up to the same cliff, then followed how each came through it.
  • The method kept enlarging the question, as it did in Built to Last: a study of corporate vision with Jerry Porras became one of enduring great companies.
  • You face the question three times: leaving youth, after a big cliff, and in later decades, so that your 50s-90s are your biggest years.
  • Fog (lost, uncertain phases) hit every person in the study, sometimes for a decade; Jim's were his 20s and 2013-2014. Tim's rule one: don't freak out.
  • Joanne also reframed the method: "people are not stock returns". Pairs show different choices, not better ones.
  • Self-renewal turned out to be a byproduct; none of the subjects pursued it as a goal.
Pull quote
“Cuz that identity as as a world champion athlete, this thing that she so encoded for, that she so loved doing, was being taken away from her.”— Jim Collins
Framework 70 POINTS ON TRUST, 30 ON DISCOVERYcareermindset

Encodings: you're built for many things, and trusting them matters most

Encodings are durable capacities discovered through experience, not strengths you build, and everyone is encoded for a wide range of things.

  • Picture a constellation of encodings seen through a window frame: life shifts the frame, putting you in or out of frame. Most people die with large swaths never discovered.
  • John Glenn was out of frame in chemistry, clicked in a government-paid pilot course, and stayed calm under fire. His Royal Crown Cola years are ~10% of his life and 0.2% of his memoir.
  • Alan Page: first defensive player to be NFL MVP, later a Minnesota Supreme Court justice. Ben Franklin had three frames: media empire, science, diplomacy and a nation.
  • What stood out was trust: Glenn's and Robert Plant's parents wanted a family business and accounting; both ignored them.
  • Jim's reframe of "what do I do better than others": ask what you do better than other ways you could expand yourself; it's in frame vs out of frame, not a contest.
  • Pattern from the lives: extend out and circle back, not radical reinvention. Plant sings bluegrass with Alison Krauss, then a bluegrass "Black Dog".
  • Over 80% had an intense side passion: disco dancing, the occult, Sunday school, mountain climbing, dinner parties.
  • Tim's search tools: ask people close to him when he's at his best and worst, consider overlapping StrengthsFinder-type tests, and Chinese/Soviet scouting via simple tests (broad jump, overhead broomstick squat).
  • Tim's own read: dumb questions, beginner's glasses and novelty seeking to bridge worlds. A friend pushed him to record his founder sessions because what's easy for him isn't easy for most.
Pull quote
“John Glenn could have done 10 MBAs and he would have never been as encoded for being a business executive”— Jim Collins
Framework RUNNING WEEKLY AT JIM'S LABcareer

Right seats, a points-based punch card, and the art of the wonderful no

Jim now sees management as getting people into seats they're encoded for, and guards his own time with a Buffett-style punch card.

  • Update to Good to Great: right people on the bus is still true, but it's about the seats. Frustration with what someone isn't means they're in the wrong seat.
  • He finds team encodings by observation and small experiments: one teammate stayed calm through COVID chaos, so crises go to her. Trust them and get out of the way.
  • Never confuse scale of impact with scale of enterprise: a small special-operations team can have outsized impact. Growth is useful because it creates more seats.
  • Stanford faculty's 50-30-20: 50% new creative work, 30% teaching, 20% everything else. Jim also requires 1,000+ creative hours in every rolling 365 days.
  • Buffett's punch card, as points: flights cost more than virtual talks; 2-day Boulder executive lab sessions cost a lot; one London trip uses about three punches. The question is never "is he free", it's "any punches left?"
  • Declines are planned from the first call: a teammate builds the relationship and sets low odds up front, and Jim sometimes records a voice memo, aiming for "the most wonderful disappointing answer".
  • Early success came as a "fog of success": unprepared for it, he said yes to things he'd now refuse.
  • Enneagram: Tim (a self-preservation six, married to a six) finds it explains who fits his small team and helped in dating; Shopify and Dropbox use it, and he calls it "an acceptable horoscope for tech guys". Jim admits a bit of four.
Pull quote
“life is the ultimate punch card.”— Jim Collins
Framework FROM GREAT BY CHOICE TO THIS BOOKmindsetcareer

Return on luck: what, who and zeit luck, and the moments that aren't equal

Winners don't get more luck; they get more out of the luck they get, especially in moments that call for an unequal response.

  • Defined with Morten Hansen in Great by Choice: a luck event is one you didn't cause, with significant consequence, that comes as a surprise.
  • 10x winners didn't get more good luck, less bad luck, bigger spikes or better timing than their comparisons, only a higher return on luck.
  • Three types: what luck, who luck (the most underrated) and zeit luck, fitting the moment: Franklin and the Revolution, Alice Paul and the 19th Amendment, Jimmy Page and British blues rock.
  • The key moments are ones where "not all time in life is equal": they call for an unequal response, often 10x intensity.
  • Jim's chain: random course sorting put him with Bill Lazier; a family tragedy opened a Stanford teaching slot in 1988; Jerry Porras read his San Jose Mercury News piece and Built to Last followed.
  • Built to Last's launch: the USA Today money section ran Jim and Porras on its front page, 50,000 copies were back-ordered overnight, and the all-out year after gave him shingles.
  • Bad luck for companies: you only learn from what you survive. Productive paranoia and reserves keep you above the "death line".
  • Personal bad luck can expose encodings: Cardiss Collins, suddenly widowed (her pair lost a husband too: one to a plane crash, one to a heart attack), served 25 years in Congress and chaired the Congressional Black Caucus. Katherine Graham's leadership at the Washington Post came the same way.
  • Tim's version: surface area for luck (Silicon Valley), angel investing in 2008-2010, and going all-in on psychedelic science around 2015. Most of his swings are "Marco with no Polo".
Pull quote
“just because something's a once in a lifetime opportunity is merely a fact. It's not a reason.”— Jim Collins
Worth noting
Jim warns the harder skill is knowing when not to take a return on luck because it doesn't fit your encodings. Tim's paraphrase of Rabbi Jonathan Sacks: separate an opportunity to be seized from a temptation to be resisted.
Counterintuitive take STUDY FINDING — DISPUTED BY TIMcareermindset

Best work late: early peaks mean out of frame, not out of energy

Jim rejects the myth that breakthroughs belong to the young; Tim pushes back with energy and distraction.

  • Toni Morrison didn't write until her 40s, published Beloved at 56 and Jazz at 61. Barbara McClintock's genetics breakthrough came after her late 40s; Grace Hopper's in a second career.
  • Franklin is "the first poster child of great stuff late". Sam Walton, Walt Disney (still planning Epcot) and Steve Jobs never slowed until the end.
  • Tim's counter: a startup-style intensity edge for the young (ramen, few responsibilities, maybe mitochondrial), and success floods people with invitations, as with Nobel winners' productivity drop.
  • Tim is having a team use Claude Code to analyze his 20 years of startup investing, including founder age.
  • Jim's protection is temperament: belligerently reclusive, he was selective before he had anything to select from.
  • Hedgehog mode: in a clarity phase, one big river dominates. Founders who lose or sell their company often lose three decades with no plan, as do veterans leaving special operations.
  • Irv Grousbeck told Jim an option to come back has negative value: in low-odds games, 0-80% commitment counts as zero, so take a 2% chance over 0%.
  • Signing the Declaration was a death warrant if they lost; Franklin, dressed down by the Privy Council, "walked in an Englishman and walked out an American".
Pull quote
“Options sometimes can have negative value because if you know you have the option to come back it will change your behavior”— Irv Grousbeck, as relayed by Jim Collins
Worth noting
Tim says his energy-and-distraction argument comes without a fine-tooth-comb analysis; Jim allows physical decline matters for some fields, such as singing.
Principle JIM'S DEFINITION OF SUCCESS, UNCHANGEDmindsetrelationshipscareer

Flip the arrow of money, and measure success by your spouse's respect

People who stayed in frame treated money as fuel for the work, and Jim's own test of success is still whether Joanne likes and respects him more each year.

  • Arrow of money: do you work to make money, or need money to do your work? Those who flipped it kept going after success; great company builders never ran out of steam.
  • Tim's add-ons: Carse's finite and infinite games; Tim O'Reilly's line that life is a road trip, "not a tour of gas stations"; Seth Godin's "what would you do if you knew you would fail?"
  • Tim wants future winnings as chips to keep in play; Branson-type "risk takers" are really expert risk mitigators who stay far from the death line.
  • Jim's grandmother met his test-pilot grandfather Jimmy Collins at the Wichita airport and married him 4 days later; Jim and Joanne got engaged four days after their first date and are at 45 years.
  • Joanne is the "strategic guidance mechanism", Jim the "creative propulsion"; she sees what needs attention and he's encoded to hear it.
  • Asked for one word to describe living with Jim, Joanne said "exhausting".
  • Jim scores his days from -2 to +2; this conversation was a +2 day.
Pull quote
“Joanne will love me regardless. But will she like me more as the years go by? Will she respect me more as the years go by?”— Jim Collins

Names and terms at a glance

Glossary & Entities

Cliff event — A life change, chosen or not, that splits life into a before and after and forces you to re-answer what to make of it.
Fog — A phase of feeling lost, uncertain or disoriented, common after an unexpected cliff.
Encodings — Durable inner capacities discovered through experience, as opposed to strengths built by training.
In frame / out of frame — Whether your current life captures a big set of your encodings or few of them.
Luck event — An event you didn't cause, with significant consequence, that comes as a surprise.
Return on luck — How much you make of the luck events that hit you; the variable that separated 10x winners.
Zeit luck — The luck of your work fitting the zeitgeist of your era.
Punch card — Buffett's idea that every use of yourself spends a limited punch you can't get back.
Hedgehog mode — A clarity phase where one big thing organizes how you spend yourself.
Arrow of money — Whether you work for money or use money as fuel for the work you're encoded for.
Simmering six — Josh Waitzkin's term for staying at medium intensity instead of oscillating between full rest and full activation.
