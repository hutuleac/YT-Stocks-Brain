PHP week: models are finding vulnerabilities everywhere, and the fix is new architectures

Altman expects far more serious vulnerabilities than even pessimists assume, because models keep improving and nobody had pointed them at all the code on the internet.

  • Collison: the durable fix is changing architectures, not just patching — Altman: a new generation of startups is building a whole new stack, which 'would have seemed insane two years ago'.
  • Open question: rewrite PHP codebases in Rust or upgrade them — and does the global software upgrade mean huge consulting work for Infosys and peers?
  • Day one of PHP week 'went much better than I expected': companies took apps offline for an hour to scan, patch and redeploy — Altman won't declare victory.
  • Cyber experts no longer trust their threat models — if AI finds deep novel hardware bugs relying on unknown physics, core assumptions break.
  • On the cyber-RL 'SNL thing' (we urge you to urge us to stop): Altman concedes the field hasn't explained 'why we think all of the potential downside of this technology is worth it'.
  • His answer is iterative deployment — unpopular with the AI safety community — and avoiding a 'capability overhang'; 'the prevented exploits do not' get headlines.

Against a single superintelligence: sandboxing, monitoring, alignment and agent teams

Altman sees a ladder — sandboxing, then monitoring, then genuine alignment — and pins his optimism on many agents checking each other rather than one giant mind.

  • Collison notes OpenAI insiders are pessimistic that sandboxing is possible; Altman: 'we can do much better than we're doing now', but would you trust a sandbox against something a million times smarter than the best security researcher?
  • Sandboxing works to a point, monitoring further — chain of thought plus activation monitoring for deception — and eventually you need near-certain alignment, including valuing 'people being in control of the future, forever'.
  • Only a model whose alignment you fully trust can help build better sandboxes, so alignment has to stay ahead of capability.
  • Agent teams ('I hate the word agent swarms') likely have interesting scaling-law properties and variance in alignment — a direction to push for safety, not just capability.
  • Teams mirror what makes society robust: pluralism, democracy, agents in dynamic equilibrium, offense vs defense.
  • Altman expects 'some limits about how smart we let these single models get', at least for a long while — any slowdown would come from that choice, not research limits.

“I would much rather intuitively have a trillion AIs of slightly superhuman intelligence join society than one AI of 1 trillion IQ join society.”— Sam Altman

From $1 of compute per problem to $10M proofs: how OpenAI allocates compute

Test-time compute and agent teams let OpenAI pour vastly more compute into one problem, and the gap between frontier users and everyone else keeps widening.

  • A few years ago you couldn't usefully spend more than ~$1 of compute on a problem (GPT-4 grinding 100,000 tokens); chain of thought and teams now run 10,000 times in parallel.
  • Agents respect budgets: 'do the best job you can with $10,000 of compute' works — Altman flags a societal question when rivals spend $10,000 vs $100,000 on the same problem.
  • Internally: wide latitude, 'no token leaderboards' — a skeptical default that turns to appreciation if the work is important.
  • The Navier-Stokes proof cost on the order of $10M in compute; hundreds of millions went into math overall, including unpublished discoveries beyond the 700+ proofs.
  • Altman bets Navier-Stokes could be redone at 1/100th the cost a year on, and expects ~10x yearly gains in how dollars or flops turn into useful output.
  • A 'Model Name-Hold My Beer' Slack channel had a new model 'knocking down' researchers' hardest career problems — Altman stayed up two nights, excited but with 'sadness and melancholy'.
  • On mathematicians' anger: 'humanity deserves fundamental knowledge' outweighs it, but OpenAI owes those fields a path; Collison compares it to longshoremen and port automation, and his father watching working horses fade in 1950s Ireland.

The invisible hand is failing: memory, the $70B gigawatt and self-replicating data centers

Altman argues high prices aren't pulling in enough supply because memory makers remember their busts, and that per-gigawatt costs must fall sharply.

  • Expect irrational exuberance and busts, glut and shortage — but demand for capable, cheap enough models 'only goes one way'.
  • OpenAI's response: its own chip (strong intelligence per watt), learning to build data centers, and a robotics program aimed at building data centers — self-replicating data centers as 'the real RSI'.
  • Data centers are permitting- and component-bottlenecked; robots could make more components eventually.
  • DRAM history: invest $20B of capex to make $19B, near-bankruptcies — now each maker is heading to ~$100B free cash flow a year, yet capex stays conservative.
  • Collison: Intel could get back into memory 'as is tradition'. Altman: local inference wastes scarce DRAM sitting idle in PCs ~95% of the time; cloud utilization matters.
  • Supply responses so far: turbines, batteries, concrete, steel and industrial-robot actuators — 'not the humanoid fake demos'. OpenAI selling its chip could be another.
  • Chip design is 'a great problem for AI'; lithography is known by 'one company on Earth'.
  • Cost per gigawatt is ~$70B over five-year TCO; Altman wants $20B with 10x better performance a year — that needs cheaper energy and compressed margin stacking, not just zero profits.

AI doing AI research, and how much compute each employee should get

Models can already find incremental efficiency wins but not transformer-scale leaps, and companies have yet to set real per-person compute budgets.

  • Today you can ask 'find me a 5% compute efficiency win'; nobody knows how to ask for a new architecture as big a leap as the transformer.
  • What's missing is smarter high-level planning and conceptual reasoning — applying the math pipeline to AI research 'will probably work'.
  • The open question is what to ask GPT-7 and how to use agent swarms — Altman expects to 'learn a lot about that in the next three months'; there's no codified OpenAI method.
  • Stripe spends roughly 1% of OpEx on tokens; Collison expects 5%, 10%, 20% and 'tens of percentage points' at steady state for many companies — Altman agrees.
  • Altman: AI isn't a company's biggest spend problem — echoing Paul Graham on managers saving $17 on snacks while costly meetings run unchecked; more firms under-use AI than over-use it.

Personal agents, computer use and why Amazon wins agentic commerce

Altman thinks personal agents are a few ideas from mainstream, and both speakers argue businesses' moats are brand and fulfillment, not their HTML.

  • OpenAI launched Dots weeks ago; the winner needs intelligence, context and tools — it'll look like Dots, Muse or GrokBot 'but it'll just work'.
  • No one yet says 'I cannot imagine my life without this' about personal agents, as they do about coding agents or ChatGPT.
  • Computer use: Altman expects fights both ways, converging on 'you're allowed to tell an AI to use your computer on your behalf' — low confidence; Collison likens it to the semantic web.
  • Collison: every company adds a chat widget, but consumers want their primary chat app and businesses resist — he compares it to the iPhone App Store, which big firms feared would hand customers to Apple.
  • Altman picks Amazon '10 out of 10 times' inside ChatGPT, even at a higher price — its fulfillment network is the moat.
  • Collison: any web form is 'basically fake work' — you're never inventing new information.

Tokens are a bad unit, benchmaxxing persists, and the 'senior coworker' target

Altman dislikes tokens as AI's unit but customers rejected the alternative, and he says the real targets are scientific discovery and being pleasant to work with.

  • OpenAI nearly priced its next model with no tokens — a prompt in, a price back, 'like AWS' — but customers wanted a visible unit, even inflated tokens at a higher cost.
  • Outcome pricing doesn't work as a standard because outcomes vary too much by use case; Altman would 'rather not make up a fake unit' like chip 'nanometers'.
  • Collison: choosing between 10-cent-per-million small-model tokens and pricey big-model tokens, plus guessing chain-of-thought length, is pure cognitive overhead; chain of thought is billed.
  • Benchmaxxing: OpenAI 'endeavors not to' and is better at it now than historically; there are no good benchmarks for enjoyable-to-work-with or top-scientist ability.
  • Models went from the 'glazing era' to a 'stroppy, ornery' anti-glazing era; current ones are 'pretty good' — Altman cites Emmett Shear's ideal of a thoughtful, challenging, respectful senior coworker.
  • Greg Brockman: 'singular on the Greg Brockman scale' — intensity, monster productivity, dedication to the mission.

Names and terms at a glance

Glossary & Entities

RSI (recursive self-improvement) — AI improving AI; Altman extends it to 'self-replicating data centers' built by AI-run robots.
Capability overhang — Capability built up but withheld from the public, which iterative deployment tries to avoid.
Activation monitoring — Inspecting a model's internal activations, beyond its chain of thought, to detect deception.
Test-time compute — Compute spent while answering (reasoning, parallel agents) rather than in training.
Benchmaxxing — Optimizing a model for benchmark scores at the expense of real-world usefulness.
Five-year TCO per gigawatt — Total cost to build and run a gigawatt of AI compute for five years, ~$70B today.
