The one-paragraph takeaway

Video Snapshot

  1. Gary Vaynerchuk walks through his holding company Vayner X (almost $400M a year) and the 13 businesses around it, in an interview with the host of Open Residency.
  2. His agency thesis: big agencies sell ideas, production and media separately; Vayner is a production company that makes social content at scale and routes winners to performance or to the bigger brief.
  3. Content is a volume game: he aims for 1,000 pieces a day across 10 platforms and 100 handles, posted natively, and calls quantity non-debatable and quality subjective.
  4. The one rule he won't break: never compromise the audience's trust; Gary Vee and Gary Vaynerchuk stay separate, and he declines clients whose product he doesn't think will sell.
  5. Hiring runs on family and speed, and his admitted weakness is lack of candor when firing; he encourages people to leave and builds them up.
  6. Next bets: sports agency, TV production (Vayner Watt), a restaurant group, and VeeFriends as a Disney/Pokemon-scale collectible and storytelling IP over about 20 years.
  7. Media gatekeepers are gone (alt sports leagues, live shopping, long-form on every screen), so the winners are operators who are self-aware and use AI with a real team.
  8. His self-description: detached from professional outcomes, with identity separate from the career; "detachment" is his unfair advantage.

Vayner Media: an ad agency built as a social-first production company

Gary argues the old split of creative retainers, production and media buying is obsolete, and Vayner wins by making social content at scale and reading what performs.

  • Vayner X holds Vayner Media, Chuck Media (a conflict shop, run by Mark Rolland), the Tamara Group (named for his mother; Attention and Media and Relevance Agency), the Sasha Group (small businesses, named for his father) and Zor Vayner speakers; it will do $385-390M this year, "almost 400".
  • He is active CEO and chairman of Vayner Media; Ryan Harwood (Gallery Media Group, PureWow) reports into him.
  • The old model: creative agencies charge $3-20M retainers for ideas and decks, a production company makes the 30-second spot, then $30M of media makes people see it. Vayner makes social-first content at scale, extracts the quant and qual, sends winners down to performance (CAC, LTV, conversion) or up as the brief for the bigger idea.
  • Work spans social, long-form video, Super Bowl ads (William Shatner for Raisin Brands), live shopping, experiential booths, podcasting and sampling that doubles as content.
  • Clients he praises: Fanatics (Michael Rubin), Pepsi (Mark Kirkham), Mug Root Beer, Scott's Miracle-Gro (Nick Morus), Bose (Jim Malachi). He expects to lose one client whose new CMO distrusts social and prefers a TV campaign.
  • Big public holding companies (Publicis, Omnicom, WPP) sell "fake reports" and principal ad buying, which he calls fraud, but it works on older boards; Gruns and Liquid Death show where the value went.
  • Tingley Lane Trading (Josh Smith) is a barter business: brands swap expiring inventory for media credits, and Vayner delivers creator and social content instead of 2am TV; he calls it a strategic liquidator and suggests folding it into the agencies.
  • Growth comes from word of mouth after 15 years; key-man risk still exists, and Wine Library (run by Brandon Warrick) dipped without him, going from 4 to 60, down to 30 and back to 60 after WineText (units not stated).

The content machine: record long-form, chop it, post natively, never stop

Gary treats long-form conversations like this podcast as production days that feed a volume engine, run by a lean team and strong editors.

  • Target: 1,000 pieces of content a day across 10 platforms and 100 handles, up from the 10-15 posts a day that once seemed excessive; he is on this podcast because he "needs content".
  • The Gary Vee pyramid (make long form, chop it up) was a deck he published a decade ago; he calls himself the first clipping agency and does streaming, vlogging, podcasts and speaking for the content, not the event.
  • His personal-brand team is 20-30 people, down from 50 as AI took over work; Rav and Rhea run it as executive producers and Sid is in special ops. Tweets used to be him and are now extracted from long-form and timed.
  • Post natively on each platform (LinkedIn, Shorts, Snapchat, Threads), not one cut everywhere; there is a shortage of editors and strategists who know each platform, and he just hired two more.
  • Senior leaders on his team all did the work themselves first. His early Twitter edge (2007-2011) was engagement more than posting, and he says YouTube may be the most valuable platform.
  • Quality vs quantity: do both; for a premium brand, test lo-fi concepts at volume and re-run the winners with premium visuals (his advice to the host's Rolex-of-podcasts concern, with LVMH's streetwear collabs as the analogy).
  • Much of his day is private strategy for CEOs he can't show, so public content skews general; his 4Ds sessions held the real playbooks, 90% of that footage unreleased. Last year was a bad year for Brand Gary because he was CEO and COO, and he plans to "eat up a ton of oxygen" in 2027.
  • A good personal brand is honest to yourself, like his garage-sale videos that elite peers disliked; the host recalls Gary's advice to care about the 1% who matter, not the 99%, and Gary confirms it.

Trust is the asset, and creators are the next Fortune 500 companies

Gary's non-negotiable is separating his attention from his equity, and his next book argues creator-led enterprises can scale from millions to billions.

  • Rule one: don't compromise the audience. Gary Vee and Gary Vaynerchuk are different; startups where he has equity must do a lot of work before he will even consider mentioning them.
  • Vayner Media doesn't take clients that offer millions because they think their product won't sell; he vets the offer, its value to his ICP and its positioning.
  • New book, The Individual Empire, due around March or April: personalities exploding online (he names Bartlett, Hormozi, Cody Sanchez, MrBeast, Alex Cooper, Logan and Jake Paul) are the next Fortune 500 if they build enterprise value, the "big boy version" of Crush It (written 2008, saying you can make $100K a year online).
  • The host adds that AI lets you start with low opex, but you must then add people; he describes his own company's second chapter as going on "savage offense" with hires.
  • Start with self-awareness: writer, on-camera, interviewer? Everyone can be a content creator but not a Hall of Fame one (his basketball analogy).
  • His own mistake: he gets to 85-90% on many fronts and doesn't "put the final nail in the coffin".
  • Operators win: asked about the biggest arbitrage in the attention economy, he says AI, and operators are "about to win everything"
  • Mindset first: built on insecurity, companies crashed; the common thread of winners was knowing themselves well. Ev Williams (Blogger, Odeo, Twitter) is a favorite entrepreneur he thinks is underrated next to Jack Dorsey.

Personal story FAMILY HIRES · CANDOR GAP · DETACHMENTcareermindsetrelationships

Hiring family, firing badly, and staying detached from the outcome

Gary's team runs on long relationships and speed, his admitted weakness is candor, and his stability comes from a self that isn't his career.

  • Hiring: mostly family-like longtime partners (Brandon Warrick, 36 years; Sid, 12 years; AJ at Vayner Sports; Andy and John Troutman at VeeFriends); the reason is speed, not loyalty, because they know how he works. About 150 people have been with him 10+ years and Vayner has 2,000+ employees.
  • His self-described kryptonite is lack of candor; he struggles to give negative feedback because his father gave it harshly, and he has improved over time.
  • He sets people free and springboards them (Babin, D-Rock, Caleb Ralston left and started companies) "I don't want to keep people, I want to build people".
  • He has built exits: Resi (nine-figure, to American Express, co-founded with Ben Leventhal) and Empathy Wines (to Constellation Brands within 18 months, with Nate Schroeder and John Troutman).
  • He took no salary near $200K a year until well into his 40s; the business is not there to fund toys, and he over-hires to avoid short-staffed moments.
  • His day is firefighting and 15-minute meetings across time zones; his calm comes from not fearing it falls apart and from identity not tied to his career: "the sport of business".
  • The host and teammates say he is the same off camera as on; he says people adored in public are often zeros in real life.
  • At 34 he restarted from zero after building his father's wine business; he and AJ got free office space for two years because they had so little money.

Beyond the agency: sports, TV, restaurants and VeeFriends as a 20-year IP play

Each venture follows the same pattern, a rough first five years and then a tipping point, with VeeFriends the biggest swing.

  • Pickleball: he bought a team for $500K and says teams now sell for $20M; he also owns Big League Whiffle's New York Green Apples (first World Series winners) and says alternative leagues are high risk, high reward, best grown slowly because leagues fail by raising and burning money.
  • Vayner Sports (run by AJ) took about five lean years then tipped: Sauce Gardner, Aidan Hutchinson, Kirk Cousins, $200M baseball deals, the seventh pick, six kids going in the top 50 of the NFL draft and a shot at CJ Carr; it is now one of the largest independent sports agencies in under 10 years and the one company he could sell or joint-venture. Tip: always ask for whitelisting on athlete deals.
  • Vayner Watt (with Eric Watenber, as captioned, and Matt Higgins of RSE Ventures): a TV production company with a Jets show on Amazon, a Kevin Bacon history show and many shows next year; deals vary (sell outright, produce for a fee, co-produce, back-end).
  • VCR Group (restaurants; David Roditz as captioned, Chef Capon, Chef Connor): Flyfish, Capon's Chop House (his Hillstone-style concept) and Capon's Burgers, which he says could be a billion-dollar company.
  • VeeFriends: 286 characters, aiming for Disney, Pokemon-scale (worth $100B+) IP over 20 years, six kids' books next year, comics already in Hollywood option talks; NFT-era backlash is the dot-com parallel, and series 2 buyers got trading cards that now "rip".
  • Early investments: Facebook, Twitter, Venmo, Snapchat, Liquid Death, Uber (passed at angel, entered soon after); he passed on Airbnb after an email asking him to invest.

No gatekeepers: live shopping, alt sports and every format at once

Gary sees unlimited distribution turning every format into merit, with live shopping the next big creator career.

  • Live shopping has two meanings: QVC-style selling live on social, and affiliate content. He claims China's live shopping will reach $1 trillion in GMV this year, selling cars, homes and yachts, and thinks it will make fortunes for salespeople who could never be Alex Earl or Logan Paul.
  • The biggest sellers beyond collectibles are makeup; per the host, creators run it from whole office buildings in China, and the best version is one swipe to buy with no friction.
  • Sports leagues used to need one of 13 or 36 channels; now social clips from slamball, volleyball or pickleball are a "gateway drug" to TV, so success is on merit.
  • Nothing dies, it iterates: podcasts are radio, and there is no broadcast TV, only streaming; long, mid and short form all stay.
  • He is bullish on print because it is underpriced (but still overpriced) and wants to start new magazines; television commercials are the worst platform; he would bet on Zuckerberg because he is adaptable.
  • Sports cards vs S&P over ten years: he leans S&P (tied to America's weapons and money printing) but says it could be close; collectibles are cyclical and cards could be down in 2036.

Names and terms at a glance

Glossary & Entities

Whitelisting — Running paid ads through a creator's or athlete's own account, so one piece of content can be reused in ads.
Principal ad buying — An agency buying media inventory itself and reselling it to clients, which Gary calls fraud when disguised as advice.
Conflict shop — A sister agency that lets a holding company serve rival brands (like JetBlue and American Airlines) at once.
GMV — Gross merchandise value, the total sales volume through a platform.
Key-man risk — The danger that a business suffers badly if one central person leaves.
Barter media — Trading unsold goods for advertising credits instead of cash.
