The bottleneck is speed-to-power, and it is the wires — not generation, not cost

Power is a rounding error in AI economics and the hardest thing to get: hyperscalers would take electricity at twice the price tomorrow, but nobody can sell it to them for years.

  • A gigawatt data center runs ~$50B capex, ~$35B of it chips — power is roughly 5–10% of the build, so cost is not the issue, delivery is.
  • Generation interconnect queues: 15 months twenty years ago → approaching 45 months. Load-side waits at three US sites rose another 6–7 months in a single 7-month window.
  • ERCOT peaks around 80 GW and is sitting on well over 200 GW of load requests — mostly speculative, and the volume itself jams the queue.
  • GPU manufacturing scheduled to 2030 implies ~230 GW of power demand (Naam's range: 200–275) versus ~100 GW of projected US grid buildout — chips are running at twice the pace of wires.
  • Most forecasts miss half the load: people multiply Blackwell nameplate by unit count and forget the rest of the rack plus cooling, which nearly doubles total draw.
  • Utilities are paid cost-plus (~10% on capex) and outgun their own commissions — there is no incentive to build fast, and Naam has seen only 2–3 startups ever attack poles-and-wires speed, none impressive.
  • Politics is now a live risk: 71% of Americans oppose data centers — higher than oppose a nuclear plant nearby — and Governor Abbott just ordered an audit of Texas data center requests as election cover.

Behind-the-meter gas is how it's actually happening — and everything adjacent to a turbine is pivoting

If the grid makes you wait five years, you build your own power plant — which is more expensive per megawatt and completely rational when power is a tenth of your capex.

  • Large grid-scale gas turbines (~400 MW class) are sold out roughly seven years out; GE and Hitachi are standing up new assembly lines to catch up.
  • Buyers are dropping to smaller units — Solar Turbines' 38 MW truck-mounted machine (40 of them make a gigawatt) — and even those have backlogs.
  • Boom Supersonic has pivoted its jet engine design into natural gas turbines for data centers, an illustration of how strong the pull is on anyone with turbine expertise.
  • This is exactly how Elon Musk stood up the Colossus data centers — which Anthropic now leases.
  • Naam's answer on why Nvidia won't vertically integrate into power: it doesn't need to (the incentives already pull turbine makers in), and powergen is low-margin and frictionful — the same reason its neocloud push retreated.
  • His preferred fix is regulatory, not corporate: pay utilities and their executives on how fast they deliver power rather than on capex, and technical solutions will appear.

200 GW is already hiding in the grid — flexibility is the undervalued near-term trade

The US grid averages ~500 GW but swings from 400 GW on winter nights to 600 GW on summer afternoons; that 200 GW gap is roughly $10 trillion of AI capex sitting on wires that already exist.

  • Tyler Norris's paper (published early this year, before he joined Google) is Naam's pick for best electricity paper of the year: be flexible 100 hours a year — four days, 1% downtime — and you unlock ~100 GW, about $5T of data center capex including chips.
  • ERCOT enacted the rule in June: qualify as interruptible load and your interconnect drops from five to seven years down to 12–18 months. FERC has since told the six other largest grids to do something similar.
  • Batteries are the other route — charge at midnight when the transmission line is idle, don't draw at peak. Naam's framing: batteries are a cache for electrons, and this makes the grid cacheable.
  • The constraint being relieved is the transmission line, substation and transformers — not the generators. There is no shortage of power plants.
  • EVs hold the bulk of US battery capacity, and every EV charging company Naam knows has pivoted to using its load-shaping tech for data centers.
  • His bottom line: flexibility plus EV load-shaping is worth roughly 100 GW — the next five years of AI data center growth — without building a single new wire.

Solar+battery is the fastest megawatt you can buy — but the next 1000x isn't coming

A watt of solar went from $100 in 1975 to 8 cents from China, batteries are down 14x since 2010 with a potential 10x still ahead from sodium-ion — and yet Naam caps the remaining solar decline at 4–8x, not another thousandfold.

  • The UAE has a 1 GW round-the-clock solar plant outside Dubai — guaranteed minimum output, built from 5 GW of solar and 19 GWh of batteries at about $6/W capex. Compare $15/W for the last US nuclear build, $4/W for the cheapest Chinese one.
  • Solar plus battery is now the fastest energy project available at 12 months — decisive when gas turbines are sold out for years.
  • Sodium is far more abundant than lithium; sodium-ion could take battery costs down another factor of 10, and Naam expects batteries to drop ~10x ultimately regardless.
  • The cost mechanism is Wright's law, not Moore's law — roughly 30% off per cumulative doubling of scale. Solar is 8% of global electricity, leaving 4–6 doublings: a 4–8x decline, not 1000x. Dyson spheres would need far more.
  • The blocker in the US is land assembly, not economics — and the federal government is the largest landowner west of the Mississippi, concentrated in Nevada and Arizona. Naam: 'not something that interests the current administration.'
  • 85% of panels come from China and US tariffs double their price — 'we hurt ourselves by keeping them out.'
  • Geography works differently for sun than for oil: insolation varies at most 2x across inhabited places, while oil is wildly concentrated underground — solar's advantage is broad and shallow, and latitude matters far more than location.
  • Naam's siting picks are Australia above all (friendly government, land, world-class sun), then Chile and Mexico — though Chihuahua bans private behind-the-meter generation above ~500 kW, and labs want ironclad model-weight protection first.
  • Winter, not night, is the unsolved problem: London gets a sixth or seventh of the January insolation it gets in June, and electrifying heat with heat pumps roughly doubles Northern European winter demand.

“The problem for solar is not cost and it's not nighttime, because batteries are plunging in cost and are going to drop another 10x ultimately. It is winter.”— Ramez Naam

Nuclear's problem is that we do it rarely — and AI may be the best thing that ever happened to it

Fission is ruinously expensive outside China and South Korea for one reason: you never get good at what you do occasionally — which is why the entire investable thesis is manufacturing repetition, not reactor physics.

  • The cheapest gigawatts are the boring ones: stop closing plants (Germany shouldn't have), extend life, uprate, restart Three Mile Island — but that totals only a few GW.
  • China took the Westinghouse AP1000, localized the supply chain and built more than a dozen at 1.4 GW versus the West's roughly four at 1 GW. France stamped one light-water design out repeatedly and reached ~80% nuclear across ~60 plants; its newer EPR is a boondoggle.
  • Naam — a critic of this administration on other energy fronts — credits it with the best nuclear policy in recent memory, including loan guarantees for about eight large reactors. First units always overrun; costs sort out after three to five.
  • The SMR case is that manufacturing gets cheaper and construction does not — so move as much as possible into a factory with the fewest moving parts. Naam is skeptical of the awkward middle (Natrium, BWRX-300) that still requires field assembly. The awkward middle he distrusts is the ~1/3 GW class (Natrium, BWRX-300): factory-made components but field assembly, capturing neither large-plant economies nor factory learning rates.
  • Gen 3+/4 designs are passive safe — built to survive a 747 impact plus total grid loss. Fukushima melted down because grid-powered coolant pumps died with the transmission lines.
  • Fusion's quiet regulatory win: the NRC regulates it like hospital radiological imaging rather than like fission, decided during COVID. Physically it cannot melt down — fission is default-on, fusion is default-off — though neutron damage still forces parts replacement every ~5 years.
  • No, you can't put a reactor beside every GPU: criticality sets a minimum size, and radiothermal generators deliver too little power per unit mass at too high a cost to leave satellites and deep-space probes.
  • Hyperscaler SMR announcements are 'still kind of a fiction' on Naam's read — outside the five-year investable window — even as data center pull gives every nuclear company a massive tailwind.

Orbit is a launch-cadence problem; the ocean is quietly the nearer moonshot

Space compute pencils out only when launch gets 4–10x cheaper, and Elon's 100 GW target implies a Starship launch every 15 minutes — while wave-powered data centers are already floating and targeting 2 cents a kilowatt-hour.

  • One gigawatt in orbit on SpaceX's design takes about six times SpaceX's best launch year and twice its cumulative all-time launch mass. 10 GW/yr means 1,500–2,000 launches a year — five or six Starships a day.
  • Musk's stated ambition is 100 GW/yr in orbit before 2030, roughly today's entire world compute (~80 GW), first launch 2028. At 20–30 satellites per Starship that's ~30,000 launches a year, one every 15 minutes.
  • Naam's constraint isn't the rocket, it's permission: he doubts the world tolerates more than ~200 Starship launches a year — already 20,000 tons to orbit, several times all human launch to date — and FAA reliability rules ground you after one explosion.
  • The inversion worth holding: AI is a gift to SpaceX, not the reverse. Starlink alone can't finance Mars-level cadence and Mars has no business model — AI demand is what pays to drive launch cost toward marginal cost.
  • Space is also a regulatory hedge — no interconnect queue, no local opposition, no permitting — which is why it survives being more expensive than land.
  • The ocean version: a bobbing 80-metre column open at the bottom drives a turbine on wave motion near-continuously, targeting ~2 cents/kWh, with free cooling from 40°F water that appears to reduce GPU failures. Three units are in the water, a fourth launching soon; Peter Thiel led the most recent round. Naam passed at seed five years ago and bought in twice this year at far higher valuations.
  • Advanced geothermal is the possible fifth path — Fervo in the US, Eavor in the UK, Quaise drilling with plasma beams — opening cheap geothermal anywhere rather than only near crustal hot spots.

Names and terms at a glance

Glossary & Entities

Interconnection queue — The waiting line to connect a new power plant or a new large load to the grid — now ~45 months on the generation side in the US, versus 15 months two decades ago.
Behind the meter — Generation built on-site and connected directly to the load, bypassing the grid and its queue entirely — currently mostly natural gas turbines.
Interruptible load (CLR/PCLR) — A grid customer that lets the operator curtail its power at peak; under Texas's June rule this cuts interconnection from five to seven years down to 12–18 months.
Four nines — The grid reliability bar: 99.9% uptime still means 8 hours of outage a year, which Americans treat as unacceptable, so 99.99% is the real target. That is why accepting 1% downtime is a real concession and buys such a large interconnection discount.
Wright's law / learning rate — Cost falls by a fixed percentage per cumulative doubling of production — ~30% for solar. Unlike Moore's law it's driven by volume, not time, which is why remaining declines are bounded by remaining doublings.
Warm shell — A powered, cooled, ready-to-populate data center building — the thing Nadella identified as scarcer than GPUs.
SMR / micro-reactor — Small modular reactors built largely in factories rather than on-site; micro-reactors shrink further to shipping-container scale. The bet is factory learning rates, since construction costs don't fall.
Passive safety — A reactor design that cannot melt down without external power — heat removal happens by physics, not pumps. Fukushima failed because its coolant pumps ran on the grid.
Triple product — Temperature × pressure × confinement duration — fusion's progress metric, marching up and to the right on a log-log scale since 1956. NIF crossed 1 with a theoretical, not practical, net energy gain.
Tokamak / FRC — The two leading fusion geometries here: tokamaks are magnet-confined donuts (ITER, Commonwealth Fusion); field-reversed configuration machines slam plasmas together like rail guns and can capture energy directly as current (Helion).
pB11 — Proton-boron-11 fusion — a harder fuel with a much bigger prize: potentially 1–3 cents per kWh, and compact enough to imagine powering aircraft or interplanetary propulsion.
Capacity factor — The fraction of nameplate power a plant actually delivers over time — why wave power near Antarctica beats wave power near where people live.
