Iran, oil and the US Treasury's two-front fight

The Iran conflict revived fears over oil and rates, but Valentin insists it's a passing episode, not a long-term trend driver.

  • Iranian natural-gas facilities were hit and Iran's power grid is being sabotaged by the US; Iran in turn strikes US targets in the region (not just military bases, but facilities of major companies too).
  • Concrete market risk: data centers of companies like Google, Amazon or Microsoft in Bahrain, the UAE, Oman or Qatar could be targets — Iran has threatened to cut the region's internet cables before but hasn't done so.
  • If oil rises sustainably (not just a short geopolitical spike), Valentin concedes inflation could rise and the Fed would face hard decisions — but he thinks this kind of shock stays short-lived.
  • The conflict could intensify depending on the US midterms: if Republicans don't win a chamber to back Trump's agenda, the Iran situation could take an unexpected turn.
  • Monetary front: Japan's 10-year yield is easing slightly, but the 2-year keeps accelerating; the ECB and the US Treasury appear to be actively supporting the yen, because yen borrowing has propped up every asset class for 20-30 years.
  • The US Treasury, in Valentin's telling, is fighting on two fronts: buying back its own bonds while printing money to buy yen and support Japan's currency.

The labor market cools and the US consumer starts trimming the budget

Converging signals point to slightly rising unemployment and a US consumer trading down — enough, Valentin says, that the Fed can no longer hike.

  • The implied odds of a rate hike hit 68% at one point this week, then shifted quickly — before the official US jobs report.
  • Valentin expects slightly rising unemployment for a longer stretch and fewer new jobs in each report — partly because temporary jobs tied to the summer World Cup are disappearing.
  • His argument: with unemployment rising and new jobs falling, the Fed can't afford to "sabotage the economy" by raising rates.
  • On inflation, he expects a decline because the US took full control of Venezuela's oil reserves, which should lower pump prices and, indirectly, goods transport costs.
  • Signal from Costco: Americans are shifting from beef to chicken, canned tuna and pork — an early retail-chain signal of an ever-tighter consumer budget.
  • Similar signal: more Americans are dropping regular restaurants for McDonald's; Valentin says he saw the same signal at Walmart, which he considers, alongside Google, an attractive stock for a moderate-dynamic risk profile right now.
Worth noting
The falling-inflation reading assumes lower oil prices pass fully into consumer prices — Valentin himself isn't sure whether retailers will actually cut prices or just avoid raising them.

Earnings week: Broadcom isn't Nvidia, and Dell stunned everyone

A wave of strong reports exposed a bad Broadcom-Nvidia comparison and an active repositioning by Valentin toward companies with real doubling potential.

  • Broadcom (AVGO) fell 5% despite a good report; many analysts wrongly compared it to Nvidia — Valentin calls that "absurd," like "Renault versus Mercedes," because Nvidia remains the absolute engine of semiconductors.
  • His explanation for Broadcom's drop: guidance came in below market expectations, possibly on purpose, since Broadcom tends to lowball expectations to beat them later — it stays on his short buy list, next to AMD, below a certain price, with revenue acceleration expected into 2027-2028.
  • On Nvidia (NVDA): Valentin sold long ago, re-entered and took profits, but now sees very little chance it rises another 100% in the next 2-3 years — so he wants to move capital to companies with real doubling potential.
  • UiPath (PATH) reported strongly and rose from $11-13 toward $18, a level Valentin had anticipated; his advice to UiPath employees holding stock in their pay package is to rebalance gradually (15%, then 15%, then 20% at higher prices), not wait in "hope investing" for a return to $40-70 without fundamentals to support it.
  • Snowflake (SNOW) had an extraordinary rebound over the past 6 months, after being ignored as Palantir's former "shadow competitor" — Valentin now sees it as overvalued and would think twice before buying after such a run, though a high forward P/E can be justified by very high annual growth (as with Palantir (PLTR) or CrowdStrike (CRWD)).
  • AppLovin (APP) is seen similarly — well off its all-time high, with explosive rebound potential after 2-3 more good reports.
  • Dell (DELL) had the best report of the week — revenue, EPS and profitability guidance all "superlative" — a company once seen as dated ("a kind of IBM") that repositioned aggressively into AI data-center infrastructure; Valentin thinks it's no longer worth buying after such an explosive rise.

Elon Musk: the next crisis is energy, not semiconductors

Musk's G20 remarks on an energy-crisis risk open a debate on how an investor positions in a sector with too many possible directions.

  • At the G20, Musk said the biggest problem for data centers and AI infrastructure is energy, not chips — an energy crisis looms; he proposes solar combined with gas and fossil fuels.
  • Political context: Trump reportedly told infrastructure companies to build as much as they want, but to disconnect from the public grid so as not to add pressure on it.
  • Musk also discussed data centers in orbit with constant solar access — but Valentin notes that would be far more complex (different semiconductors, different cooling).
  • Investment frame: you can't invest in every energy direction at once (solar, nuclear, gas, oil) — China is cited as a country that massively built wind (center and north) and solar (south), cutting its oil dependence, including large wind farms toward Mongolia.
  • For the US, Valentin recommends a nuclear-plus-solar mix and points to companies like Constellation Energy and small modular reactor makers as a growth area.
  • Vacation anecdote: a Greek head waiter from Davos told Valentin about Davos Forum talk on falling birth rates and the need for robots (from kitchen robots and vacuums to humanoids) after imported-labor policies failed (Germany and other European countries, with workers from Pakistan and elsewhere).
  • Valentin ties this to Tesla (TSLA): before his vacation he pointed members to the stock at $320-330, where a bottom formed; he sees potential in robotaxi, megapack energy storage and Optimus robots, which will work on production lines first and later become a high-margin commercial product.

Cybersecurity stays expensive, but Valentin buys aggressively on every panic

Anthropic's partnerships with CrowdStrike and OpenAI confirm Valentin's thesis that cybersecurity is mandatory spend for any company adopting AI at scale.

  • Anthropic signed a partnership with CrowdStrike (CRWD) on API security — driven by the risk of big firms giving AI access to all their internal data; CrowdStrike rose on the news and fell back when Anthropic struck a similar deal with OpenAI, offsetting the move.
  • Added context: a China-originated hack of Anthropic, used as a further argument for massive cybersecurity investment as AI accelerates.
  • Valentin says he wasn't visionary, just understood early the sector would matter — he invested in CrowdStrike, Palo Alto Networks (PANW), Fortinet (FTNT) and SentinelOne (S); the sector was always expensive but justified by 3-5 year contracts (except 2022, when contracts shrank to 6-12 months amid economic uncertainty).
  • He recently sold CrowdStrike and Cloudflare (NET) at a profit "to fulfill a dream," but is willing to sell other holdings — or use family reserve funds — to buy both back at a good price.
  • He recounts buying CrowdStrike aggressively during a major company system glitch (when the stock fell from about $380 to $200-250) — many retail investors sold fearing bankruptcy, but Valentin saw the opportunity; the stock later split 4-for-1, and he's now waiting for another such glitch to re-enter.
  • For an aggressive investor, he sees CrowdStrike and Cloudflare as the equivalent of an ideal Google-Amazon-Microsoft-Meta portfolio for a moderate-dynamic profile.

The biggest risk for Romania's stock market: nationalizing Pillar II pensions

After an aggressive BET rally, Valentin warns pension funds could be forced to sell, and the darkest scenario — nationalizing Pillar II — is not science fiction.

  • The BET index rose almost vertically, and many "experts" suddenly started predicting a correction — Valentin mocks them for waking up only after the move, with no technical analysis beforehand.
  • His core thesis: Pillar II pension funds must keep a minimum share in government bonds (cited as around 8.5%, targeting 7%); if the market rose too much, their equity allocation exceeds the limit and they're forced to sell — not because they think the market won't rise, but strictly for compliance with the fund prospectus.
  • Structural problem: if all pension funds want to sell at once, who's the buyer? Romanian retail owns practically zero BVB shares, and foreign funds avoid the market for lack of liquidity, even though P/E multiples are very low and the market looks "cheap."
  • Romanian companies the pension funds could buy but are steered into government bonds instead: Banca Transilvania, OMV Petrom, Transgaz, Romgaz, Nuclearelectrica, Hidroelectrica.
  • The most serious risk Valentin raises: the Finance Ministry nationalizing Pillar II private pensions if Romania can no longer attract external liquidity — a precedent other countries already set; he says he wouldn't add voluntary money on top of the mandatory contribution precisely because of this political risk.
  • Other country risks listed: currency risk (the leu moved a lot over the past year), downgrade risk, regional risk (drone incidents, e.g. Tulcea) and European political risk (Marine Le Pen in France, a possible strong leader in Germany) that could bring harsh immigrant-deportation policies with spillover to Romania/Bulgaria.
  • Added context: a comment by Ursula von der Leyen about Europeans' money "sitting idle" in accounts and not being "productive" is seen by Valentin and Alex as infringing people's right to decide what to do with their own money.
  • His advice for Romanian investors: after such an explosive BET rise, consider an exit from the BVB and a reallocation to US companies (or, selectively, good European companies, like the defense names earlier this year).

Names and terms at a glance

Glossary & Entities

Pillar II private pensions — Romania's mandatory private pension system, where part of payroll contributions goes automatically to privately managed pension funds.
BET — The Bucharest Stock Exchange's main index of the most traded Romanian companies.
PEG ratio — A stock's P/E divided by its earnings growth rate — a low PEG suggests the price isn't excessive relative to how fast the company grows.
Hope investing — Valentin's term for holding a position only in the hope the price will recover, without concrete fundamentals to support it.
13F — The mandatory quarterly filing where large US funds disclose their holdings — used by Alex to read institutional positioning.
