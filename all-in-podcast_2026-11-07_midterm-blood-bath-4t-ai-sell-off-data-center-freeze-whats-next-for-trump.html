The midterm rout: war-driven inflation, or a broken economy?

All four agree affordability decided the vote; they split on whether the Iran war caused it or only exposed it.

  • Results: Democrats at 54, 55 or 56 Senate seats (Alaska, Mississippi still counting), at least 250-260 House seats, 29+ governorships; Republicans lost ground with every group, even white men.
  • Sacks: gas rose from about $3 to over $4, diesel higher; inflation went from 2.4% in January to 3.7%, rate cuts turned into a hike, mortgages hit about 7.5%; families pay $200 a month more on fuel.
  • Sacks' charts: Trump's net approval tracks the gas price; the Senate prediction market only turned after the war began on February 28, 2026; 34% called the war decisive and 40% significant, so 74% in all.
  • Sacks admits he expected a 'red splash': jobs strong, unemployment 4.1%, GDP 2.1-2.5% in H1 and 3.7% in Q3 per GDPNow, real household income up 3.1% in year one; young men swung 36 points and the podcasters (Tucker Carlson, Joe Rogan, Theo Von, Tim Dillon, Megyn Kelly) left.
  • Jason: the slide began before the war; per Nate Silver's Silver Bulletin, approval went from +5 to -28, via Liberation Day tariffs (lost at the Supreme Court), masked ICE raids, and the Epstein files; he now votes 55/45 Democrat, from 70/30.
  • Chamath: money and gerrymandering failed (of 15 redrawn Republican districts, Democrats won 11); centrists focused only on affordability won, so ideology matters less than a broken economy.
  • Friedberg: the end of DOGE on June 5, 2025 ('China just won') left a $7 trillion budget with a $2 trillion deficit; costs keep rising, so voters drift to socialist policies without choosing socialism.

Trump's path back: end the war, then strike populist deals

Everyone puts ending the Iran war first; beyond that the prescriptions run from tax deals to blanket pardons.

  • Sacks: Brent fell 10% on Election Day as markets priced an end to the war; he sees it going from about $117-110 to $70, and Trump's approval back above 40%.
  • Sacks' precedents: Clinton (1994) and Obama (2010, minus 7 Senate and 63 House seats) adjusted and won again; Bush doubled down with the Iraq surge after 2006 and Republicans lost 2008; neocons now push a similar escalation.
  • Jason's five-point plan: pardon everyone now; tax margin loans over $10 million at 50% capital gains, credited back on repayment; a health plan; $15 minimum wage via $2 a year for four years (only 10-15 million earn under $15); a stock-trading ban for officials (Rahm Emanuel's idea); a housing-construction plan.
  • Chamath: zero federal tax on income under about $150k (later $125k), end federal loan guarantees with student-debt cancellation, close loopholes; trade those for keeping the border closed.
  • Chamath: like Reagan, tariffs plus allocated funds could look better as domestic production builds; the 2028 winner will run on economic populism, left or right.
  • Friedberg expects Congress and the President to fail to agree a budget in 2027, so costs keep rising; the bond market will signal where it goes.

The data-center freeze: 2027 capex holds, the 2028 pipeline thins

Data centers became the voters' symbol of oligarchy, and Sacks thinks no politician will sign new permits for years.

  • About $4 trillion of market cap was erased early in the week, the worst day in dollar terms; it overshadowed the Anthropic IPO.
  • Polymarket odds of an OpenAI IPO this year fell to 71% from 95% before the midterms; the WSJ reports a valuation near $1 trillion versus $1.5-2 trillion (and Gavin Baker's $3-4 trillion on this podcast).
  • Texas governor-elect Gina Hinojosa beat Abbott on economic populism, tying data centers to higher utility bills and water use; she says no new permits until legislation passes.
  • Sacks disagrees with Gavin Baker's calm read; a SemiAnalysis analyst notes projects under construction may still lack final permits (e.g. air emissions), risking half-finished sites.
  • Sacks' base case: fully permitted projects finish in 2027, but by 2028 the pipeline is closed or sharply reduced; both parties ran ads attacking politicians who approved data centers.
  • Sacks: a gigawatt data center costs about $50 billion, and nobody commits that under injunction or protest risk; he expects Democrats to blame Trump for a 2028 capex drop.
  • Chamath: a SpaceX team (Riley and Katie) pitched a 6 GW site in Cochise County, Arizona (40% lower power costs, no water, 20% lower property taxes) and was shouted down; it is emotional, not factual.
  • Jason: blocked projects move to Canada, Mexico, Singapore, Malaysia (where Google is building a huge one) or the Gulf after the war, making the US a net importer of tokens and losing $300-400k construction jobs.

Scarce compute makes hyperscalers kingmakers, and one 90-day clause the key move

Chamath argues a slowdown helps those who already own capacity and squeezes tenants: neoclouds and frontier labs.

  • Winners: Amazon, Meta, Microsoft, Google, SpaceX own the capacity; neoclouds and foundation-model labs are tenants and at risk.
  • Option two: hyperscalers keep new capacity for their own models (Google to Gemini) to slow Anthropic and OpenAI, which improve on 60-90 day cycles.
  • The 'crazy way': the SpaceX–Anthropic compute deal has a mutual 90-day termination; repricing at spot could lift SpaceX's Anthropic revenue 2-3x and force capitulation.
  • That move would push neoclouds to buy all compute they can at any price, or face bankruptcy.
  • Signal: Chamath says Google just took control of a Crusoe data center and evicted the tenant, because it holds power and other key resources.
  • Jason disagrees: Elon's close ties to Anthropic and hostility toward Sam Altman make a middle compromise likelier; Chamath says it is math, and rising spot prices make old contracts unprofitable.
  • Chamath: if building slows, cost per megawatt-month and GPU-hour soar; capacity could go to Google or Meta. Friedberg adds compute both serves users and trains the next model, so Google, Microsoft or Nvidia hoarding it could decide the frontier.

Who controls AI: compute rules, a federal AI department, or regulatory capture

Friedberg and Sacks fear state control of AI; Chamath expects a deal that favors incumbents; Jason calls it Doctor Doom.

  • Friedberg: AI is like Sauron's ring; governments will regulate compute rather than chips or models, and treat data centers as public utilities.
  • Friedberg: models are code and a free-speech question; existing laws already cover misuse; a state with better AI than its citizens is a Second Amendment-style power gap.
  • Jason puts Friedberg's scenario at about 1%; left politicians want to ban data centers, models and their use; California passed a bill Newsom vetoed, and states pass a patchwork of hundreds of laws.
  • Sacks: it is 100% clear Democrats push AI legislation; a federal Department of AI pre-approving frontier models would build a surveillance and censorship state, like COVID-era social media.
  • Sacks: federal level handles AI safety; infrastructure gets decided at state and local level.
  • Chamath: no nationalization; expect 'service for service', with more power for government and regulatory moats for closed-model duopolies, while Meta, Amazon, Google, Microsoft and SpaceX stay the real owners.
  • Friedberg: Gallup finds 93% of Chinese optimistic on AI versus 36% of Americans, fourth from the bottom, below Afghanistan; that made the oligarch narrative easy.

OpenAI says it refuted the exponential time hypothesis

Friedberg presents an OpenAI paper claiming 3-SAT can be solved in subexponential time, a 27-year-old open question.

  • The 1999 exponential time hypothesis says 3-SAT needs time exponential in problem size.
  • OpenAI's 175-page paper claims a subexponential method; done in under 10 days, some reports say hours.
  • Friedberg reads it as keeping P = NP possible, meaning any problem could in theory be computed.
  • The proof is still being assessed by outside researchers.
  • Sacks: AI will bring many benefits; if the US doesn't build it, China will, and the US will import tokens.
