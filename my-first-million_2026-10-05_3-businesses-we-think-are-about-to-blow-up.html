Oxytocin as the next biohack: a "magic pill" for pair bonding

A near-unregulated bonding hormone is being repackaged as a divorce and loneliness fix, and the hosts think consumer telehealth brands are next to sell it.

  • Palmer Luckey (Oculus, Anduril founder) answered "what would you start with from zero?" with oxytocin doping for marriage counseling; the hosts rate the pitch itself a textbook first-principles answer (divorce hits ~50% of people).
  • What it is: the hormone released in mothers at birth, in men after sex, and when you make eye contact with your dog; prairie voles with high levels bond for life.
  • Human studies cited are tiny: people on oxytocin sat ~15 cm further from an attractive stranger and looked at them less; it acts like a "highlighter", amplifying the existing feeling (warmth, or distance from an abusive partner) rather than acting as a love potion.
  • Where it is sold: a nasal spray (not a pill or injection) bought as powder from chemical suppliers (Sigma-Aldrich named, ~1 kg available) or via "sciences/pharmaceuticals" sites with no prescription; the Reddit community r/oxytocin, largely autistic and PTSD users, keeps daily dose journals.
  • A doctor the host heard says small teams given low doses worked together better, but there is not much data; efficacy and safety are unstudied.
  • The prediction: Meta ads promising to "cure divorce" within 12 months, with Hims and BlueChew as likely sellers of a nasal-spray version.
  • Host's own test: 30-60 seconds of silent eye contact with a spouse; one host says it makes his wife cry within 30 seconds.

Fringe health ideas go mainstream faster than people expect

The hosts' edge is spotting biohacks early (GLP-1s, TRT, IVF), and they argue social acceptance shifts quickly once the first product lands.

  • Their prior early calls: GLP-1s before Ozempic reached market, TRT businesses (they invested in one that worked), sperm freezing, and IVF, where the open idea is a trusted chain, "the Chick-fil-A of IVF".
  • Peptides: a friend's BPC-157 "Wolverine stack" for an Achilles injury sounded crazy years ago; peptides are now "shockingly common".
  • Eli Lilly (LLY) just bought a psychedelics company (captions render it "Atea Beckley") for roughly $2.8-3B, called the biggest psychedelic acquisition ever; psychedelics went from "tripping" to a PTSD intervention among smart friends.
  • The GLP-1 precedent: "there's no magic pill for weight loss" used to be a saying; now there is one, so a pill for bonding sounds less far-fetched.
  • Feelings are framed as a chemical brain state with a conscious readout, so safely nudging the chemistry is a real product category.

Monetize loneliness: interest communities that sell commodity financial products

Build belonging first, then sell insurance and similar products to people who prefer you because you are their community.

  • Knights of Columbus: founded 1882 by a New Haven priest (Fr. McGivney) after a parishioner died and left a family unsupported; ~200 men pooled money, which became life insurance.
  • Today ~2-3M members, ~$30B in assets, ~$300/yr dues, ~14,000 local chapters, revenue in the single-digit billions (speaker unsure), mostly from life insurance, and a powerful lobby.
  • Growth signal: the fastest-growing segment is men aged 30-40, after decades of mostly older members.
  • Same template elsewhere: Good Sam (RV owners, discounts and RV insurance) and the USCCCA (concealed-carry training plus legal-defense insurance, ~$300/yr, ~$400M revenue).
  • AARP (captioned "AP") makes billions by licensing its name to a health-insurance partner (United); it is the trusted brand and marketing channel, not the insurer.
  • Healthcare-share programs (often religious) pool ~$1,000/month instead of ~$2,000 to Blue Cross, and the group covers a $40,000 surgery, but payout is not contractual; tax benefits exist for some mutual-benefit structures but are not required.
  • The model in one line: membership gives belonging plus discounts that justify the fee, and the real money is the commodity financial service sold to members; most partner with an insurer rather than carry the risk.

Angel Studios: values-based studio run by its members

A filter built for family-safe movies turned into a roughly top-10 studio by letting ~3M paying members vote on what gets made.

  • Origin: the founder (a Latter-day Saint who did viral marketing for Poo-Pourri and Squatty Potty) built a filter that cut cursing, sex and drug scenes from films like Cinderella Man; he was sued and paid ~$10M.
  • Workaround: rent the DVD for $1, then stream the filtered version; after repeated suits he switched to making clean original content.
  • Scale: ~$450M ARR at ~$13-20/month, ~2-3M members; the host calls it about the 10th biggest studio in the world, and it is public.
  • Members watch a trailer or snippet and vote; under 70% approval the deal is dead, and its hit rate and Rotten Tomatoes scores beat the big studios.
  • The Chosen (a show about Jesus, season 7) is claimed at 200M viewers; one in ten Angel titles is religious, and the rule is no cursing, drinking or drugs, with heroes. Current film: The Runner (Owen Wilson, a stolen-liver thriller).
  • Marketer's pattern: a Salt Lake cluster of strong marketers, many Latter-day Saint-affiliated.

A24 as a reaction to the action, and why movie math is brutal

Studios chased safe four-quadrant sequels, which left cheap, edgy indie films and guerrilla marketing for A24, but even a hit returns only about 2-3x.

  • Framework: great business ideas are the reaction to the action: Facebook's permanent photos produced Snapchat; abundant calories produced GLP-1s; endless screens produced screen-time-limiting apps and bricks.
  • Four quadrants (male/female, under/over 30): studios fund Spider-Man 7 and Harry Potter (HBO is putting ~$1B into the new series) because they hit all four; that left unproven scripts uncompetitively cheap for A24.
  • A24's marketing: Kevin James ran a fake painter-teacher Instagram, then sat at the Super Bowl in a tuxedo holding flowers with 10 empty seats beside him and paid influencers nearby; Timothée Chalamet's trailer was a fake leaked Zoom recording.
  • One host's example hit: $14M to make + $40M marketing = $54M, $250M box office, 50% to theaters = ~$125M, about a 2.3x return that must cover ~6 flops.
  • Shooting takes only ~30-37 days; marketing spend rivals or beats production for films and big video games (the host suggests GTA).
  • Why films get made: "Have you ever met a billionaire who doesn't want to make a movie?"; a film write-off requires production to actually start, so $1M in might give a ~$300K write-off and still a likely loss.
  • Book analogy: Mark Manson's book sold ~20M copies and, per his story, outsold Hillary Clinton and carried the publisher's profit year; hits drive everything in creative businesses.

Names and terms at a glance

Glossary & Entities

Oxytocin — A natural hormone released at birth, after sex and during eye contact that promotes pair bonding.
Prairie vole — A monogamous rodent used in studies of oxytocin and lifelong pair bonding.
Reaction to the action — A business-idea method: build the counter-product to whatever trend currently dominates.
Four-quadrant movie — A film aimed at males and females, both under and over 30, giving the largest audience.
Mutual benefit society — A membership group whose members pool money to cover each other, the root of fraternal insurance.
Healthcare share program — A community pool that pays members' medical bills without a binding insurance contract.
