From a 1,000-website agency to a €500M exit: Trilulilu and LiveRail

Two young founders with nothing to lose built a local YouTube, and a free-equity deal with a UK client turned into the real payday.

  • Started at 17 building websites; at 24-25 he and Andrei Dunca were doing ~1,000 sites in an agency and felt 'like a hamster on a wheel', so they built their own product.
  • Trilulilu (2006, the year Google bought YouTube for $1.6B) was a localized video/image/audio site; valued at €1M in 2007 and €2.5M in 2008.
  • By 2011 it was Romania's most visited site: 3M unique visitors a month against ~5M internet users, but it ran ~56 servers and an in-house sysadmin team — extremely costly.
  • UK client Mark Trevgard wanted video-advertising tech (2007, before skippable ads existed); they offered to build it free for co-founder equity. Trilulilu became its first client, likely a top-three European site with video ads.
  • LiveRail raised only €12M from a single London fund, hit ~€100M revenue, ~€15M profit, 100+ staff (about 60 developers in Cluj), and sold to Facebook for €500M in 2014.
  • At times it served more video ad impressions in a month than Google; clients included CBS News and Major League Baseball.
  • Sale day: he and his wife drank champagne in the kitchen — 'like winning the lottery', a bet made seven years earlier.

Zonga: seven years in a business he should have closed after three

Chasing Trilulilu's music traffic into a subscription streaming product defocused the team and ran into a music-label oligopoly.

  • Biggest regret on Trilulilu: lost focus — building two products with different markets and business models on one team.
  • Zonga (launched 2012) was a Spotify-style paid service when Romanians still pirated via torrents.
  • Label talks: Universal Music's Eastern Europe head opened with 'why should I give you my music when I have 15 others queuing?' Universal, Sony and Warner hold 60%+ of the market; Universal alone ~35%.
  • Deal terms: a €400,000/year minimum guarantee for Romania alone, structured as the greater of that, a per-user fee, or share of listens times revenue — leaving ~10-15% margin for marketing.
  • Bluffed both sides: told labels the Vodafone deal and finished product existed, told Vodafone the label deals existed; both aligned within nine months. The French investor Alexis Bond (earlier exit via last.fm, ~$1B+) smoothed the label deal.
  • Peaked at 120,000 paying customers, break-even — but Vodafone CEO turnover (~every 4 years) and a CFO taking decisions squeezed it.
  • He put in €500,000 of his own money, took no salary for three years; final idea was franchising the platform with local labels (Greece: 80-90% local music), but the Greek label would not advance even a few thousand euros. Closed in 2019; 'nobody cared'.
  • Recommends Netflix's The Playlist about Spotify's early years — he lived the same things.

Elrond / MultiversX: a metaverse bet that never got its wave

He joined for the metaverse-plus-blockchain thesis, ran a regulated finance arm, and left when the role became management.

  • Was already an Elrond holder before joining; Beniamin Mincu convinced him over ~9 months of late-night talks.
  • Thesis: a metaverse needs a blockchain as an immutable ledger for ownership; a wallet could become the user's identity, so winnings could be spent in real life.
  • Metaverse never reached mass adoption: 'the tide lifts all boats' only works if the wave comes, unlike with AI.
  • Elrond bought a company with the only e-money licence in Romania at the time; he launched a Visa/Mastercard debit card and was general director for ~1.5 years after passing the BNR approval exam.
  • Left after ~4 years: AI arrived, he was turning into a manager, and 'I'm not a manager, I'm a builder'.
  • Asked if he could have made €100M had it gone perfectly: 'maybe 100 — but what's the difference between 80 and 100?'

One builder, ten Claude Code terminals, and Invoflux

With development cost near zero, he tests many products in parallel and keeps only those with product-market fit.

  • First ChatGPT reaction: 'a revolution like the iPhone' — told friends to remember it as T0, 'game over' for the old world.
  • A year ago the models lost context fast; today he runs ~6 Claude Code projects in parallel plus a 7th orchestrator that controls them and pushes to GitHub — about 10 terminals in all, often just from his phone.
  • He has not named the agents (T1, T2, T3...); if the orchestrator had a name it would be Celibidache.
  • Invoflux grew from his own pain across 2-3 SRLs: missing Booking invoices and receipts. Pillars: collect (email, WhatsApp, photo app, Chrome extension), understand (dashboards for revenue, expenses, cash flow, receivables), and share with the accountant.
  • Bank data comes via PSD2 open banking through a licensed provider, delivered like a CSV statement — the AI never has account access.
  • Not replacing accountants: the entrepreneur-accountant channel stays, he just 'clears the sludge' between them.
  • Host (reach: 3.3M Facebook views in 28 days, ~1M Instagram, ~3M TikTok, ~1M YouTube) offered to push a product and kill it if it flops.

Venture studio plus distribution: where the edge moves when building is free

If anyone can build, the winners are those who can reach buyers — and reaching them is a team sport.

  • Venture-studio model: launch many products with own resources, test product-market fit, pair winners with co-founders (e.g. a CTO for Invoflux, maybe a CEO), stay on as strategic investor.
  • 'Potential is infinite'; the difference will be teams with distribution and marketing — 'who have 3 million on TikTok'.
  • Market will flood with product clones; the Adidas-vs-Abibas gap is how it is built and how it is positioned.
  • A 25-year-old can't match him alone but can with a team of 3-5 friends; he doubts his own one-man model is sustainable.
  • If starting from zero: pick someone's problem, dissect it into ~7,000 pieces, build for the smallest one. Happy to copy a proven product (Nike, Adidas, Hoka each found a niche) — go vertical (restaurants, plumbers) or horizontal.
  • Programmers are 'fearful': entrepreneurship needs risk-taking and talking to clients. His line: the only way to be truly rich is equity in a business.

Jobs, juniors and humanoids: a hard transition, not an end of work

He expects programmers to survive as AI orchestrators while juniors and easily replaced roles vanish.

  • AI is 'the industrial revolution on steroids'; no industry or person escapes it; a rough transition period with confused, resistant people.
  • People will split into AI users, resisters and acceptors (the 'fork').
  • Programmers won't disappear: complex systems block autonomous AI, so they move to orchestration; juniors and easily replaced jobs will go; strong AI managers will earn well.
  • Musk's humanoid-robot vision: a billion robots, abundance, falling service costs, and universal basic income. The host's robot walked Cluj streets at Texilvania and drew public backlash.
  • Counterpoint raised: work gives purpose and belonging (his parents after retirement). Will people be creative or just idle?

Best Actionable Takeaways

🛑
Mindset: Set a kill date for any project up front; Zonga ran 3-4 years longer than it should have.
🎯
Focus: Run one product per team; the Trilulilu-plus-Zonga split is the stated cause of its decline.
🤖
AI workflow: Test ideas cheaply with a few parallel coding agents and one orchestrator before hiring.
📣
Distribution: Build an audience or a distribution partner before the product; build cost is no longer the moat.
🤝
Careers: Offer equity-for-work to a trusted client or partner; it produced LiveRail.
🏃

Names and terms at a glance

Glossary & Entities

Venture studio — A firm that launches products with its own resources, tests product-market fit, then attaches co-founders and stays on as strategic investor.
PSD2 / open banking — EU rules letting licensed providers share bank account data with a customer's consent.
Minimum guarantee — A fixed yearly payment owed to a rights holder regardless of how many users the service has.
Product-market fit — Evidence that enough customers really want the product to keep it alive.
