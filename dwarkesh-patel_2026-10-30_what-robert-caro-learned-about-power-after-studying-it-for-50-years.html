Perspective THE POWER BROKERpolicymindset

Can democracies build without a Moses?

Caro says gridlock is recurring, and it ends when someone with talent and will finds a way through.

  • In the 1920s people also said nothing could be built: highways were blocked by property owners. Moses saw Jones Beach, 27 miles from the city, and built the road anyway.
  • Dwarkesh: the Second Avenue Subway, nearly 50 years, 1.5 miles, over $4B; California spent $15B in a decade with no new rail line. China today resembles 1940s-50s New York.
  • Caro doubts there is a "right place" between the two; decades pass with no builder, then someone arrives. He sees this in Johnson on civil rights and Moses on public works.
  • The Great Depression helped: Moses hired thousands of idle architects for very low wages and fired them at will.
  • Cheap skilled labor also explains ornate old buildings, like stone carving on a rooftop only two neighboring apartments can see.
Pull quote
“I certainly do n't want someone as ruthless and defiant of public opinion as Robert Moses. But at the same time, you need someone who can find a way to make things happen.”— Robert Caro

How power hardens an idealist

Moses went from civil-service reformer to a builder deaf to all opposition, and Caro's one question for him would be whether he noticed.

  • Young Moses pushed merit-based civil service and devoted hundreds of pages' worth of energy to giving city people beaches.
  • He became a man who built regardless: the Cross Bronx Expressway evicted tens of thousands without relocation help and opened to huge traffic jams.
  • When Bronx politician Henry Epstein proposed a route two blocks south that would evict only a few hundred, Moses destroyed him by exposing an affair.
  • Stacked posts (State Power Authority, Triborough, parks) made him nearly impossible to fire, until his resignation threat in 1962 got "I regretfully accept" from Nelson Rockefeller.
  • Caro's last interview: no phone messages were waiting, and he felt the pain of someone losing power. Moses, in his 80s, never realized it.
Pull quote
“Do you realize you're changing?”— Robert Caro, his one question for Moses
Worth noting
Moses stopped speaking to Caro after a seventh interview raised a parkway rerouted to spare a donor's golf course.
Perspective MASTER OF THE SENATEpolicymindset

Johnson: persuasion backed by leverage

The arm around the shoulder worked because every senator knew Johnson could grant or withhold what they needed.

  • Senators knew Johnson controlled their committee assignments and the bills they wanted passed.
  • John Connally explained Johnson's notes on donations ("OK 1,500", or 500, or nothing): some congressmen were never going to take Johnson's money.
  • A Kennedy-era Johnson as majority leader would not have passed the 1964-65 laws; Kennedy had charm and foreign-policy skill but not the drive to change America.
  • Teaching poor children in Cotulla shaped Johnson's education and anti-poverty agenda, and Vietnam wrecked it. Caro is still working out why Johnson escalated.
  • Caro wished Coke Stevenson ("Mr. Texas") had won the 1948 Senate race; he never imagined anyone would do what Johnson did to win it.
  • Both men's energy came from willpower, not physiology; Johnson made calls at 2 and 3 a.m.
Pull quote
“There was power behind his persuasion.”— Robert Caro
Practical 50 YEARS OF METHODcareermindset

Caro's craft: one sentence, every document, every interview

He refuses "no" from sources, reads every document, and anchors each book to a one-sentence purpose.

  • Before starting, he sits at his desk until he can say what the book is about in one sentence or one paragraph, a lesson learned the hard way on The Power Broker.
  • He read LaGuardia's papers in file cabinets three floors below the 79th Street Boat Basin, sometimes finding Moses's own notes.
  • The Power Broker manuscript was about 1,050,000 words; Bob Gottlieb cut about 350,000 to fit one volume, including material Caro still mourns.
  • Digital archives speed up finding documents (for example oral histories for his Vietnam volume), but finding is minor next to reading them.
  • About 700 interviews for The Power Broker alone; Sam Rayburn still lacks a good biography and the material sits in Caro's files.
  • He gave his archive to the New York Historical Society for much less money than other offers, so researchers can reach it easily.
Pull quote
“Bob, you learned that you have to sum it up in one sentence”— Robert Caro

Loneliness on the frontier and at the desk

Hill Country isolation shaped Johnson, and Caro's own solitude is the price of concentration.

  • Johnson grew up on a farm outside Johnson City; Caro had to cut pages on his mother's isolation, walking up a hill and seeing only hills.
  • Interviewing elderly neighbors meant driving 22 miles without passing another house or person.
  • He thinks America still doesn't understand the heroism of frontier families living in that solitude.
  • Caro turns off his phone every morning and talks to no one all day, and returns calls only at night, which many friends don't understand.
Pull quote
“I come here in the morning and turn off my phone. I'm not going to talk to anyone all day.”— Robert Caro
What to do with this

Best Actionable Takeaways

✍️
Writing: Write the one-sentence purpose of a long project before starting, and test every chapter against it.
🏗
Policy: Judge building reform by whether it lets a builder through without licensing a Moses.
🤝
Leadership: Build real leverage first; persuasion works when people know you can deliver.
🧭
Mindset: Ask yourself periodically whether you're changing, since power hardens people without their noticing.
