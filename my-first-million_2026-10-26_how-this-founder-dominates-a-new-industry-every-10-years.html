How this founder dominates a new industry every 10 years

Channel: My First Million · Speaker: Nick Kokonas (Alinea Group, Tock), Shaan Puri · 2026-10-26

The one-paragraph takeaway

Video Snapshot

  1. Nick Kokonas: options trader, then co-founder of Alinea (called the best restaurant in America), then Tock, sold to Squarespace for hundreds of millions. A new career roughly every 10 years.
  2. His thesis: every arbitrage is a question of timescale. The floor's 30-second paper ticket and the restaurant phone call were the same gap.
  3. Trading arb: a direct New York-Chicago line between QQQ and Nasdaq futures. The firm reached a couple % of AMEX daily volume within six months and made over $100 million in a year. He was 31.
  4. Alinea was built in public on the eGullet forum before it opened, and designed like theater and close-up magic rather than like other restaurants.
  5. Restaurants are a skill issue: Alinea went from $5M in sales at 7-8% margins to $20M+ at 18-20%, paid quarterly distributions for 18 years, and returned 27-28x overall.
  6. Tock exit: 896 diligence questions answered in three days, no investment bank, and a deal closed against Squarespace's March 31 IPO deadline.
  7. Next boulder: Strange House with magician Nate Staniforth. It's an immersive illusion venue in 90,000 sq ft under Wall Street, betting that in-person wonder gains value as AI spreads.
Principle A NEW CAREER EVERY ~10 YEARSmindsetcareer

A tingle of nausea means you're taking the right amount of risk

Kokonas treats discomfort as the signal he's still learning, and quits a field once he becomes an expert in it.

  • The rule came from former partner Keith Goggin in the 90s, while they held a 'ridiculous' short on Plug Power: feeling great means too little risk, throwing up means too much.
  • House rule from his wife: AT&A, short for all talk, no action. It got him to make a lowball bid on the Napa property he'd watched for 30 years. The seller accepted within 15 minutes, uncountered. He was 55.
  • Puri's parallels: Moiz Ali, founder of Native deodorant, would 'know everything' about deodorant within six months, and his brother sets up 'a new prison of my own choosing' every decade.
  • Expertise is a trap: someone younger and hungrier will break convention and beat you, so quit before that happens. It gets harder with age, because success removes the incentive.
  • Naval: the truly great ones go back to the bottom of the mountain. He keeps his father's Sisyphus statue on his desk ('the work is the win') and is now building a Napa wine bar he didn't want to build.
  • At the Napa Valley Grape Growers keynote he told the region it isn't owed $38,000 a ton after 30 years of growth. His own grapes won't be good until he's 70.

Personal story CHICAGO TRADING FLOORS, 1990S-2002financecareer

From $5.25/hour runner to $100M+ a year on a 20-second edge

He got into the arena by taking a job he was overqualified for, then found an arb by bypassing the paper ticket that made the market slow.

  • Philosophy major with programming skills. He left college off his resume to get a $5.25/hour runner job at the Chicago Mercantile Exchange. His goal was to be trading within a year, and he made it with a week to spare.
  • Found a geeky University of Chicago quant trading small and offered to pay to work for him; the quant paid him $250/week instead. He started on Swiss franc options with $60K ($20K his own, $40K from a backer), which he calls badly undercapitalized.
  • Rule for the 30-40 traders he trained: make one trade on day one in the pit. Puri likens it to Facebook engineers shipping code on day one. Kokonas recruited 'corporate refugees' (unhappy lawyers) at $7/hour and had them trading within eight months.
  • The arb: QQQ ETFs in New York hedged with Nasdaq futures in Chicago. A direct line between the two gave his firm a 20-second window on Goldman Sachs.
  • Read the rules: nothing in the CFTC rulebook barred it. When the exchange blocked permits for a dedicated T1/fiber line, he bribed the union crew with cases of Jameson.
  • The first trade made a couple hundred thousand dollars risk-free, and he immediately shut it down rather than 'destroy them on day one'. He came back through a pilot program and reached a couple % of AMEX daily volume within six months, over $100 million that year. He showed his father the multi-million IRS check, and his father asked 'is what you're doing legal?', then told him it won't last: no boats, no women, no drugs, and paying taxes is his patriotic duty.
  • Every arb window closes: by 2002 volumes fell, partners cashed out and the firm over-expanded without management skill. After his father died in 2001 and 9/11, he quit.

Reservations were the next slow paper ticket, and dynamic pricing is the prize

Tock started as a know-your-customer fix for the host stand and grew into a belief that time-slotted businesses should be priced like markets.

  • A phone ringing at the host stand while the guest waits is 'terrible hospitality, but that's every restaurant'. Alinea had to know who was walking in without asking.
  • His 2005 OpenTable analogy: a sweater store where you can't have the blue cardigan, so you guess among 300 others. In his view, OpenTable's arb was owning the customer relationship.
  • A Tuesday 9pm table in Chicago and a Saturday 7pm table aren't worth the same, so they shouldn't cost the same. A full restaurant that turns guests away is 'paying people to sell to your rivals'.
  • Elizabeth Warren's call to ban dynamic pricing gets it backwards. To stop gouging after a shock, you price as fast as possible, because everything is already dynamically priced, just slowly.
  • Uber had the right idea under the wrong word: Travis Kalanick's argument about drawing more drivers out was right, but it should have been called dynamic, not surge.
  • Restaurant owners don't want to do pricing. They need a big dumb button that says 'make more money', and AI may finally make that button possible.

Framework ALINEA, CHICAGO — OPENED 2005consumer

Alinea: built in public, staged like theater, served like close-up magic

With no restaurant experience, he won by borrowing from adjacent crafts like theater, concerts and magic, not from other restaurants.

  • The chef was Grant Achatz. Chicago was due a successor to Charlie Trotter, and 'every bite of food the guy put in my mouth was the best thing I'd ever tasted'.
  • Open-sourced the business plan as the 'Alinea Project' on eGullet: names they rejected, menu development, 4,000 words on the purpose of bread. Hundreds of thousands of views pulled in the press, and the New York Times' head critic came on opening night.
  • The stated goal before opening was the best restaurant in America, so good that Michelin, not yet in the US, would look bad for staying away. Chef Sean Brock was the first customer through the door.
  • His 'universal restaurant calculator' fit the whole model into one tab of about 80 cells. The low barrier to entry is why restaurants fail.
  • From David Byrne's Stop Making Sense: start with an empty stage. No tablecloths (they hide bad tables) and no 18 forks. An early object on the table pays off as a callback in hour two.
  • From magic books: effect first, then method, then presentation. Servers stand beside the diner, never across from them, and he trained staff with Derren Brown videos. Challenge diners in only one direction at a time, e.g. a dish that looks like glass goes on a plate.
  • He steered a table of six off a $1,000 mismatched red by reading a brand-new pair of Louboutins and a $55,000 watch. His question was 'what would you drink on Tuesday with a pizza?', and he sold two or three better bottles for the same spend. In software he deliberately builds 'dead ends', like an omakase.

Principle MAKE THE 50 SAUCESmindsetcareer

Greatness is volume plus editing, not inspiration

Every great practitioner he's met grinds out mostly bad work and keeps cutting out what doesn't work.

  • When Next opened with its Paris 1906 menu (a new cuisine every four months), Grant Achatz fixed an off sauce by making 20 versions in blue-taped pans. Nobody knew what it was supposed to taste like. That became his Culinary Institute of America commencement address.
  • Wilco's Jeff Tweedy writes two or three songs a week to have 12 good ones a year. Kokonas walked in on him writing one that 'was honestly not good'.
  • Chris Rock to a young comic: write jokes every morning, because 'the money is made in the day' and only collected at night.
  • Art & Fear pottery class (Puri): the half graded on volume also made the better pots. His advice to young founders who cold-email him: skip the logo and name, go make the 50 sauces.
  • Market Wizards' 'one lot wonder': a successful trader figures it out, editing out the stupid stuff after every day's postmortem instead of blaming the puncher.
  • From his father: show up early, prepared or honestly unprepared, and reply fast. 'Early is on time, on time is late.' Most people don't follow through.
  • Admit what's broken: he once watched a company keep spending $10 million a year developing an acquisition with 14 competitors that would never make money.

Counterintuitive take ALINEA GROUP SOLD 2023 · TOCK SOLD TO SQUARESPACEfinanceconsumer

Restaurants are a skill issue, and so is selling your company

Running an art-craft business openly for profit is low status, and he says that's exactly why most of the industry loses money.

  • Alinea did about $5M in sales at 7-8% margins in year one. At its peak it did $20M+ a year at 18-20%, which took building software and getting rid of the phone lines. The CFO's menu-costing charts were shared freely with the industry, and nobody adopted them.
  • Investors got quarterly distributions from 2005 until the 2023 sale of the Alinea Group, then 8x their money at exit, about 27-28x overall.
  • During COVID a famous TV chef on CNN said restaurants make 2-3% and asked for a $2 billion bailout. Kokonas emailed him to stop: 'a very successful tv chef not a very successful restaurant owner'.
  • The trader in him sold Tock at the peak of 200% COVID growth, knowing a 50% year would get it labeled legacy ('you're Comcast').
  • His team answered all 896 diligence questions in three days using their own dashboards, against bankers' four-to-six-week estimates. He skipped the investment bank (3-4%) and offered the board to do it for 2%. Lawyers billed about $3,200/hour per call.
  • Squarespace's S-1 needed the deal locked by March 31. When opposing counsel kept running out the clock ('I will take that to my clients'), Kokonas called him 'Spock' and told him to shut up on a 30-person Zoom call.
  • Align incentives: an Alinea manager held back Wednesday seats against a 140-person waitlist to avoid burnout. Kokonas opened 110 seats a night for two weeks and sold them all, and blames himself for never explaining why.

Personal story IN DEVELOPMENT — LEASE STILL BEING SECUREDconsumer

Strange House: an immersive magic venue with Disney's throughput

A restaurant built like a haunted-house ride never worked on cost, but the format works for illusion, and he thinks in-person wonder gains value as AI spreads.

  • Origin: he cold-emailed magician Nate Staniforth about putting the lottery-trick numbers in the wrong order. Staniforth called within five minutes, since only one other person had noticed. Staniforth later consulted on David Blaine's Vegas show.
  • The economics: a 50-seat restaurant is capped at 1-1.5 turns. A haunted-house ride moves 30 people a room every 8-10 minutes. Pop-ups rarely make money because production costs run too high.
  • Staniforth spent eight months on a proposal, sent by FedEx with QR-coded videos of scale-model rooms. Kokonas's floor is that it can't gross under $20 million at about a $120 ticket.
  • Space: 90,000 sq ft underneath Wall Street, six months negotiating the LOI. REITs balk: no magician, no stage, no comparables.
  • Team: the builders of Taylor Swift's stage would build room three; game designer Elan Lee (Exploding Kittens, met at Ord Camp) is on board. Nobody can play-test it without spending a couple million dollars on one room.
  • Comparable: Sleep No More, which made hundreds of millions in revenue. His pitch is Disney's throughput and a Cirque du Soleil-style rollout, 'you could build five of these things'.

What to do with this

Best Actionable Takeaways

🧭
Mindset: Calibrate risk to a mild nausea: if work feels comfortable, look for the next harder game.
⏱️
Strategy: Hunt for the slowest step in any market (the paper ticket, the phone call); that lag is where the arbitrage is.
📜
Strategy: Read the actual rules yourself; incumbents run on case law and habit, not on the text.
🎭
Craft: Borrow your playbook from an adjacent craft (theater, magic, concerts) instead of copying your own industry.
🧪
Craft: Ship volume before polish: make the 20 sauces, then edit out what fails after every attempt.
🤝
Leadership: Explain why the goal matters before you push for it; misaligned incentives quietly cap output.

Names and terms at a glance

Glossary & Entities

AT&A — Kokonas family shorthand for 'all talk, no action', issued like a citation.
Runner / phone clerk / pit clerk — The open-outcry floor's entry ladder: carrying paper orders, writing tickets, then recording trades in the pit.
ETF-futures arbitrage — Buying an index ETF (e.g. QQQ) while selling the matching futures to lock in a small, near-riskless price gap.
ADR arbitrage — Trading the gap between a foreign stock and its US-listed American Depositary Receipt.
Callback — A theater and comedy device: an early setup that pays off later, which he applied to the courses of a tasting menu.
Effect / method / presentation — Magicians' order of design: the result the audience sees, the mechanics behind it, then the craft of performing it.
Smallest highest hoops — His term for niches with a big prize and few competitors because they're hard to reach.
