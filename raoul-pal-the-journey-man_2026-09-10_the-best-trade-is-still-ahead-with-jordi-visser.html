The Bond-Yield "Collapse" Is, In Visser's Words, "The Biggest Marketing Scam I've Ever Seen"

Jordi Visser argues the 30-year-yield panic is a social-media-driven chart illusion that ignores total return, record profit margins, and hiring data that actually proves AI productivity.

  • The 20+ year Treasury ETF is down only 3% year-to-date on total return — the "worst bond collapse in 20 years" framing comes from cherry-picked yield charts, not total-return reality; he notes anyone who sold Treasuries at last May's 30-year yield highs would be down ~70% versus holding.
  • He names Ray Dalio and Stanley Druckenmiller ("the op-ed from Druck") as sources of the bearish bond narrative he's arguing against, and credits Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent with actively managing the supply problem — negotiating swap lines with Japan, China, South Korea and the UAE to keep foreign buyers absorbing issuance while pushing for a weaker dollar and steeper yield curve.
  • Global bank stocks are up 30% year-over-year — his signal that markets aren't pricing real credit stress; he treats the stock market's rate of change as a discounting mechanism that would already be flashing red if a real problem existed.
  • Uses Amazon as his central productivity proof: an 8-year transformation into an AI/robotics-driven company with rising margins and earnings while barely hiring — his model for what the broader economy looks like once it "catches up."
  • Historical framework: pre-1997, 10-year Treasury yields traded ~200bps above nominal GDP; at today's ~6% nominal GDP that implies ~8.5% yields, yet the 10-year sits around 4.75% — he reads this gap as evidence yields are already anchored low, not poised to spiral.
  • His structural point: record profits + near-zero hiring (excluding healthcare, unaffected by AI for now, with essentially no net hiring for 20 months) is itself the evidence of AI productivity gains that GDP accounting isn't built to capture.

Both Hosts Are Now Spending Thousands a Month Managing Dozens of AI Agents

Raoul and Jordi describe agent demand as effectively infinite — the more you deploy, the more your time and money get absorbed, not freed.

  • Raoul: monthly AI subscription spend jumped from a couple thousand dollars to $11,000 on top of his Mac subscription; he says his time is now "completely saturated" — taken from sports and other leisure, not freed up as promised.
  • Jordi is running 75 agents across Grok/Grokbot, Hermes, Claude and (previously) OpenClaw, and says managing them ate roughly two hours of his mornings before he built a dedicated "chief of staff"/COO agent just to keep the others coordinated and fix breakages (dropped connections, a Google Drive integration going down, etc.).
  • Concrete use cases described: a fully automated personalized morning "newspaper" curated from chosen sites and cross-referenced against trending X posts; shipping a new feature on Real Vision's GMI portal 40 minutes after an idea came in by email, with Jordi doing nothing but dictating notes via voice.
  • When Jordi's video-editing software abruptly shut down mid-week, he ran an agent "staff meeting" (his words: "literally like being a managing director at Morgan Stanley") that picked Google Slides as the fix and coached him through learning it well enough to rebuild a 100-slide video workflow in days.
  • Raoul separately floats that AI agents' real bottleneck input isn't just energy/compute but data — he expects an agent-driven data marketplace to become one of the largest markets that will ever exist, with no visible prices, functioning as a hidden market for information rights.
  • Both say they know essentially zero non-technology people their own age (near 60) using AI as actively as they are — even people at hedge funds are mostly delegating AI use to junior analysts rather than using it themselves.

Tokenization Is Visser's New Main Focus: "It's Real. It's Going to Happen. And It Is a Race."

Visser argues tokenization will disrupt asset management the way AI is disrupting labor, replacing stock-picking alpha with volume-driven "toll collector" economics.

  • Core disruption argument: asset managers are judged on human-narratable track records, not pure returns — he argues if Bitcoin had a human manager telling its story, it would already be worth trillions more given it's been the best-performing asset of the last 15 years; younger investors are comfortable with no human narrator at all.
  • Structural example: aggregator hedge funds like Millennium and D.E. Shaw run a "mother ship" (capital allocation, compliance) plus many pods of uncorrelated return streams — he expects tokenization to let those pods (and eventually whole strategies) become individually investable, collapsing management fees industry-wide.
  • Nvidia (NVDA) as his flagship example that fundamentals-driven alpha is dying: the stock was effectively flat from October to his most recent taping despite blowing away earnings and guiding 70% revenue growth for next year versus a 44% street estimate — he says in an earlier era that kind of beat would have moved the stock hard.
  • He's built a public equal-weight basket across ~10 crypto sectors that also includes public stocks like Circle (CRCL), Robinhood (HOOD), and PayPal (PYPL) — its correlation to Bitcoin is "close to one," reinforcing his view that the crypto/tokenization trade and digital-asset exposure are converging into one basket.
  • Names Robinhood specifically as the best-positioned "middle ground" platform for merging traditional and crypto retail communities, citing its event community and rapid on-chain trading-volume growth.
  • On the dollar: he sees two opposing forces — tokenization initially strengthens the dollar by making the most attractive US assets (equities, SpaceX) instantly investable to global capital, but weakens US geopolitical control over money flow as stablecoins let value bypass SWIFT and sanctions over time.
  • Cites Axie Infinity as the case study that first pulled him into crypto — evidence a virtual economy can function as a real economic model, which he says is directly relevant to how tokenized economies will behave.

The "Idiot-Proof" Portfolio: A Third Nasdaq, a Third S&P, a Third Bitcoin — and Never Touch It

Both hosts argue most active portfolio management is wasted effort chasing control, when a static Bitcoin/equities split since the Bitcoin whitepaper has already beaten most strategies.

  • Jordi says he has never sold a Bitcoin and has never taken money out of digital assets back into a bank account, ever — appreciation only, by design, because he doesn't need to spend the money (states he owns one home in Maine and drives a 2021 Tesla).
  • Their shared framework: split a portfolio roughly a third each into Nasdaq, S&P 500, and Bitcoin since the Bitcoin whitepaper and never rebalance — Jordi's point is that even without rebalancing (which skews the mix toward ~97-98% Bitcoin over time), the compounding has outperformed most active strategies.
  • His crypto allocation beyond Bitcoin: a Zcash position for the privacy narrative, plus a market-cap-weighted basket of 3-5 dominant smart-contract chains — currently led by Ethereum and Solana, with Hyperliquid treated separately as "a business built on blockchain" rather than pure infrastructure.
  • He expects Ethereum to outperform Bitcoin over the next year, crediting the tokenization/stablecoin boom and Ethereum's "trust factor" — its longevity and merge history make it the chain institutions and banks are most likely to build financial rails on.
  • Psychological framing for why people over-trade instead of doing this: it's about a sense of control/agency, not expected returns — he compares it to people who call themselves poor while flying to the Hamptons, chasing "the next big thing" instead of just compounding a diversified position they already have.

Compute and Energy Scarcity Are a Good Thing — and Humanoids Go to the Mines First

Visser argues the compute shortage is buying society time to adjust to AI, and that humanoid robots' first real economic role will be dangerous industrial and mining jobs, not household delivery.

  • His central claim: without the current compute shortage relative to AI capability, "it would be a much more dystopian world" — friction from politics, human behavior, and physics is slowing AGI's real-world rollout enough for people to adjust, which he now views as a positive rather than a bottleneck to fear.
  • Notes the shift toward world models over pure LLMs — cites Google's video-trained models and a recently released video-generation model he calls "Atlas" (from World Labs) as evidence "you cannot tell what's film, what's not film" anymore, and mentions his own Tesla rental in Napa that drove itself the entire trip.
  • Full self-driving is technologically ready but not socially accepted yet, which he expects to delay humanoid robot adoption even longer — Optimus-style robots will be deployed first in restricted environments (factory lines, service centers) because they lack the visual-spatial awareness to navigate the open human world.
  • His specific economic call: humanoids' biggest impact over the next 5 years will be in mining and other dangerous manufacturing jobs (he notes China is already doing this) — pushing down the input cost of commodities like oil, copper and gold, though he says this growth will not go "parabolic."
  • In a recent debate (his words, "in the last 48 hours") with an unnamed famous macro investor over gold versus Bitcoin, he argued AI will eventually be able to "hack" the supply side of any resource including gold (he cites a CERN example) — reinforcing his preference for Bitcoin's digital scarcity over physical scarcity that AI/robotics could eventually erode.
  • On the US data-center political backlash: he's become less worried, crediting "behind-the-meter" power innovation (repurposed airplane turbines as gas turbines, underused natural gas pipeline capacity) with solving the bottleneck faster than politics could block it — and argues the current slower build-out pace is protecting the bond market from a bigger overbuild-driven shock.

Jordi's New Book Was Written by Training AI on His Own Back Catalog

The Everything Code is billed as the first major-publisher book built this way, and Visser says the process is fully disclosed to readers.

  • Publisher (Pan Macmillan / Harriman House) trained an AI on Jordi's existing content and his own supplied material; the AI built the narrative structure, which he then worked with an editor to craft further — he says this collapsed the time to write a full book from what would normally take far longer.
  • When asked directly whether he's worried about a Wall Street Journal-style backlash over undisclosed AI authorship, he says the entire premise of the book is that its AI-assisted process is disclosed upfront, not hidden.
  • Raoul notes he's absorbed more of the ideas from hearing Jordi discuss the book across multiple podcast appearances than from any single explanation — framed as his own reason for planning to buy the book anyway.
