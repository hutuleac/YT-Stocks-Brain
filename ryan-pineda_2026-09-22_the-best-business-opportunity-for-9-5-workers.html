The one-paragraph takeaway

Video Snapshot

  1. Ryan Pineda hosts Parker Smith of Home Service Experts, who helps 9-5 workers launch home-service businesses without knowing the trade.
  2. Parker's thesis: the trade is the least important part. Licensed installers are easy to find; sales, marketing and operations are what's missing.
  3. Proof case is his 90-day-old Raleigh water-filtration business: a ~$1,000 system competitors sell for $12,000, sold at ~$3,200 by phone, $80,000 revenue in month two.
  4. Pricing rule: be the cheap new entrant or the premium reputation brand. The middle price is where businesses go to die.
  5. Ryan and co-host Brian argue Nike and McDonald's are riding 50 years of brand while better, cheaper rivals out-innovate them.
  6. Ryan's operating system: set the outcome, list your constraints (he works 9-5), and solve for X instead of copying people whose path cost their families.
  7. Shared closing view: opportunities come in windows. Run them up while open, but time-limited windows (kids, a sports career) come first.
Personal story 90 DAYS IN — RALEIGH, NORTH CAROLINAconsumercareer

Water filtration: a $900 product, four hours to install, sold for $12,000

Parker picked water filtration for the margin, not the water: a ~$950-1,000 whole-home system that competitors sell for $8,000-12,000.

  • First 90 days: ~50 jobs, average ticket $3,200, materials $1,100, labor $500-550, so roughly a 50% gross margin.
  • Labor is easy: he offers licensed plumbers clean four-hour installs at $500 ($125 an hour) three days a week, and "people lining up to work with us".
  • Drafting the big players: they spend on awareness and six-hour in-home demos at $12,000, and his ad reads "your neighbor's water softener $12,000, your water softener $3,000".
  • Phone sales only, no in-person sales team: $200 a day of ads, ~5 leads, 20% close rate, about one job a day at a $200 CAC on ~$1,200-1,300 gross profit.
  • Month two hit $80,000 with ops manager Jack answering the phone and Parker working five hours a week; 28% net operating profit; $60,000+ booked by September 6.
  • Licensing varies by state: installers are licensed; the owner can act as a referral partner (a $3,000 job = $2,000 product + $1,000 install contracted direct). California, Georgia and South Carolina are stricter. Parker got a North Carolina GC license in 7 days of study.
  • Same gap elsewhere: a main panel upgrade quoted at $8,000-12,000 is ~$600 of parts and two hours of labor, per electricians he knows in Chicago. Ryan, in hard-water Las Vegas, already owns a $12,000+ reverse-osmosis setup and paid about $5,000 for a saltwater pool system.

Framework PRICE LOW, BUILD REVIEWS, THEN RAISEconsumercareer

Why the middle price dies, and how to size a small market

A new business should be the clear cheap option or the premium reputation brand. Pricing in the middle makes you interchangeable with the $2,000 option and leaves you without the $8,000 brand's reputation.

  • Parker's plan: 53 five-star reviews in 90 days; at 200 reviews and the #1 search result for "water filtration Raleigh", the price goes up to ~$5,000.
  • Ryan applies the same logic to M19: members expected $30,000-50,000; it has been $10,000 for a year and will step up to $15,000 then $20,000 while staying the best value. Tesla is his analogy: cheaper than everyone and better.
  • TAM math for Raleigh-Durham: ~2.5M people, ~1.2M eligible, 5% share over five years = 60,000 customers x $3,000 = ~$18M revenue at a 30% margin.
  • Small city = smaller TAM but lower CAC and a shot at becoming the "local celebrity": his first cleaning company in Madison, Wisconsin (200,000 people) was ranked #2 after nine months. Big city (Chicago) means niching by neighbourhood and buyer.
  • Three growth levers: more subs in the same market, more service types for the same customers, or another service area.
  • Tommy Mello's self-described biggest mistake: expanding A1 out of Phoenix too early, where it now does tens of millions selling one product. Ryan tells wholesalers almost no one is ever capped out in their market.
  • Why Parker stays in the $3,000-8,000 sweet spot: roofing (Ryan saw $35,000 roofs after hail in Louisville, ~$10,000 more with basements) and solar are insurance-dependent with ~120-day cash cycles.

The business side of blue-collar is the real gap

Accounting, marketing, sales and hiring skills from a 9-5 transfer directly. What stalls people is picking a market and a service, so Home Service Experts sells the infrastructure and takes a share of revenue.

  • The three usual paths each fail: start from zero (too daunting); buy an SMB (good deals are gone, LoopNet and BizBuySell show the same listings, six months of diligence ends in key-man risk); or franchise (territory, branding and pricing restrictions).
  • Brian is the live example: talking to an AC company asking $5M with seller financing. Parker calls the "buy from a retiring boomer" advice (Codie Sanchez) not flawed but unrealistic.
  • Parker's path: ~$10,000 to start plus ~$5,000 of ad spend, "$20,000 tops", then a 30-day ramp, market analysis and a defined wedge. Going solo it can be ~$1,000 (a 50% deposit buys the equipment); $30,000-50,000 is plenty to target $250k net in 12 months.
  • He says clients with full-time jobs do $100,000 a month at 35% margins (~$350,000+ EBITDA a year).
  • Each niche gets dedicated training; the top-performing ad from his own businesses goes to every client's Meta account that week.
  • Why now: an aging population of service providers is phasing out with far fewer young replacements. A 500-review moat locks in the area before the landscape shifts.
  • AI risk is low: Parker says we are "decades away" from a robot electrician or crawl-space trenching. Cleaning goes first, and affluent buyers will then pay extra for humans.

Personal story CLEANING SOLD AT 23 — LIGHTS STILL RUNNINGcareer

Cleaning and holiday lights: Parker's sandbox businesses

Parker uses his own businesses as a sandbox, starting with cleaning, which he calls the entry-level home-service business.

  • Cleaning: first client acquired for $35 on Meta before he had a cleaner; bought a mop and vacuum at Walmart and cleaned a house for $200. Later serviced three JCPenney and three Kohl's locations plus AT&T stores. Sold for $300,000 at 23, two years in.
  • Cleaning is now crowded because entry is so cheap ("buy a mop and work"). Ryan notes Pace Morby's partner Cody also scaled a cleaning business.
  • Exterior services stack on a seasonal arc: windows in spring, pressure washing and gutters in summer, gutter guards in fall, holiday lights in winter.
  • Holiday lights in Chicago: first season (winter 2024) 100 installs, $105,000 in seven weeks off 200 yard signs at a ~50-cent CAC. Last season 250 homes, ~$1,100 average ticket, $275,000 in eight weeks, 11 homes on one street.
  • Lease model: $8 a foot charged, $2.50 to the sub, $2.50 for product, so $3 a foot in year one and $5.50 after since he keeps the lights. Ryan stopped hanging his own lights once flipping went from $50,000 (2015) to $200,000.
  • Next: interior/exterior painting in Q1 (big tickets, paint is cheap, mid-level skill).
  • Origin: a pro soccer player with spare time who failed at drop shipping, a white-label supplement brand, fitness content and a social media agency. When he started cleaning, the other browser tab was an Amazon delivery driver application. He started on a loan from his wife and still drives her old Mazda.
Counterintuitive take CONTESTED — BRIAN DEFENDS MCDONALD'Sconsumer

Nike and McDonald's are riding 50 years of brand

Ryan argues Nike and McDonald's coast on brand equity while rivals with better, cheaper products out-innovate them, and that they will be passed if the trend holds.

  • Ryan's figures: Nike just fell out of the S&P 100 with the stock down ~80% from a peak "a year ago or something".
  • Product critique: $60 cotton t-shirts that end up at Marshalls; Lululemon, Alo and Vuori "destroy Nike's quality". No new Air Jordans worth collecting since the classics (1s, 4s, 5s, 11s), so "they sell nostalgia".
  • Nike Golf was shut down even with the best golfer ever, because TaylorMade, Ping, PXG and Titleist built better gear.
  • The direct-to-consumer push cut out Foot Locker and the mall retailers that were the "lifeblood" of the business, and it backfired.
  • New Balance is the contrast: a new CEO embraced being the "dad brand" and it took off, while Nike is caught between discount and $300 pricing.
  • McDonald's vs. Chick-fil-A and In-N-Out (cheaper and better, per Ryan). Brian disagrees. Ford and Chevy had a 100-year head start too, and Tesla is now worth more than the top 10 carmakers, per Ryan.

Framework OUTCOME + CONSTRAINTS = Xmindsetcareer

Solve for X: build to your constraints instead of copying someone's path

Ryan doesn't ask who already did it. He fixes the outcome and his constraints (9-5 hours, family, skills) and treats the path as the unknown to solve for.

  • Copying has hidden costs: "I want to be like Elon Musk" means the divorces too. He says he knows entrepreneurs personally who lost their faith, marriages, kids or health.
  • Couch flipping came from constraints: newly married and broke, six months a year free from pro baseball at $1,200 a month, practice every afternoon. ~$200 per couch for an hour of physical work, $6,000-8,000 a month.
  • In 2020 he turned down a house-flipping TV pilot (~60 hours a week) because he thought one TikTok could beat a TV audience. It wasn't the norm then; now it is.
  • M19 came the same way: he met the richest people golfing, but "golf with me" (~$30,000 a month) and trips ($50,000-100,000 each, the last at Payne's Valley) were capped by his time and his marriage.
  • The fix was a membership where he's "the low man in the room": almost 500 members a year in, average member revenue $32M (median $5M). They play each other's clubs weekly without him.
  • He says M19 will be his fastest business to eight figures (by the end of this year) and expects copycats in about a year, so he's building the moat now.
  • His wider ask: "why do you have to do it that way?", put to his content, sales and marketing teams too. He also turned down the usual golf options (build a course, be a YouTube golfer).

Principle TIME-LIMITED WINDOWS FIRSTparentingmindsetfamily

Run up the windows, but the kids' window closes first

Opportunities come in windows, plural: run each one up while it's open, and put the windows that can't come back (kids, a sports career) ahead of business, which will still be there at 70.

  • Brian on Ryan: when something works, he scales the bet 1,000x during the window. Brian put "every single dollar" into real estate when rates went to 0% in 2020, until rates rose.
  • Every hot streak ends (the trend, the economy, competition, a recession), and "the number one regret's always I wish I would have just did more". Parker: you will miss some, like passing on $200 Bitcoin.
  • To young athletes: play as long as you can. Ryan played until 28 with no regrets. Parker's dad told him at 21: drop out, school will always be there. He signed, made his pro debut and won man of the match.
  • Kids give you 18 years in the house, so build around that. Ryan's answer is a school inside his office.
  • Fatherhood advice: the gift is time and energy. Work 9-5 Monday to Friday, home for dinner and bedtime, weekends with the kids. Don't let youth sports replace church or family dinner, because "you don't have to be a stud at 10".
  • Identity trap: athletes cling to failing careers, and real-estate people fear being called failures if they switch. Ryan went from baseball player to "couch flipper" to golf guy; Parker from pro soccer player to house cleaner ("now I tell people I'm a plumber").

What to do with this

Best Actionable Takeaways

🔍
Business: Pick a home-service niche by the product-cost-to-quoted-price gap, not by what you know how to do.
💲
Pricing: Launch clearly cheap, stack reviews fast, and raise prices once you're the top search result. Never price in the middle.
📏
Strategy: Run the TAM math on your home metro before expanding; add service types or subs before adding cities.
🧮
Mindset: Write down your outcome and your constraints, then design the path, instead of copying someone whose path you wouldn't accept.
⏳
Family: List which of your windows close for good (kids at home, a sports career) and schedule around those first.

Names and terms at a glance

Glossary & Entities

CAC — Customer acquisition cost: ad spend divided by customers won.
TAM — Total addressable market: everyone who could plausibly buy the service in your area.
Key-man risk — When a business depends so much on one person (often the selling owner) that it can't survive their exit.
Referral partner — Selling the product and handing the install to a licensed contractor who contracts directly with the homeowner.
GC license — General contractor license; requirements vary sharply by state.
UGC — User-generated content: ads made by customers or creators rather than the brand.
