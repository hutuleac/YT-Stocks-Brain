Contested REPORTED — $96.2B quarter, guidance raisedsemisai-infra

Nvidia's blowout quarter meets its first real inference competitor

Nvidia posted $96.2B in single-quarter revenue at 80-85% gross margins and guided even higher — but the panel's real debate is how much of that demand Nvidia is financing itself, and whether OpenAI's new chip just cracked its inference monopoly.

  • Nvidia revenue: $96.2B in the quarter, +106% YoY, with guidance of $108B (+10%) for the current quarter — over $1B/day; Jensen Huang is guiding 70% growth for 2028 against a Wall Street consensus of 44%.
  • Nvidia's biggest structural vulnerability, per Dave Blundin: TSMC is its sole manufacturer (roughly a third of TSMC's capacity goes to Nvidia, another third to Apple), and Nvidia can't risk openly building its own fabs without angering TSMC.
  • Alex Wissner-Gross's "elephant in the room": he wants clearer visibility into how much AI infrastructure demand is directly or indirectly financed or backstopped by Nvidia itself through intermediaries — if a disproportionate share turns out to be Nvidia-financed, he'd call that "too bubbly." His own base case even so: at most a brief "mini winter," not a collapse, because compute is structurally substituting for real estate and labor as civilization's substrate.
  • OpenAI's new inference chip, built with Broadcom, delivers 1.5-1.9x more AI work per watt and up to 3.6x lower latency than Nvidia's GB200/GB300, running at 700W versus the GB300's 1400W — described as "the first 2x of a 100x performance gain."
  • The most startling number: OpenAI claims up to a 54x throughput-per-second-per-user increase hosting the open-source GPT-OSS model on its own chip versus existing Nvidia-based architecture.
  • CUDA's moat is described as already dead for inference (easy to port to AMD/Intel) but still dominant for frontier training research; Nvidia's real new moat is its Mellanox-based interconnect, needed to run 100,000-to-a-million-GPU clusters coherently for training.
  • Speculative scenario floated: OpenAI could become its own hyperscaler, offering "OpenAI compute" on its own chips and even hosting rival Anthropic's models for a fee — a world where "OpenAI and Anthropic win at the same time."

Elon's new SpaceX number: $3.5 trillion in revenue by 2033

Musk has revised his own SpaceX revenue target upward from $1T by 2030 to $3.5T by 2033 — Dave Blundin calls it his single largest personal financial bet.

  • Musk's prior guidance (from an earnings call) was $1T in SpaceX revenue by 2030, potentially 2029; he tweeted a revised $3.5T figure for 2033. For scale, today's highest-revenue company, Amazon, does $828B.
  • Dave's read: Musk has historically been right about the scale of an opportunity but wrong on timeline by a couple of years, usually due to regulatory or engineering delays rather than a flawed thesis — he sees no reason to doubt the $3.5T figure directionally.
  • Alex's path to that number: a SpaceX-Tesla merger (bringing Optimus robot revenue, which he thinks could itself be a multi-trillion-dollar/year business), an orbital data-center/Dyson-swarm compute business (potentially trillions more), and a growing hyperscaler cloud business — with Starlink and cars reduced to "rounding errors" by comparison.
  • Dave frames SpaceX as a diversified bet across the whole AI stack — chips, data centers, models, comms, infrastructure, and launch — versus a single-layer bet on OpenAI or Anthropic's models alone; he calls the biggest risk simply Musk's personal safety and longevity to execute the vision.

Contested DIVERGING — video vs. language model betsgeopoliticssoftware

China is betting on "world models," America is betting on language models

Video generation now makes up roughly 70% of all AI token consumption in China — Alex's read: American labs are revenue-maxing on enterprise LLM use cases, Chinese labs aren't, so they default to video.

  • Alibaba's Wan 3.0 can generate 30-second single-pass videos from documents, spreadsheets, slides, and web pages, priced at $0.05-0.20 per second of generated video (roughly $20K-60K for a 90-minute film) — announced a day after Alibaba's largest-ever private follow-on offering on the Hong Kong exchange, at $10.2B.
  • Runway's co-founder reports video generation is now ~70% of Chinese AI token consumption, growing faster than Claude Code grew in the US — framed as "America is LLM-pilled, China is world-model-pilled."
  • Alex's explanation: when OpenAI tried video generation, Anthropic "ran away with their lunch" on revenue-per-token via code-gen; Chinese labs, by contrast, give model weights away for free and aren't optimizing for revenue per token, so spending compute on video is relatively costless for them.
  • Dave's framing of the actual race: it isn't world models vs. language models toward recursive self-improvement (RSI) — it's simply about smarter engineers with more cash and training chips. He credits DeepMind's Demis Hassabis with first conceiving an RSI-focused strategy but says Google's bureaucracy blocked him from acting on it, while Anthropic's Dario Amodei executed on a pure "model that improves itself through better code and training" focus first.
  • Omnimodal capability gap flagged by Alex: Fable 5's visual reasoning is comparatively weak versus its language capability; he expects Anthropic to close that gap within 6-12 months, possibly via acquisition.
  • Cultural-influence angle (citing absent guest Immad Mostaque's prior point that AI is a highly persuasive, culture-spreading force): China is on track to become the dominant global producer of video content in any language, while domestically restricting content (e.g., blood shown green in games/video) even as it exports unrestricted content abroad.

Contested UNRESOLVED — healthy growth or self-referential bubbleai-infrafinance

Is the AI economy a healthy new economy, or a closed financing loop?

The panel debates whether the AI buildout is becoming a self-contained economy that barely touches the legacy one — and whether that's a feature or a warning sign.

  • Alex distinguishes two forms of "circular" economy: a relatively healthy income-statement version (Company A sells to B, B sells back to A) versus a less healthy balance-sheet version (private credit financing the very purchases that generate reported revenue) — he says he lacks visibility into which one is dominant right now.
  • Dave's structural framework: the whole AI economy reduces to training (needs coherent, co-located Nvidia GPU clusters) versus inference (can run anywhere, including in space) — and everything investable, from robotics to drug discovery, depends on which of those two a given business actually leans on.
  • Salim's "Alpha Centauri" metaphor: a superintelligent civilization wouldn't bother trading with a distant, slower economy — the AI economy is becoming similarly self-contained, generating value (e.g., new drug discoveries) that increasingly doesn't need to touch the legacy economy to be worth trillions.
  • Cited data point: revenue from Ozempic and Mounjaro alone is already larger than all AI inference revenue combined — used as evidence that AI-driven breakthroughs (more drug discoveries) could soon dwarf the legacy economy's flow back into AI.
Pull quote
“Every economy is circular in nature — the new economy only cares about itself and cares very little about the legacy economy.”— Salim Ismail
Worth noting

Structural critique FLAGGED — civil-liberties riskpolicy

Flock Safety and the disappearing cost of mass surveillance

Flock's license-plate camera network now covers 6,000+ US communities and scans 20 billion vehicles a month — Salim's read: the constitution wasn't written for a world where surveillance is free.

  • Flock Safety (founded 2017) operates AI-powered license-plate cameras for police, schools, and businesses across 6,000+ communities in 49 states, generating 20B+ vehicle scans per month; its platform has expanded into video cameras, gunshot/audio detection, mobile surveillance trailers, and drones.
  • Documented misuse cited by the panel: police officers using Flock access to track ex-partners, and at least one case of a fatal police stop triggered by a misread license plate.
  • Salim's structural point: civil liberties were historically protected by the sheer cost of following someone; AI pattern-matching has driven that cost to near zero, and "the constitution does not update when transaction costs go to zero."
  • Salim's own border-detention story: repeatedly held for hours at US immigration because his name matches a wanted Afghan warlord's, illustrating false-positive name-matching risk at scale — he frames due process ("innocent until proven guilty") as effectively eroded by this kind of automated matching.
  • Proposed fix (Alex, citing David Brin): give ordinary citizens the same license-plate-tracking access as police, treating public-space surveillance as a shared public resource — citing Russia's dashcam culture, which emerged from citizens protecting themselves against corrupt police, as a working precedent for pointing the camera back.

Confirmed-good, contested framing DATA-BACKED — actual survey contradicts doom narrativecareerpolicy

Three competing jobs stories — and the actual survey data

The Washington Post says the job apocalypse won't happen, Goldman Sachs warns professional services face existential risk, and a real 130,000-company survey backs the optimistic case: only 4% expect AI to cut staff, versus 31% expecting to add.

  • The Washington Post's argument: historically, technology transitions (loom, automobile, internet) created more jobs than they destroyed by displacing specific tasks rather than whole occupations; AI-fluent workers become more productive, not unemployed.
  • Goldman Sachs's counter-warning: professional-services firms (consulting, law, accounting) face existential risk because AI capability is improving faster than senior partners can adapt — creating a skills gap between AI-fluent juniors and AI-resistant partners, not between educated and uneducated workers.
  • Principal Financial Group's survey of 130,000 small/mid-size business customers: only 4% expect AI to reduce staffing or wages, versus 31% (a majority of the workforce) expecting increased staffing and wages; only 10% report not using AI at all (down from 19% a year earlier); of companies that did reduce staff, only 1.4% attributed the cuts to AI or automation.
  • Recent 3-month trend from the same survey: 52% of companies increased staff, 30% held steady, 12% reduced — and historically, 100% of net job growth over the last 50-60 years has come from small and mid-size companies, not large ones.
  • Salim explicitly dismisses several high-profile doom commentators (see Hot Takes) as poor forecasters despite strong track records explaining the past.
  • Dave's framing for how human work changes: workers move "above the loop, not in the loop" — doing curatorial judgment and reconciliation rather than rote data entry, illustrated by his own book-writing process going from 3 years of manual work to 6 months of "unadulterated joy" with AI assistance.
  • Alex's practical advice: treat any AI model as the most patient teacher available and spend 30 minutes a day in conversation with it to build fluency — framing high agency as one of the few human traits actively and increasingly rewarded, in a window he estimates may only last "a handful of years."
Pull quote
“The skills gap is not between the educated and the uneducated. It's between the AI-fluent and the AI-resistant — and the AI-fluent are increasingly the juniors, not the partners.”— Peter Diamandis, summarizing Goldman Sachs
Worth noting
The Principal Financial Group data covers small/mid-size businesses specifically — the panel doesn't address whether large-enterprise or professional-services trends (Goldman's focus) look different.
Confirmed-good APPROVED — new drug and lab result this weekhealthbiotech

A new pancreatic cancer drug and the first real tooth-enamel regrowth

The FDA approved the first RAS-inhibitor drug for metastatic pancreatic cancer, nearly doubling median survival — and a UK team demonstrated a gel that actually regrows tooth enamel for the first time.

  • Daraxonrasib (FDA-approved, oral daily tablet) is the first RAS inhibitor for metastatic pancreatic adenocarcinoma, nearly doubling median survival from 6.7 to 13.2 months and tripling tumor response rates from 11% to 32%; RAS mutations drive roughly 30% of all human cancers, so the approach may generalize beyond pancreatic cancer.
  • FDA approved it 60-90 days ahead of its target decision date — read by Alex as a sign of a faster, more Bayesian-friendly FDA that's requiring fewer clinical trials for exemplary cases.
  • Alex's framing: this drug used conventional drug design with no AI involvement, making it likely one of the last pre-AI-era cancer drugs — he expects AlphaFold-style protein-folding and virtual-cell modeling to push response rates toward near-100% in the next generation.
  • University of Nottingham (published in Nature Communications) demonstrated a fluoride-free, elastin-like protein gel that regrows real tooth enamel — previously considered non-regenerative tissue — restoring hardness, stiffness, and acid/friction resistance; still tested ex vivo on extracted teeth, moving toward human trials. Oral disease costs an estimated $500B/year globally.
  • Dave connects this to Moderna's recently reported melanoma vaccine result (its stock rose on the news) as a platform technology applicable to other cancers — arguing the real prize is repeatable, scalable regenerative medicine (teeth, limbs, organs) rather than one-off results.
Pull quote

Confirmed-good PROGRESSING — new fuel supply, China's solar surgeenergygeopolitics

A uranium-enrichment breakthrough and China's 8x solar surge

A startup demonstrated uranium enrichment at 5x the traditional purity — potentially solving the fuel problem stalling every SMR company's 2030 timeline — while China's monthly solar output has grown eightfold since 2020.

  • Startup Actinide demonstrated enrichment of natural uranium to 15.38% purity HALEU (high-assay low-enriched uranium), roughly 5x the 3-5% purity traditionally achieved — every small modular reactor (SMR) company targets 2030 for coming online, but none currently have a qualified fuel supply, which this could solve.
  • Alex's regulatory-history riff: he traces the fuel bottleneck to how the Atomic Energy Commission was structured after WWII, arguing civilian nuclear physics has been stuck on Manhattan-Project-era enrichment technology (uranium hexafluoride gas + centrifuges) for 80 years while alternatives like electromagnetic calutron-based separation, built with modern power electronics, were never pursued.
  • Dave's VC framing: no venture investor would touch nuclear when the regulatory cost of standing up a reactor ran to roughly $1B, but SMR companies are now attracting VC funding and going public as that calculus shifts.
  • China's monthly solar generation grew roughly 8x (800%) over six years — from about 20 terawatt-hours/month in 2020 to about 160 TWh/month in mid-2026 (Ember data); Chinese nuclear output is up at least 50% over the same period while US nuclear output has stayed flat.
  • Salim's read: China's advantage isn't better solar panel technology, it's the entire industrial system around it (manufacturing, permitting, deployment speed); the US bottleneck is grid interconnection and permitting, not the technology itself — he proposes a "Manhattan-style" push on perovskite and next-generation solar.
  • Dave's blunt aside: most US-installed solar panels are actually manufactured in China, making the US chart look even worse than it appears at face value.
  • Context cited for US data-center energy costs: natural gas runs roughly $6 per million BTU for US data-center power generation, versus China's near-zero marginal cost from solar.

Speculative SPECULATIVE — consultant estimates called lowball by the panelspace

Deloitte says the lunar economy is worth $566B by 2050 — Elon's own numbers imply quadrillions

Deloitte estimates $566B in cumulative lunar economic value by 2050; Musk's own Star Mine vision, if realized, implies roughly $2 quadrillion a year — the panel treats the consulting-firm number as systematically too conservative.

  • Deloitte's estimate: $566B in cumulative lunar economic value by 2050, led by infrastructure, transportation, power, comms, mobility, and construction, with SpaceX cited as the biggest likely winner via Starship.
  • Musk's own Star Mine concept: starting with 100-200 gigawatts/year of orbital AI compute launched from Earth, transitioning to manufacturing AI satellites on the Moon via a mass driver, potentially scaling to 100 terawatts/year of lunar-produced orbital compute — which the panel calculates would imply roughly $2 quadrillion/year in gross revenue at that scale.
  • Salim's critique of consulting-firm forecasts generally: institutional caution (legal review, stale source data — he cites one AI-agent report built on data 2.5 years old) systematically lowballs estimates; his own rule of thumb is to multiply their numbers by 10-20x.
  • Alex argues the real killer app for the Moon isn't tourism, transport, or government projects — it's chip fabrication and data-center manufacturing there, leveraging the Moon's low gravity well for cheap material transport and power generation once local manufacturing scales.
  • Named source: Jared Isaacman, cited by the panel as an unusually effective, accelerationist NASA administrator championing the government-side case for lunar infrastructure.

Speculative DEMONSTRATED — 19M gallons from 10 dronespolicy

Weather modification just went from theory to a measurable drone product

Startup Rainmaker flew 10 drones over Alaska's Kenai Peninsula and generated an estimated 19 million gallons of additional rainfall in 3 hours — the panel's real interest is that they could prove the attribution, not just make it rain.

  • Rainmaker's 10-drone flight over the Kenai Peninsula used glaciogenic cloud seeding to generate an estimated 19 million additional gallons of rain in 3 hours; for scale, the atmosphere holds an estimated 3.4 quadrillion gallons of water total.
  • Salim's key point: the breakthrough isn't that cloud seeding works (known for decades) — it's that Rainmaker could measure and attribute how much additional rain their intervention caused, which is what unlocks a real, accountable business model.
  • Alex envisions a longer-term "global weather control grid": continuously airborne, solar-powered drone swarms dispensing seeding particulates under centralized AI model control, capable of dispersing hurricanes, redirecting precipitation, or enabling a tradeable market in rainfall between regions.
  • Salim flags a real tension this creates: weather doesn't respect political or property boundaries, so a region seeding clouds could be accused of depriving a downstream neighbor of rainfall they'd otherwise have received.

Apple's new M5 Ultra Mac Studio can run large open models locally on 512GB of unified memory — Salim calls it an obvious win for regulated industries, while Dave calls Apple's overall AI strategy "pathetic."

Names and terms at a glance

Glossary & Entities

CUDA — Nvidia's software layer that translates AI researchers' Python/PyTorch code into optimized Nvidia GPU instructions — the moat that made Nvidia dominant for AI training, though the panel says it's no longer a moat at inference time.
HALEU (High-Assay Low-Enriched Uranium) — Uranium enriched above the traditional 3-5% purity range but below weapons-grade, needed to fuel most next-generation small modular reactors.
SMR (Small Modular Reactor) — A smaller, factory-buildable nuclear reactor design intended to be faster and cheaper to deploy than traditional large reactors.
Glaciogenic cloud seeding — A weather-modification technique that introduces ice-nucleating particles into clouds to trigger or enhance precipitation.
World model — An AI model trained to predict the next state of physical reality (used for video generation, robotics, and simulation), contrasted here with language models that predict the next token of text.
