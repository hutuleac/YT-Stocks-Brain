Bending Spoons: buy cheap, fix quickly, run for cash, repeat

A little-known acquirer of software companies that uses AI to push margins toward 50% and recycle the cash into more deals.

  • Market cap is ~$42B with ~$5B of debt; 2026 revenue guidance is $2.8B at roughly 45% adjusted operating margin.
  • It has made 50+ acquisitions and owns products with about 500 million monthly active users, which it can monetize over time.
  • Typical purchase price is ~2.5x revenue; the two latest deals were Airtable at 2.7x and another at 2.3x.
  • AOL was reportedly transformed to about 90% of target within roughly 9 months; the team says the transformation time doesn't scale with a company's revenue.
  • The founders' earlier startup failed for lack of distribution, so they now buy companies that already have large user bases.
  • LG's framing: unlike infrastructure plays, there is no single end-client demand to slow; it sells to 500M users and evolves the products.
  • Martin and LG argue some software can't be vibe-coded at scale (StreamYard, Notion), so there is a middle ground that agents don't replace overnight.

A sum-of-the-parts model: $13 floor, $30 platform, plus the debt tax shield

Martin builds value from three pieces rather than one multiple and says the market is paying only about $15 for the future deal machine.

  • Existing portfolio: ~$13 per share, from discounting ten years of cash flow; he treats it as a floor because a lower price would imply the platform is worth zero.
  • Acquisition platform: ~$30 per share, from the operating system, scale economies and the learning flywheel.
  • Financing: debt is tax-deductible, so using it to fund growth adds value; he points to hyperscalers issuing debt despite large cash piles.
  • At ~$33, after deducting the portfolio and the tax shield, the market implies only ~$15 for the platform versus his $30.
  • His sensitivity chart varies two inputs: investors' required return (base 11%) and the exit multiple on free cash flow (base 12x).
  • He picks 12x as the low end of software's typical 10-18x range and says narrative-driven stocks like Tesla and Palantir trade far above such multiples.

The assumptions behind the model: margins, growth, success rate and learning curve

Martin deliberately models slower margin gains and weak organic growth, and gives credit only for compounding scale effects.

  • Acquired companies reach ~30% margins in year one, ~42% in year two and ~50% in year three, with a 90% deal success rate.
  • Existing businesses grow only ~2% a year organically because the team prioritizes cash over user growth.
  • Group revenue is about $4B including Airtable and the other recent deal, which add roughly $1.2B; those aren't yet at target margins, so group margin dips in 2028.
  • Learning curve: each doubling of revenue adds one percentage point of margin as more data and companies join the platform.
  • Its AI-driven efficiency lets it outbid rivals in auctions and still earn more per dollar than a conventional buyer.
  • Martin projects out to 2039 so the last acquisitions finish transforming; he calls the margins and timelines his own assumptions, not guidance.

Limits: copycat buyers, a hurdle rate and a debt pile near its 3x cap

The model caps growth because rising competition for software targets raises prices above what the company will pay.

  • Competition: as more firms copy the playbook, acquisition multiples will rise; Martin caps deal spending at $10B a year from 2031 for five years.
  • Hurdle: the company won't buy above ~3x revenue, tied to a 25% unlevered internal rate of return per deal.
  • Leverage target is ~3x and it is close to maxed after the IPO and a stock-funded deal; debt is ~$5B now and ~$39B by 2035 in his model.
  • Free cash flow is expected to exceed $1B next year, so he calls the balance sheet manageable because it buys real, mostly profitable companies, unlike GPUs.
  • Bull case (a call option): the market treats it as a darling and ignores valuation; Martin says the downside is limited by the businesses it already owns.
  • Bigger deals: LG asks about a mega-target; Martin says the CEO has said the company is looking for 'bigger fish' of tens of billions rather than hundreds of billions.

Names and terms at a glance

Glossary & Entities

Hurdle rate — The minimum return an investor requires before doing a deal; here a 25% unlevered IRR.
Unlevered IRR — The internal rate of return on a deal before the effect of debt financing.
Exit multiple — The multiple of free cash flow assumed when valuing or selling a business; Martin uses 12x.
Tax shield — The value from deducting debt interest from taxable profit.
Sum-of-the-parts — Valuing a company by pricing each business or asset separately and adding the pieces.
