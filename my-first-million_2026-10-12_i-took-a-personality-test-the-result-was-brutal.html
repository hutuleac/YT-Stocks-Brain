A 110 narcissism score: fuzzy specialness drives the hunt for validation

Believing the rules don't apply to you gets you to act — but when you can't say what's special about you, you outsource the proof to other people.

  • Context: he recently sold a company, made more money than ever, and hired a life-direction coach because he didn't know what to do next.
  • Result slide: low scores across categories, one off the charts — narcissism at 110, over the top of the scale.
  • Coach's definition: people with the trait feel special, feel their life will be special, and that averages and rules don't apply to them.
  • Upside: it's why you ignore "90% of businesses fail" and start anyway — "what served you can also screw you. The dose makes the poison."
  • Downside: specialness is vague, so you seek it externally (social media, content, speaking) — a higher high when seen, a lower-than-normal low when merely not seen.
  • His example: hosting an event with 30 founders of the world's biggest companies and still feeling a pang of wanting them to know how great he is — "you can make hell out of heaven."
  • Coach's fix: notice it in the moment, then get clear on what's specific about you — "when you've filled your own water cup, you're not waiting for the waiter."

Scott Harrison's call about saying "like" 500 times an episode

Feedback that's uncomfortable for the giver and does nothing for them is the rarest and most useful kind.

  • Scott Harrison of Charity Water — an acquaintance, not a friend — called for the first time in 10 years.
  • His message: you're good at podcasting, but you say "like" ~500 times an episode; it's fixable, just pay attention to it.
  • Two simultaneous reactions: embarrassment at being called out as a professional talker, and real gratitude.
  • Takeaway question: how many people in your life would tell you an uncomfortable truth that isn't self-serving? He says he's still working on the "like" habit.

“The truth is such a rare gift.”— Shaan Puri
Cautionary tale PERSONAL STORY · AGE 21, SUSHI CHAINcareermindset

The 250-page business plan that earned pity, and the switch to "tuna on toast"

Planning can be the comfortable version of hard work that lets you avoid the real work — the fix was to default to action every time.

  • At 21, with two friends, he set out to build the next Chipotle / Subway / Five Guys — in sushi — having first tried sushi only 3-5 months earlier.
  • Confidence came from winning a business plan competition; they cold-emailed founders of Chipotle, Quiznos and others, and only the Noodles & Company founder replied.
  • Three months went into forecasts, financial models, uniforms, promotion paths — a ~250-300-page binder he thumped on the lunch table.
  • The founder flipped without reading; the worst part wasn't rejection but pity. Asked to give it straight: "This is nothing... this is not how you start a restaurant."
  • His version: find a location, put down a deposit, commit to opening in 90 days, serve customers, learn what makes them happy.
  • Living in a co-founder's aunt's basement, they watched Seinfeld's George swap chicken salad for tuna on toast and "do the opposite" — Shaan adopted it: action over planning, doing over impressing.
  • Result: a year later a mentor saw them act on his advice within 48 hours, three times, and blogged they had "the highest bias for action of any founders I've ever met."
Pull quote
“My version of hard work was... you avoid the actual work and you instead do the flavor of hard work that you feel more comfortable with... And for me that was planning.”— Shaan Puri
Framework PERSONAL STORY · HOUSTON TO CHINA, 10TH GRADEmindsetrelationships

From the "laugh track" to whoever you decide to be

Identity is a decision you can remake on the spot — the decision is upstream of all action.

  • Growing up shy, his older sister called him the laugh track — not even a background character at school.
  • At 14-15 he dropped an abstract love note ("the cringe testament") by his crush's locker; she asked "who's this for?" and he invented a girl from another school, then kept the lie going for ~6 months.
  • When the family moved from Houston to China in 10th grade, his sister reframed it on the plane: nobody knows you, so "you could be anybody" — pick the coolest guy you know and be him.
  • He did: at the new school he asked "what would he do?" in each situation; awkward at first, then it simply became him.
  • Same move as an adult: a tech entrepreneur who decided he wanted to be Tim Ferriss — "he probably wouldn't go to meetings" — booked a studio, got a friend as guest one, and that became episode one of My First Million.
  • Closing frame: after any embarrassment, ask what you gained, not just what dignity you lost.

What to do with this

Best Actionable Takeaways

🪞
Mindset: Write down specifically what's special about you, so validation stops being the fuel.
💡
Self-awareness: Catch the moment you want a room to see how great you are — that's the trap, not the event.
📞
Relationships: Give one person the uncomfortable, unselfish truth they need — and thank whoever gives it to you.
🏃
Startups: Replace the plan with a deadline: commit to serving a real customer within 90 days.
🔄
Identity: Pick someone you'd want to be and ask "what would they do here?" in the next hard moment.
