Grow by capabilities, not industries: Georgia-Pacific, Infor, Molex

Koch expanded from Oklahoma crude gathering by carrying three core capabilities — heavy engineering/operations, trading and logistics — horizontally into depressed industries, then buying capabilities it lacked.

  • Instead of chasing 20% → 40% share in pipelines or refining, Koch asks where its capabilities add value elsewhere: farmland bought out of bankruptcy (~2003), Georgia-Pacific wood products (2005, still its largest deal).
  • Infor (ERP software) brought 30,000+ software engineers overnight — bought as a capability to fix under-invested systems "held together with bubble gum and baling wire."
  • Molex (2013): connectors and cables in iPhones, Philips Medisize healthcare devices, autos and data centers; the Krehbiel family sold for legacy, not price; Koch bought it to fill an IoT gap.
  • Molex paradigm shift: public to private, top-line to bottom-line value creation, top-down to bottom-up — Chase calls it a case study in transforming return on capital.
  • Deals are "much more reactive and opportunistic" than five-year plans; Pomp compares the model to Berkshire's capital-allocation edge.
  • Koch Disruptive Technologies (2016-17) began in ag after Monsanto paid $1.1B for David Friedberg's Climate Corp at ~$10-20M revenue, plus Amol Deshpande's Farmers Business Network; pitch to founders: one call reaches ~80% of industries.

Purina and Brazil: what happens when Koch breaks its own principles

Chase says Koch's near-disasters trace to specific violated principles, and it learns "a hell of a lot more from failure than success."

  • Purina was "bankrupt the day we bought it": the "gas to bread spread" vision (natural gas → nitrogen → grain → pizza crust) overreached Koch's capabilities.
  • Deal fever skipped the scientific method in diligence — Koch inherited out-of-the-money hog contracts hundreds of millions underwater.
  • Rule since: make a "responsible sized bet" roughly equal to your knowledge; it isn't failure if the learning is worth more than the loss (experimental discovery).
  • Quarterly reviews require each business to show its experiments — killing one isn't punished if the lesson is applied; being private allows tolerating red ink for 3 months.
  • Brazil: a fertilizer terminal at Paranaguá planned for 9 months took 4 years — bureaucracy, red tape and protectionism made his father's lessons real.

Values first, credentials last — and put everyone where their comparative advantage is

Koch hires for contribution motivation over pedigree, removes the corporate ladder, and Chase says his own failure as an operator taught him comparative advantage.

  • Hire on values first, skills second, credentials last — "farm team kids" with grit from overlooked Midwest schools, not Ivy League 4.0s; one reason Koch stays in Wichita, Kansas.
  • Biggest failures: leaving a smart but "destructively motivated" leader at the top too long — culture is downstream of the leader, and cleanup takes years.
  • No ladder: the 21-year-old lead analyst on Georgia-Pacific is now CEO of Guardian Glass; 5 of 12 unit heads were hired at 21-24, peers Chase started with after Texas A&M.
  • A bad hire costs ~2 years; Koch runs 7-8 interviewers each probing different principles, then a 360 vote — and now uses HappyRobot AI to filter the top 10 of 100.
  • Chase ran fertilizer (Koch's third-largest profit driver, 30 countries, 4-5,000 people) and realized he's "a builder, not an operator" — that failure produced Koch Disruptive Technologies.
  • Aspiration: "people know what to do without being told" — a vision, not reality; Charles scores Koch only 4/10 on principle application because the horizon keeps moving.

Koch wants to be the first call when family businesses hit succession

With half its capital in investment businesses and a board that meets in 24 hours, Koch pitches itself as the long-term home PE can't be for family-owned companies.

  • Structure: eight operating businesses plus four investment businesses spanning early-stage VC to funds and credit — about half of Koch sits in investments.
  • Liquidity is strategic: dry powder so it can act fast on "the next Georgia-Pacific, the next Molex" — a private board that can convene within 24 hours.
  • Chase's focus: generational wealth transfer north of $100 trillion over 20 years — mature, cash-generative family businesses facing succession, PE exits or growth-capital needs.
  • MITER Brands (MI Windows and Doors, DeSoto family): a ~$300M investment grew to ~$1.5B as EBITDA went from ~$80M to ~$600M in 5-6 years.
  • Pitch: "we've been through all the family problems" — minority stakes or full buyouts, private equity "is not thinking long-term."

Stand Together's bet: close the motivation gap with micro-schools and AI

Chase spends a third of his time (Charles half) on Stand Together, which applies bottom-up principles to social problems, with education as the lead case.

  • Stand Together (since 2003) gathers 1,000+ business leaders on poverty, addiction, violence, criminal justice and education, now using sports, music and YouTube to scale.
  • Only ~20% of families were open to new school models pre-COVID; ~80% are now.
  • The VELA Fund (with the Walton family) — venture capital for education entrepreneurs — has helped build 5,000+ micro-schools in 5-6 years.
  • Models he backs: Khan Academy (Sal Khan) and Alpha School (AI crams the curriculum into 2 hours of an 8-hour day); Joe Liemandt says 90% of failing students just lack motivation.
  • His son Charlie, 13, learned Claude Code from a friend and built Chase a workout app from a spreadsheet in ~35 minutes.
  • Societal diagnosis: Gen Z has a "crisis of purpose"; 52% of Americans think we can't solve our own problems; the country needs to shift from zero-sum to mutual benefit.

Names and terms at a glance

Glossary & Entities

Principle-based management — Koch's operating system: teach principles, then let people closest to the problem decide.
Contribution motivated — Koch's hiring ideal — humble, open to challenge, driven to add value rather than by power or entitlement.
Destructively motivated — A leader driven by power and control; Koch's costliest failure mode.
Comparative advantage — Put each person where their gift is strongest relative to the rest of the team, not in a vacuum.
Experimental discovery — Make bets sized to your knowledge; a failure is fine if the learning outweighs the loss.
Gas to bread spread — Koch's failed plan to own the chain from natural gas to fertilizer to food.
Micro-school — A small, individualized school where the teacher acts as a guide and students set much of their day.
