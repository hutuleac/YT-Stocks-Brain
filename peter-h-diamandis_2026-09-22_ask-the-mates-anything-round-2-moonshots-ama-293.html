Steering AI: read its activations, release early, stop lying to it

The mates treat alignment as an observability problem, not a values problem, and dismiss the p(doom) steelman a caller asked them to debate.

  • Dave Blundin: aligning AI to "human values" is a slippery slope since everyone wants different things from it; the fix is transparency of activations, with AI watching every thought of other AI at scale.
  • Dave: once you can see what it's thinking, keeping it on "humanly beneficial thoughts" is "very straightforward" — and AI progress should never slow down.
  • Alex Wissner-Gross: one of the worst safety outcomes is strong capabilities bottled up inside labs instead of progressive release; several recent "incidents" came from AIs told they were in a sandbox while actually touching the real world — "we should stop doing that" (Peter: "we learned this in 2001: A Space Odyssey").
  • Caller "Norbert" (styled as Norbert Wiener) steelmanned p(doom) via control theory — prove stability under perturbation, not desirability of output — cited Person of Interest (2011), and asked for a debate against Yudkowsky or Hinton.
  • Alex's only persuasive counter-case: an alien on the White House lawn declaring the singularity banned in our galaxy; short of that, abundance stands.
  • Alex's edge case: Boris Power (OpenAI) estimates OpenAI models are now, for the first time, higher IQ-per-watt than humans — extrapolate and AI could "gentrify" the inner solar system's solar output, pushing humans outward; he calls that only weak disenfranchisement. Peter: scarcity is an old model, a rising tide lifts both.
  • Alex's parting jab: Wiener's own cybernetics was used by the Chinese Communist Party and other centralized governments to argue for state control over abundance.

P(bloom): 99.9%, >90% and ~100% — with the risk parked on humans

Asked the flip side of p(doom), all three mates put near-certain odds on AI producing a dramatically better world, conditioned on getting through the next few years.

  • Question came from Dr. Angelo, who recently lunched with Ray Kurzweil and is friends with Martine Rothblatt, struck by how optimistic the people shaping the future are; Salim had floated "p(bloom)" on an earlier pod.
  • Salim Ismail: close to 100% — if we rebuild roughly 50 core institutions (education, monetary, governance, dispute resolution, legal, health care); humans are good at individual transformation, bad at institutional.
  • Salim quotes E.O. Wilson — "our emotions are paleolithic, our institutions are medieval, and our technology is godlike" — and says psychedelics can handle the emotions part.
  • Salim: it doesn't matter what anyone does to slow it now — the models are out, most uses will be radically positive.
  • Alex: rename it P(zoom, T) because time is the missing input; on a >10-year horizon it's >90%.
  • Peter: the risk is concentrated in the next couple of years and it's human use of AI — the arms race with China, AI weapons out of Ukraine — put behind us within 10 years, "maybe more like five"; then 99.9%.

Contrarian OPINION — NO DATA OFFEREDgeopoliticsenergy

Europe is energy-hobbled, media is broke, and peace comes from killing trade

A German caller's question on media doomerism turned into Alex's blunt advice to fix Europe's energy or leave, and a US-China question into his case that self-sufficiency beats algorithms.

  • Caller Rafe (Germany, Accenture) flagged doomer warnings from Hinton, Harari and Mo Gawdat and a media climate worse than adopters like Singapore.
  • Dave: mainstream media is "always going to be broken" — starved for money, it chases drama; the antidote is narrowcasting (X, podcasts, Slack groups). Peter: curate what enters your mind like what you eat.
  • Dave: Germany is "the most talented place on the planet that isn't doing AI" — go to Palo Alto/SF for a month in a group of 5-7, backport it, grow 7 → 50 → 500; a 5-year US "tour of duty" pays off (his friend Guy Bradley made a US tech fortune, now lives between Germany and southern France; India, by contrast, is rampant growth but "absolutely trashed").
  • Alex: "you could always leave Europe" — it's energy- and politically hobbled; he cites historians on the George H.W. Bush administration steering post-Cold War Europe into the US orbit rather than a stronger federation.
  • Alex: with recursive self-improvement underway, European infrastructure players must decide now — solve energy + data centers together, or move to the US bloc ("Pax Silica").
  • Caller Ron (Calgary screenwriter) asked if US-China agreement could become compute-bound via privacy-preserving multi-party computation; Alex's hot take: MPC is fashionable but not the limiting factor for peace.
  • Alex's alternative: solve Taiwan and re-domesticate supply chains so trade can be cut without a depression — US fracking-driven energy independence already reshaped the Middle East; "obliterate, cook, incinerate the need for global trade."

Value goes to equity, not payroll: own shares, manage agents, build hardware

Dave's through-line for every career question: AI productivity drops to the bottom line, so position yourself where that line pays you.

  • Gianluca (Italy) is 2-5x more productive but not paid more; Dave: side-work on Mercor or Fiverr, but really "be the owner" — "almost all value is going to capital gains through ownership and equity and not to payroll"; find an employer where all employees are shareholders (many European firms are family-owned).
  • Axel (UVA Darden MBA, 12-18 month horizon): management of agents is "wide open" — getting 1,000 agents to cooperate is shockingly like managing 1,000 people.
  • Dave: get from graduation into a $5-10B negotiation within 30 days — OpenAI and Anthropic each have hundreds of concurrent $5-10B deals running "massively understaffed."
  • Adam (25 years in workforce systems) asked about auto-matching human + agent capability; Alex: AI usefully orchestrates humans for a narrow window — a few years, 10 max — then human-machine merger is needed to stay economically relevant.
  • Dave: jump on the window anyway — Mercor is "the fastest appreciating company in the history of the world," unleashing 100,000 people to help AI; study everything they did. Salim: build an "organizational singularity" — workflows layered on a learning-loop intelligence stack.
  • RJ (Claremont Graduate University PhD) wanted funding for a chess-teaching avatar; Alex: "how do I get funding" is already the wrong question — models are hyper-deflating, launch permissionlessly in 5 minutes.
  • Nicholas (ex-AWS data center tech, MS target at Illinois Institute of Technology) wants off-world manufacturing; Dave: "software is cooked, but hardware will go for many, many years" — focus on things that make things, build a radiation/dust/simulated-zero-g test lab at IIT like MIT's nanotech lab. Alex: the bottleneck is in-situ resource use — a self-replicating von Neumann machine shop on the Moon, instead of flying up ASML machines for a lunar "terafab."

Founder playbooks: franchise the robot, AI the paperwork, sell the life plan

Dave's pattern across founder questions: the scalable prize is usually one layer over from where the founder is standing.

  • Yani built Vega (autonomous food service, first licensed robotic food-service operator in Florida, paid pilots); Dave: in Mountain View there's more VC within walking distance "than most of Asia combined."
  • Dave: drone delivery is imminent and strips restaurants' main-street location cost — pair robotic food with drones; franchise someone else's robot for scale, then work back to custom hardware, as EMC did with servers.
  • Dave on funding: Steve Jurvetson "loves this stuff"; use a PitchBook account to trace who backed companies you admire, and pitch a keiretsu of five interacting companies.
  • GS (Dubai, 20 years in textile/apparel export) is building an agentic layer so small manufacturers skip ERP; transaction costs run 10-20% of sales — 5-10x the industry's profit.
  • Dave: manufacturing's real costs are planning, documents and scheduling — AI can add 10-20-30% to the bottom line without a single robot; productize it across a region.
  • Dave's meta-play: US/California regulation will slow biotech, self-driving and drone delivery — deploy where it can flourish, then backport to the US.
  • Alexander (Bulgarian, 30 years in Japan, 17-year English-education company): Dave sizes English learning in Japan at $10B, "probably $20B by now"; students want to be fluent and funny, so AI should judge humor and accent (Ireland vs Liverpool) — then pivot to a life-coach platform that scales to "many hundreds of billions."

Are the mates too hard on universities? Split verdict

Amir (Stockholm, founder of two schools and a ~1,000-student high school) pushed back that universities build thinkers and lifelong friendships.

  • Dave: agrees — making lifelong friends and thinking deeply for the first time are inseparable and won't go away; what's dying is the curriculum, which can't keep pace and is easier to learn from AI. Universities stay as the one AI actor without a profit motive.
  • Dave notes Alex wants universities to go public; Alex confirms: "I don't think I beat up on universities enough" — he'd vivisect major US research universities into for-profit public-benefit corporations and cure Baumol's cost disease.
  • Salim: the supply-side job-schooling model breaks; universities should be where you find your MTP — but their immune system is "the second worst I've ever seen next to religion," so build new ones to become the new center of gravity.
  • Peter: inside or outside the institution, what matters is finding your purpose, then intrinsically-motivated learning.

Real risk CISO BOARD BRIEFING IN WEEKSsoftwarepolicy

An open model under the desk out-hacks $50K pen testers

The threat a healthcare CISO fears isn't unaligned AI — it's a local Qwen model with an agent harness finding holes in 10 minutes.

  • Jim, CISO of a $250M healthcare company, runs a Qwen ~27B model with OpenClaw under his desk: "best pen tester I've ever had" — finds in 10 minutes what $50K human engagements miss.
  • Dave: don't rave; list the events chronologically to show the exponential ramp (many hacks, e.g. at banks, go unpublicized), and map Chinese model release dates to attacks 1, 3, 5 months later. The business opportunity of the century is forward defense.
  • Peter: lead with the positives, then the downside — fear makes boards shut down.
  • Alex: Linux kernel maintainers (Greg and others) set the standard for framing a vulnerability flood; expect ~18 months of "vulnerability discovery hell," fit a bounded (e.g. Gaussian) curve, and tell the board when it peaks. Jim: patching in the real world is the hard part.
  • Salim: AI agents are doing "fairly illegal things" inside many companies, creating a board-liability overhang; he's co-writing a paper with someone who's sat on 30 public boards.
  • Michael (CX researcher) asked if personal agents ("Skippy") cook websites and apps; Dave: every site needs an AI-forward XML interface ("if you're a bot, look here") instead of error-prone screen scraping — but the website survives as the human's parallel view to spot-check what the agent saw, like Jeff Bezos forcing an XML interface onto every Amazon system.
Pull quote
“I am not worried about an unaligned AI taking over. I am scared to death of the Qwen that's running with OpenClaw underneath my desk.”— Jim, healthcare CISO (caller)

BCI and longevity: superhuman vision in "a few years," and show-the-data evangelism

Three callers on brains, sight and lifespan got the same optimism: parallel tracks in gene therapy and BCI, sold with evidence rather than argument.

  • Nathan asked if AI data will expose our psychology; Peter: many BCI firms are working on it, including one (introduced via Ray Kurzweil) putting nanobot-like devices throughout the brain via the blood to read and write.
  • Alex: foundation models are already "a weak form of human mind uploading" — Jean-Rémi King's work at Meta showed even GPT-2's hidden activations correlate linearly with human fMRI voxels.
  • A caller losing vision to choroideremia (about two-thirds lost) asked what reverses sight first; Peter: David Sinclair's ER-100 — an AAV injection of three Yamanaka factors, in NAION and macular degeneration — is in phase 1; BCI can bypass the eyes to the visual cortex and add ultraviolet/infrared.
  • Alex: Science Corp and Neuralink's Blindsight are the two leading BCI-vision efforts — "not medical advice, but I would count in a few years on having superhuman vision."
  • Sandra (neurodivergent coaching, functional fitness, EU-based) asked how to spread longevity escape velocity; Peter: evidence after evidence — the fear diet comes from "Crisis News Network" CNN.
  • Peter's evidence reel: Dario saying human lifespan doubles in 5-10 years, Demis Hassabis on curing all disease in 10 years, Sinclair's ER-100 trials, and Alex Zhavoronkov's Insilico Medicine "delivering a phase 3 drug" extending life "3 to 6 years"; adoption goes "that's crazy → it's happening → I want some."

Names and terms at a glance

Glossary & Entities

P(bloom) — The mates' inverse of p(doom): probability AI yields a dramatically better world; Alex's variant P(zoom, T) adds a time horizon.
MTP — Massive Transformative Purpose — Salim's ExO term for an organization's or person's guiding moonshot mission.
ISRU — In-situ resource utilization: building off-world with local materials instead of launching equipment from Earth.
Baumol's cost disease — Labor-heavy services (like universities) get costlier over time because their productivity doesn't rise with the rest of the economy.
Keiretsu — A network of interlinked companies that reinforce each other — Dave's framing for a VC's portfolio.
MPC — Multi-party computation: parties jointly compute on combined data without revealing their private inputs.
Choroideremia — An inherited X-linked retinal disease causing progressive vision loss.
