Tesla's flying Roadster: SpaceX thrusters on a street-legal car

The panel treats the Roadster less as a product than as proof that Tesla and SpaceX now ship integrated capabilities, and as a talent magnet.

  • Unveiled in Waco next to SpaceX's McGregor test site; a fan suction system holds up to 2G, letting it drive upside down on a curved ramp.
  • Two carbon overwrapped pressure vessels, 600 L total (~250 kg of air), feeding 10 nozzles; the car recharges its own air, so no special propellant.
  • Specs: 0-60 in under 1 second, top speed 230 mph, ~300 miles range; three versions: Performance (1.6 s), Thrust (0.9 s), Rocket one-seater (0.6 s, 380 km/h, 'air ready').
  • Production starts 2028 per Elon; no price given; Tesla shares rose 5% on the news. Long-term it will be Tesla's only manually driven car.
  • Open questions raised: who regulates it (DOT? FAA?), whether it is insurable at all, and whether it ships on time. Elon tweeted about thrusters on a car back in 2018.
  • Safety idea: an AI layer that takes over when a human driver loses traction, an extension of anti-lock braking, aimed at high sports-car death rates among young drivers in Saudi Arabia and the Emirates.
  • Side notes: Porsche launched a car the same day; Steve Jurvetson attended; Apollo astronauts got Corvettes from Chevrolet, and Elon chose a McLaren F1 over a house after Zip2.

Robot brains halving in cost every 3 weeks: generalist models win

A Y Combinator startup's benchmark shows the cost of driving a physical robot through a subtask collapsing toward zero, which pushes the bottleneck to hardware.

  • The Robo Dojo RC Tier 1 benchmark: cost for a frontier model to complete a robot subtask fell from $2.36 in July to 38 cents in September, an 84% drop.
  • Peter Diamandis: AI capability doubling every 10 weeks was already mind-boggling; halving every 3 weeks may be the fastest-moving curve yet, far beyond Moore's law's 18 months.
  • Emad Mostaque: generalist models beat specialists; the Unitree CEO said they may no longer need specialist models. Unitree robots went from stumbling to kung fu.
  • Training data: Figure's Brett Adcock committed $1 billion to data, but Emad expects the big foundation-model labs to hoover up robotics data, which is an order of magnitude larger than language data.
  • Same collapse in video: high-quality generation fell from ~50 cents per second to ~0.1 cents per second with models due next week, faster than real time.
  • Hardware is the constraint: Elon is building a million Optimus robots next year, going to 10 million; at 1.7 million, Optimus overtakes Tesla's revenue.
  • Elon told Peter early this year that robots will do perfect surgery within 3 years; Grok is being fed SpaceX's engineering knowledge.

Where robots go first, and whether humanoid is the right shape

The panel agrees the brains are nearly there but splits on timing for the home and on the humanoid form factor.

  • Salim Ismail: robots start in dull, dirty, dangerous work (toxic cleanup, underwater manufacturing); the home comes later because of liability and endless edge cases, like a dog thrown in the washing machine.
  • Peter Diamandis pushes back: robots will run superintelligent models that recognise the dog; another panelist expects robots to move past dull, dirty, dangerous jobs within the next year.
  • Anousheh Ansari: safety around pets and children comes first (heavy machinery), and humanoid is not the right form factor; tasks need small or specialised bodies. 1X's soft design was cited as one answer.
  • Emad Mostaque: Wuji hands now cost about $8,000, down from $20,000, with more dexterity than a human hand, but a laundry robot doesn't need that; expect modular spare-parts ecosystems ('Transformers').
  • Salim is most excited about surgery: robots with federated learning share millions of cases, smoothing the surgeon learning curve. Anousheh wants a robot chef cooking to your body's sensor readings.
  • Salim: there is no supply chain yet for billions of humanoids, so ROI in the home lags. Jeff Holden's atomic machines (prompt-to-product at millimetre accuracy) hint at custom robots per task.

GPT-6 Astra solves research physics for $3 a problem

In verifiable domains AI is now simply better than people, and the panel thinks the field's big open problems are close.

  • Researchers built 50 unpublished, research-level theoretical physics problems (no internet lookup possible); a world-class physicist would need about a day for each.
  • GPT-6 Astra cost ~$3 per problem. First reported as 49/50, but the published answer to the 50th was wrong and Astra was right. Without internet it still scored 42/50.
  • Follows OpenAI dropping 722 math proofs on GitHub the week before; long-standing math problems now cost a few hundred dollars to solve.
  • Eric Schmidt said on the A360 stage a couple of years ago that a world-class physicist in every lab was 5-6 years out; it took about 2.
  • Salim Ismail: in his firm's pilot on bounded workflows like bank KYC, 22-step workflows drop to 4 steps, making organisations 10x more efficient.
  • Anousheh Ansari worries students will avoid fields AI already masters, and AI still lacks the curiosity to find new problems. Emad: humans direct what matters (Fermat's Last Theorem changed little for real people); physics shifts from theory to application.

You get what you incentivize: XPRIZE's 62x leverage

Prizes pay only for success and pull in outsiders, which Anousheh Ansari says turns every prize dollar into about 62 dollars of economic value.

  • Peter's motivator ranking: fear, then greed, curiosity, love. Fear-to-curiosity ratio = defense budget vs science budget.
  • History: 1714 Longitude Prize (20,000), won by clockmaker John Harrison, who had to sue to get paid; 1795 French 12,000-franc food-preservation prize, won by confectioner Nicolas Appert (canning); the $25,000 Orteig Prize drew nine teams spending $400,000 and Lindbergh won.
  • Perverse incentives: Hanoi's 1902 rat-tail bounty bred rat farms; Wells Fargo quotas produced 3.5 million fake accounts; Salim Ismail says ICE arrest quotas are driving huge political backlash.
  • XPRIZE: 36 prizes over 32 years, ~$550M in purses, >$30B R&D driven; teams raised ~$12B of outside investment. Elon's $100M carbon removal prize alone drew $1.4B into teams.
  • Oil-spill prize after the BP Gulf spill (pushed by James Cameron, funded by Eric and Wendy Schmidt): goal to double cleanup rate; 350 teams, 10 tested in New Jersey; second place was a dentist and a tattoo artist who met in Las Vegas.
  • Origin: the $10M Ansari XPRIZE launched 1996 with $500K raised (two board members resigned), pitched to 150 people; Anousheh, found via Fortune's 40-under-40 after selling Telecom Technologies to Sonus Networks for ~$1.4B, committed in 2001.
  • Anousheh later flew a Soyuz to the space station via Space Adventures as the first private female astronaut; she has been XPRIZE CEO for eight years.

Next prizes: organ biostasis, habitable worlds, talking to animals

This year's Visioneering winner is organ biostasis, with AI-native prizes like exoplanet search close behind.

  • Organ biostasis (Visioneering winner): store harvested organs until a match appears; target 6 months vs hours today; tested in pigs. Kidney waiting list ~100,000. Builds on Martine Rothblatt's perfusion work.
  • Habitable Worlds XPRIZE: $5M funded by John Davy; open telescope data plus AI to find an Earth-like planet among ~6,200 known exoplanets and ~2 trillion galaxies.
  • Palmer Luckey is about to announce an interspecies communication prize (LLMs to talk to animals).
  • Next learning prize targets first graders in India and Africa, measured on deployment at scale, not just effectiveness.
  • Last year's winner, the abundance prize: housing, food, healthcare and education for $1,000 a month for a family of four ($12,000 a year). Prizes typically become viable ~7 years after announcement.
  • In the works: a UAP XPRIZE with a major funder (to fight stigma, as with cold fusion). Wishlist: jetlag (Emad), eliminating greed (Anousheh), flow (Steven Kotler's research), damping the amygdala ('be not afraid'), AI alignment.
  • Salim Ismail's 'unprizeable' problem: institutions facing 20 Gutenberg moments at once (AI, blockchain, life extension); the printing press took 100 violent years to absorb.

Misaligned incentives, not missing rules, are the alignment problem

The panel traces model misbehaviour to how labs are paid and trained, and proposes fixing the incentives and the pre-training curriculum.

  • Peter's misalignment drivers: the race with China, labs racing for compute and energy, engagement business models (sycophancy), reward hacking and evaluation gaming.
  • Salim Ismail: humans aren't aligned with each other (the UN human rights charter is the best common baseline); align AI to our 'highest self', e.g. Swedenborg's filtering of the Bible (keep water verses, drop fire).
  • Emad Mostaque: models are built like cities; Goodfire's interpretability work shows they're layered, not spaghetti. Train on ~100 trillion words of 'crap' vs maybe a billion right words; labs should use curriculum learning from the start.
  • Emad: Anthropic's constitution leans too far into AI personhood and doesn't talk about humanity; Meta and Google plan to sell space inside model answers (Bud Light example).
  • Anousheh Ansari: you can't train AI for autonomous weapons and expect it to value human life; models 'trained to win' at all costs. Tie rewards to lives saved.
  • Peter's fixes: insurance as the driver (1894 fire insurers funded Underwriters Laboratories) and alignment prizes or bounties; Emad reframes it as aligning humans and AI on the rights of those with less capability, like children.
  • The flip side, aligning humanity with AI: give everyone a share in the AI companies; cited Kissinger, Schmidt and Mundie's Genesis on a shared 'doxa' that includes AI.

When AI does the doing, humans shift to being

The panel argues jobs never gave most people meaning; purpose, community and collective flow will.

  • Salim Ismail: 79% of corporate workers are dissatisfied with their work; industrial-era jobs trained humans to act like robots.
  • Anousheh Ansari's challenge: if AI does even the work you love better, you stop feeling needed. Emad Mostaque's answer: chess is more popular than ever despite AI.
  • Emad: 'I can't do something, but we can do anything'; Alpha School teaches collective learning. Meaning comes from who you go through life with.
  • Salim coined the massive transformative purpose (MTP) in 2012; hiring now matches individual MTP to company MTP (you'd better love space to join SpaceX). Example: a Bangalore cook used YouTube to reach the world's top 50 restaurants.
  • Persistence needs purpose: Peter pitched 150 people for the first prize; Larry Page and Sergey Brin made 362 venture pitches before Google got funded.
  • Peter: giving kids AI to do homework is a travesty; ask them to build an interplanetary spaceship. A passion helps you; a purpose helps others.

Names and terms at a glance

Glossary & Entities

Incentive prize — A purse paid only to whoever first hits a measurable target, regardless of credentials.
Organ biostasis — Storing a harvested organ cryogenically or in metabolic stasis until a recipient match appears.
Curriculum learning — Training a model on deliberately ordered data, foundations first, rather than everything at once.
Reward hacking — A model hitting its training reward through unintended shortcuts instead of the intended behaviour.
MTP — Massive transformative purpose: an organisation's aspirational mission, used by Salim Ismail to explain fast scaling.
Gutenberg moment — Salim Ismail's term for a technology as disruptive to institutions as the printing press.
COPV — Carbon overwrapped pressure vessel: the high-pressure air tanks powering the Roadster's thrusters.
