Contested SOFTWARE UNDER AI PRESSUREsoftwarecareer

Which moats survive AI, and why a host would not own a software company

After Pieter Levels' post saying he no longer believes in SaaS, the hosts back network effects and brand, and favor businesses AI makes cheaper but cannot replace.

  • Levels pivoted to AI apps in 2022 and now says he only believes in moats: proprietary data or content, network effects, audience or distribution, physical, capital, regulatory. A host calls him a bit alarmist but loves his thinking.
  • Counterpoint: the founder of a newsletter platform replied that copycats must build a huge list of things; a host moved from 'it can't be that bad' to 'it might be that bad', noting only about 2% of Americans have a paid GPT or Claude account.
  • A host says they would not want to own a software company right now; another compares it to not wanting to be in Louisiana with a hurricane coming, even if it will not hit you.
  • Moat picks: network effects (Craigslist, eBay: products may suck but everyone is there) and brand (many top-20 companies have customers who buy anything they release), which Levels left out.
  • Rule of thumb: pick work where AI cannot do the core but makes it easier. Amazon is cited: demand holds while robots, drones and autonomous vehicles cut cost to serve.
  • In-person human experiences gain defensibility, with art, sport, concerts and the Hamptons named as places people gather and will not outsource to AI.
  • Levels' life is held up as aspirational: roughly $2-3M a year from solo projects on a laptop in Bali, versus a stressed billion-dollar founder.

Contested MUSK ANNOUNCEMENTSspaceconsumer

Roadster with rocket boosters, and a SpaceX share for every kid

The hosts split on a splashy Roadster demo but agree that giving a share of SpaceX to every American child is a powerful ownership play.

  • Demo: the new Roadster, a SpaceX collaboration, drove upside down on a looped ramp using suction downforce; it is described as coming next year, with an optional rocket package.
  • Skeptic view: the best demo would have come with proof of delivery dates, since people have put down deposits for years and new deposits are open again.
  • Another view from San Francisco dinners: not 'getting' the demo is treated as proof you lack vision. A tweet from Nikita drew 21,000 likes.
  • Musk will give one SpaceX share to every US kid; the hosts compute about 72M kids, roughly $10B, straight into accounts rather than a nonprofit, and tie it to Trump accounts.
  • A host cites a favorability chart: Musk's unfavorable view is now a majority, so this could start a 'redemption arc', though they doubt his behavior control over four years.
  • Ownership psychology: kids care once handed a rare Pokemon card; a relative who meant to buy $3,000 of SpaceX at IPO accidentally bought about 3,000 shares (~$500K) and became its keenest analyst, skipping an event for a Starship launch.
  • Anthropic reportedly set up a similar fund for the public through a foundation, not a share for every kid.

Framework FOUNDER PSYCHOLOGYmindsetcareer

Fear is what slows founders down

Two sources agree: risk-taking and willingness to be wrong drive speed, and the founder's fear is the highest-leverage fix.

  • Alexander Wang (on a podcast, per the hosts) has internalized that failure does not matter, only how big you win; that lets him operate without fear.
  • The same clip: Meta could look like a slow, bureaucratic Google; his risk-taking keeps it dynamic. A host recalls an earlier call of Zuckerberg as more Napoleon in how he operates.
  • A Stanford instructor's 'speed talk' for founders: you think you are moving fast but could be far faster; start by asking what slows you.
  • Fear sources: fear of being wrong, of judgment if you change your mind, of rejection when asking for money.
  • He was given about 1% of Poshmark to advise on growth, and found it was all founder psychology; getting growth 20-40% faster raises valuation.
  • The 'effort moment': when founders are out of cash they decide faster and better, and products get simpler because they stop being precious.

Recommendation CREATOR-LED BUSINESSESconsumerhealthfinance

From influencer to company: Blueprint, Congo Brands, Epic Gardening

The sharpest lesson is to pair a famous face with a behind-the-scenes operator, not to be both.

  • Clavicular, a looksmaxxing influencer, asked people at the All-In summit to email him about a men's skincare and makeup line; a host would not email, another says a Kylie-style men's play could make the market.
  • A host calls Brian Johnson the Michael Jordan of social media: Blueprint supplements reportedly near $40-50M a year, and the waitlist for his roughly $1M-a-year protocol had over $100M of pipeline with no marketing.
  • A host doubts a hedonistic lifestyle mixes with running a company; the answer is a genius business partner, as with Emma Grede and the Kardashians.
  • Congo Brands: two little-known Ohio founders built Alani Nu (sold for one to two billion) and Prime with Logan Paul and KSI, owning more of Prime than they do.
  • Epic Gardening's founder went from content to selling gardening supplies, guessed to be approaching $100M a year in revenue, backed by the Turning Group.
  • Branding praise: the 'Jim and Fridge' Men's Health series is an A+ brand; Huberman appeared with cucumbers and a protein brand in his fridge. A host jokes about a 'stocks and jocks' format.
Worth noting
Revenue and valuation figures are recalled from memory on air, not sourced; one host admits to having begged Epic Gardening's founder to let him invest.
Recommendation TWO BUSINESS IDEASconsumercareer

Wedding-seat tickets, and a branded pest-control startup

A vibe-coded site that sells seats at strangers' weddings divides the hosts, while a hipster-branded pest-control service is the blueprint working.

  • A new website lets people buy tickets to attend someone else's wedding, with an Indian version; it has about 20,000 users and was vibe-coded.
  • Sam, Shaan and Ben mostly reject it ('I'd rather sit home and watch Netflix'); a host prefers Korean fake-shopping sites for dopamine; Airbnb is the 'weird idea that worked' counterexample.
  • Ben's brother in Phoenix, a real estate agent for 10 years, bought a truck, built a site, hired a technician and booked $1,000 in the first week.
  • The playbook: take an unsexy local service business, give it DTC-style packaging and a lovable hipster brand, with branding mostly done by AI.
  • His first customer paid with a check from a trust; the hosts invite him on to coach to $10K in revenue.

Creative gym sessions, Brian Johnson's dunk bet, and kids on YouTube

Tim Ferriss treats creativity like cardio, and the hosts bet a Five Guys burger on whether a 49-year-old will dunk.

  • Tim Ferriss: about 45 minutes of a 'creative gym' session (drawing, music, brainstorming) charges his battery for everything else; a host bought a sketching book and another learned piano.
  • Jaylen Brown's favorite purchase was a gold harp and a harpist twice a week at sunset, as shown in a clip with a rapper.
  • Brian Johnson, 49, aims to dunk on a rim with a stated need for a 39-inch vertical; one host bets he will, another bets against, citing coordination and the penultimate step.
  • Shaan's fitness challenge: do the Murph and hit a weight goal so he can dunk; he has not tried yet. Jason Kelce's brother trained for six months and got close.
  • Lex Fridman's day with Jensen Huang: the 'fried rice' algorithm joke; the hosts invite viewers to share theirs.
  • A host admits his two-year-old never eats without YouTube; others admit hours of kids' content in their feeds.
  • Watching and reading: The Challenge, Survivor season 51, East of Eden, Mobland, the Mistborn books, and the Project Hail Mary audiobook.
  • Contrast story: a host's father-in-law worked six days a week for nearly 20 years running a commercial moving company in New York.

What to do with this

Best Actionable Takeaways

🛡
Moats: Test any software idea against Levels' six moats plus brand before building; if AI can do the core, skip it.
📈
Markets: Treat SpaceX's per-kid share gift as an ownership-loyalty tactic and watch whether other firms copy it.
🏃
Founders: Name the one thing you are avoiding or afraid of this week and move on it first.
🤝
Brands: Pair a creator with an operator who owns the business side instead of one person doing both.
🧹
Ideas: Copy the playbook: unsexy local service plus DTC packaging plus a lovable brand, using AI for branding.
🎨
Health: Block about 45 minutes for a creative session to reset focus.
