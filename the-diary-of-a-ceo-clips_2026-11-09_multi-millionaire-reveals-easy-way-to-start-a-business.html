The one-paragraph takeaway

Video Snapshot

  1. Serial founder with 80+ businesses started since selling golf balls at age 9; cumulatively low hundreds of millions in revenue, low tens of millions in profit, most abandoned or failed.
  2. Core trait: the pain of your problem must outweigh how much you care what people think.
  3. Main thesis: copy a proven business exactly at the start, then tweak over time; finding your idea already exists is validation, not a stop sign.
  4. Websites he names to reverse-engineer a competitor: Wayback Machine (web archive), WHOIS, Similarweb, Alibaba, plus Facebook Marketplace, Craigslist and Facebook ads to validate demand.
  5. Bartlett's frame: ~95% of business is old problems (hiring, cash flow, legal) solved by expertise; new problems (AI) need experimentation.
  6. Copying paid: an iPhone-screen remanufacturing business did $2M, $5M, $9M before exit; trying to be different in fulfillment cost him two years.
  7. Buc-ee's stunt: built an unofficial online store, pitched a reporter, went viral, did hundreds of thousands of dollars in 30 days.
Principle THE CORE THESISmindsetcareer

Copy the proven model exactly first, add your spin later

An existing competitor is someone who took the risk and validated the market for you; start where they are today.

  • Most people Google their idea, see it exists and quit; he reads that as "this is it".
  • Insisting on being different or unique is, in his experience, a signal of pride or ego; copy the model, never the IP or logo.
  • Phone repair, 2010: a caller offered $3 per broken iPhone screen for remanufacturing in China; he copied the model via Alibaba suppliers (5-10% recycled) and cold calling.
  • That business did $2M year one, then $5M, then $9M, and was exited a few years later.
  • Had he pivoted to Samsung screens up front, there might have been no market or method at all.
  • Flight-path analogy: detours at the start land you hundreds of miles off; tweaks along the way keep you close.
  • Bartlett cites a past guest: great founders copy ~95% of the blueprint, e.g. Walmart copying regional grocery chains.

Framework EXPERTISE VS EXPERIMENTATIONcareermindset

Old problems need expertise, new problems need experiments

Founders waste years experimenting on problems thousands of people already solved.

  • Fulfillment business: he replaced storage, pick-pack-ship and per-item fees with one flat fee; over two years learned why each fee existed (clients going bust leave storage bills) and ended up looking like every competitor.
  • Bartlett: ~95% of business is old problems (hiring, cash flow, finance, legal); find an expert or mentor. New problems like AI have no blueprint, so test.
  • Koerner's rule is "test everything except drugs", but on old problems he copies someone who solved it well in the last decade.
  • Hiring: Y Combinator runs seven-minute interviews and knows in three minutes; he gives five people 30 days rather than spend 30 days vetting one.
  • Ad budget: start where the competitor is (e.g. Facebook ads), put ~5% into testing others; nine times out of ten he came back to Facebook ads.
  • Bartlett's own team now asks "why did it end up that way?" before reinventing a 50-year-old competitor's model.
Worth noting
Companies sometimes keep a method only because it works, so the copied default may not be optimal; he accepts that and tests at the margin.
Recommendation WEBSITES MENTIONEDcareer

The websites he uses to reverse-engineer and validate a business

Free tools now replace the capital, relocation or VC a business once needed.

  • Wayback Machine (web.archive.org): snapshots of a competitor's site over a decade; pricing moves (e.g. $99 to $49 every two months), headline copy, tab count, embedded Instagram feed.
  • WHOIS: look up the competitor's domain history.
  • Similarweb (similarweb.com): traffic over time; overlay with archive snapshots, e.g. a mobile-friendly redesign lifting traffic from 3,000 to 4,500 a month.
  • Alibaba (alibaba.com): messaging suppliers to source a copied product.
  • Validation channels: Facebook Marketplace (hundreds of inquiries in an hour), Facebook ads, Craigslist, surveys, a sign in front of your house; AI tools build a site or app in one prompt.
  • Why people don't start: fear of judgment, not connecting tools to ideas, and treating business as zero-sum.
Personal story VIRAL OR BUSTconsumer

The unofficial Buc-ee's online store that went viral

A brand with fanatical loyalty and no shop button was an obvious gap; he filled it without permission.

  • Buc-ee's: ~50 locations, billions in revenue, 40,000-80,000 sq ft road-trip stations; no online store at the time.
  • Trigger: Disney sells billions of dollars of t-shirts online, so a brand with similar loyalty should too.
  • Bought one of everything (thousands of dollars), photographed it in his warehouse, launched a site and cold-emailed reporters.
  • One reporter ran it after Buc-ee's ignored his emails; other outlets followed, and the store did hundreds of thousands of dollars in its first 30 days organically.
  • Partner dynamic: his cousin is the operator who calms down "the ADHD crazy guy".
Worth noting
Launched without the brand's involvement; the clip ends before any brand response is covered.
What to do with this

Best Actionable Takeaways

🔍
Startups: Treat an existing competitor as validation and start from their current model.
🕰
Research: Pull a competitor's history from the Wayback Machine and overlay Similarweb traffic before launching.
🧭
Strategy: Hire expertise for old problems; spend your experimenting time only on new ones.
💸
Pricing: Before simplifying a competitor's fees, ask why each fee exists.
👥
Hiring: Trial several people for 30 days instead of long interview loops for one.
