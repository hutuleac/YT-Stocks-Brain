Cautionary tale MISTAKE #1: FALLING FOR YOUR PRODUCTmindsetcareer

Business romanticism burns cash before revenue arrives

Founders spend first and earn later, so emotional attachment to the product drains the pocket fast, and with outside debt it turns straight into panic.

  • Romanticism = believing your product is the best on the planet and nobody else does what you do; only the customer can confirm it.
  • It costs money: you spend on the dream of sales, and the shock usually comes in under a year, faster for cash-intensive businesses.
  • Borrowing makes it worse: when loan payments come due before revenue, "din romantism, intri in zona de panica".
  • Start from zero and fail badly and you end below zero: debt, the house, the family; that image is how he wakes founders up.
  • The cheapest mistakes are other people's; still, he lets founders make some controlled mistakes, since the ones you pay for are the ones you remember.
  • Pro bono he mentors a young brand (shared friends with the host): from ~20,000 RON a month in sales to 200,000+ RON today by bringing them "back to earth" through measurement.

Principle MISTAKES #2 AND #3: NEW MARKET, WRONG PRICEmindsetcareer

You're another slice of the same pizza, so the market sets your price

In 99% of cases a new business sells something that already exists, so it must win customers from incumbents on price before quality can be discovered.

  • Founders think they create a separate market; in reality another diner just sat down at the same table to take a slice of the same pizza.
  • Cost plus a 20-30% markup is not a price; "pretul formeaza piata" through supply and demand, unless you are first with a genuinely new product.
  • Honey startup: beautiful 100 g jars with fruit flavours, priced 3x per gram vs alternatives on a ~300% margin; don't be greedy early, the buyer pays before they taste.
  • Enter at or below market, let customers test, then select clientele by raising price toward the rest of the market.
  • Consumers changed: stoves (he worked at a cooker maker) were once bought for 20 years and handed down; now design matters and people tire of things faster, but nobody pays 20% more for the same satisfaction.
  • A premium product priced too low hurts premium perception: buyers drift to mass-market or assume pricier means better.
Framework CASE: ITALIAN WADDING PLANT SAVEDcareer

Volume absorbs fixed costs, so "too expensive" is often a volume problem

Unit cost is not fixed; spreading the same fixed costs over more units changes the math, and marginal contribution is the number to multiply.

  • Simple case: 1,000 RON of salaries over 100 units = 10 RON each, over 1,000 units = 1 RON each.
  • First week on a board after PwC, an Italian group was about to close its industrial-wadding plant: total cost 2.7 RON/kg, market price 2.2-2.3, a tiny new player at 1.8.
  • His analysis: production cost was 1.53 RON/kg, so even 1.8 carried a margin; the plant held 30%+ market share and its salespeople were afraid to sell more.
  • Fix: keep the budget at 2.2-2.3 and sell an extra few tens of thousands of kg in September-November at 1.8; it all goes straight to profit. "Don't say dumping if you don't know what dumping means"; the small player couldn't hold 1.8 at scale.
  • Marginal contribution = net sales minus variable costs (or minus purchase cost for a reseller); multiply it to cover fixed costs and leave profit.
  • Volume vs margin: he'd take 15-18% net margin with €20M in pocket over 25% with €10M; big players sacrifice percentage margin for absolute profit.
  • 2008 crisis at a Deva aerated-concrete and prefab maker owned by an investment fund: prices fell, cement suppliers squeezed, and gross margins above 50% vanished.

Personal story STARSTONE: -30% PRICE, +50% VOLUMEcareerromania

Scaling means sacrificing margin and hiring the roles the founder covers

At Starstone he cut prices 30% while staying premium, and argues every growing firm hits a point where the founder can't carry one more kilogram.

  • Starstone (Oradea, 18-19 years old): paving, ready-made fence elements and exterior cladding as a natural-stone alternative; its 2021 fence line was unique and took off.
  • Copied within ~5 years: one competitor took 20%+ market share in 2024, 14 points of it from Starstone, at a price/quality "perfect match" below Starstone's quality.
  • Material margin was 83% (17 RON of materials per 100 RON sold), too high to multiply; they cut production cost per unit, then repriced categories 30% lower while staying premium.
  • Result so far: 50%+ volume growth on the category relaunched in March (another in August) while the category shrank in 2026; pricing set by a deep AI-assisted market analysis of relevant competitors.
  • EBITDA benchmarks: 12%+ is good, 12-20% reasonable, 20%+ very good or a sign you're still small; small spirits makers run 33-40% vs ~20% at scale, because the founder works unpaid as shareholder, CEO, sales and marketing.
  • The break: you "can't be in 10 places at once"; 100 kg on your back is fine until one more kg buckles your knees. Staying put "nu i alegerea ta, e alegerea pietei"; stop pushing the boulder and it rolls back over you.
  • New costs must be covered by new revenue, at least break-even; two stores don't mean double profit, because the cost structure changes. Stress-test before financing; Excel is a mirage, and most businesses die in execution.

Framework PROFILE > EXPERIENCE > COSTcareer

Hire for profile, automate the repetitive, and make every manager measure

Recruitment belongs to the managers who will run the people, and numbers belong to everyone, not just finance.

  • HR and headhunters are support; the sales director and CEO who know the strategy do the real hiring (he just restructured Starstone's sales team).
  • Ranking: profile first (you're born with it), experience second (teachable if they're curious), cost third; all three in budget is the perfect match.
  • Interview tactics: joke to break their rehearsed control, calm the nervous ones, ask the same question several times to test consistency; let them name their salary first.
  • Give an honest picture ("if it's rough, I say it's rough"); salary is short satisfaction, what they learn is what they monetize later.
  • Excel fan "since kindergarten": anything someone does repeatedly every month can be automated; his reports go from database copy-paste to done.
  • Numbers aren't only finance's job: his Oradea salespeople didn't know their market size or competitors; no manager should come to a discussion without measuring. Budgeted spend is only allowed once the topline is actually made, a lesson he aims at state budgets.
  • Selling change: the joke about meeting "Luminita" (the light at the end of the tunnel); pick drivers who pull others, and remember the word change alone scares people.
Personal story ȚIRIAC CFO, TEILOR CHAIRMANcareermindsetromania

From Țiriac's holding to Teilor's board: lessons on courage and letting people go

His best and worst career moments both came at the Țiriac group, and the lesson he carries is to decide for the long run even when it hurts.

  • At Țiriac he was holding-level CFO, on the same line as the CEO; besides the core business he ran special projects (foundations, an ice rink, aviation), Ion Țiriac's investments and exits like the UniCredit minority stake (2015) and Metro.
  • He recommended every deal as if it were his own money; the board decided. He thinks Țiriac is more appreciated abroad than in Romania.
  • Lessons: (1) don't enter fields where you lack exposure, as he did with capital-markets investing pushed on the CFO role, then the pandemic hit; (2) be firmer; (3) when you're ~90% sure and have the numbers, have more courage.
  • The pandemic loss was his first ever; he lost 6 kg in a week and it was why he left the group "not as I'd have wished".
  • Teilor: board member for ~6 years and chairman for ~4; revenue from ~100M RON (2021) to ~700M RON expected this year, EBITDA 130M RON, listed bonds; the brand is owner Florin Enache's work.
  • After the Ukraine war, Teilor had hired top people with fanfare and had to let them go months later; "empathetic with those who stay": 40 leave so 600 don't, like cutting the rot from an apple.
  • Fired only two people himself, both at PwC for discipline; otherwise he explains the change and people self-select out. Sometimes a loyal early employee, supplier or bank becomes the blocker and must go.
  • Crisis habit: in 2008 Romania said "it won't happen to us"; do the homework before the shock so you're the one taking share from those who didn't.

What to do with this

Best Actionable Takeaways

💸
Founders: Put a cash runway on your romantic phase: count the months until money runs out before revenue arrives.
🏷
Pricing: Start from the market price and work backwards to the cost structure that makes it profitable.
📊
Finance: Compute marginal contribution per unit before cutting volume or closing a line as "too expensive".
👥
Hiring: Screen for profile and curiosity first; teach the experience.
⚙
Operations: List every task someone repeats monthly and automate it this quarter.
🧭
Leadership: Decide hard calls for the next two years, not the next week.

Names and terms at a glance

Glossary & Entities

Marginal contribution (contributie marginala) — Net sales minus variable costs; the per-unit amount that pays for fixed costs and then profit.
Fixed-cost absorption — Spreading fixed costs over more units so the unit cost falls as volume rises.
EBITDA — Earnings before interest, taxes, depreciation and amortization, the operating-profit measure used to value companies.
Dumping — Selling below cost to take a market, which a small low-cost player is not necessarily doing.
BCA — Romanian abbreviation for autoclaved aerated concrete blocks.
