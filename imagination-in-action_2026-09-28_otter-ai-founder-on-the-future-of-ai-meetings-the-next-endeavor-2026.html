Why Otter built its own speech stack, and what Zoom's licensing says about it

Liang bet that no off-the-shelf model handled long multi-speaker conversations, so Otter built transcription and speaker identification itself.

  • Liang led Google's mobile map location platform from 2006 to 2010, then built a mobile startup that was acquired before founding Otter with a co-founder ten years ago.
  • The spark: Gmail and search let him find anything, but he couldn't search something he heard three hours ago; he says he has a bad memory.
  • In 2016 Google Voice, Siri and Alexa weren't trained for long multi-speaker conversations, so Otter tested everything and built its own recognition.
  • All Otter transcription and speaker identification is in-house, with no third-party API, which he says makes it highly accurate and fast.
  • Speaker attribution matters: he says Google Meet and Zoom can't separate multiple people in one room, and Otter has worked on it for ten years.
  • Zoom licensed Otter's technology for transcripts between 2017 and 2023; Liang says Zoom stopped after its IPO but that Otter still has better technology.
  • Copycats now exist: Google and Zoom embed their own transcription and summaries, yet Liang says Otter is still growing rapidly.
Worth noting
Liang is the founder describing his company's own technical lead and the Zoom history; the host says his own meetings run through Otter and a wearable recorder.

From transcripts to a knowledge graph that acts on your behalf

Voice is the most overlooked source of business knowledge, and Otter wants it to feed actions so nobody types into a CRM again.

  • Managers can spend 70-80% of their time in meetings, which is where most business intelligence is generated and then lost.
  • Three stages: capture as many meetings as possible (most are still not captured), understand the content, then act.
  • The knowledge graph links entities such as people, projects and customers, and learns who needs to collaborate with whom.
  • Action example: say 'I need to tell Simon' and the engine sends the Slack message; it can also notify you when a colleague's change affects your work.
  • Customers own their data; Otter calls it an engine because it takes action, not just stores knowledge.
  • Voice-first: Liang says he just hired someone who codes entirely by voice, and he expects most people won't need to type eventually.
  • The host cites psychology studies that people are happiest when speaking and says the keyboard is not a natural interface.

From per-seat to usage pricing, and a TAM bigger than CRM

The host asks whether note-taking becomes a free embedded feature or a hundreds-of-billions product; Liang says the business model will evolve toward usage.

  • Otter is at $100M ARR; its slide claims a $100B category, which Liang calls an underestimate because CRM covers only sales while every team holds meetings.
  • The host's CRM analogy: CRM could have been a $50 product but Siebel and Salesforce charged $10,000 a seat by proving productivity gains; Otter faces the same fork.
  • Liang says most SaaS uses seat pricing but more products are moving to usage-based pricing, and he cites Anthropic's agent products making money that way.
  • He expects Otter to adopt usage-based pricing, with outputs like an automatic product requirements document written from a one-hour brainstorm.
  • Verticals: software companies are core customers; finance is a surprisingly strong second, despite being slow to adopt AI.
Worth noting
Liang is also selling a company whose valuation depends on this TAM argument.

Recording everything: behavior change, redaction and off-the-record controls

Liang expects people to take constant recording for granted, but enterprises need exceptions and tight access control.

  • The host says people act differently when recorded; Liang agrees it changes behavior today but expects people to eventually assume everything is captured.
  • Exceptions: one-on-ones, HR, legal and financial matters need access control or no capture.
  • Otter is building a redaction mode and an off-the-record option to remove sensitive content; Liang says it should be good but is still being worked on.
  • Customer meetings are encrypted and Otter can't see them, so he can't tell the host the most outrageous thing it has transcribed.
  • Always-on glasses and pendants with video and audio are coming, but nobody in the Silicon Valley room was wearing them and many people avoid it.
Worth noting
The redaction and off-the-record features are described as still in progress, so the trust question stays open.

Live coaching, self-grading and whether meetings survive AI

Otter already coaches in real time and Liang grades his own performance with it, while he guesses meetings may fade in 5-10 years.

  • Live coaching: during a Zoom or Meet call with a customer the AI listens and feeds answers you can read back when you can't answer a question.
  • Self-grading: after customer meetings Liang asks AI whether he asked the right questions, caught cues or talked too much; he thinks it applies to personal life too.
  • A finance customer said training a new associate takes almost a year; they now use Otter to speed it up through simulated conversations scored in real time.
  • Liang's personal fantasy is capturing all of his life, voice included, as a coach and assistant, but the company is focused on the team use case.
  • The host asks whether AI could tell a CEO when they're wrong; Liang says an AI is easier to take than a person.
  • Debate raised by the host: at MIT most hold that AI amplifies a few people in a room (camp A), at Stanford more say AI will originate ideas (camp B), and nobody predicts it well.
  • Liang's ideal: at the end of a meeting, the AI says all action items are already finished; meetings may eventually not be needed in 5 or 10 years.

Names and terms at a glance

Glossary & Entities

Conversational knowledge engine — Otter's term for a system that captures conversations, builds a knowledge graph and takes actions from it.
Speaker identification — Attributing each spoken segment to the right person in a multi-speaker conversation.
Seat-based pricing — Charging per human user, versus usage-based pricing that charges by work done.
ARR — Annual recurring revenue, the annualized run rate of current subscriptions.
