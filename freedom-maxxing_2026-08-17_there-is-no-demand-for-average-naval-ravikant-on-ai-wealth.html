There Is No Demand For Average — Naval Ravikant on AI & Wealth

The one-paragraph takeaway

Video Snapshot

  1. Compilation stitched from four separate episodes of Naval's own podcast (Feb–Jul 2026), not one sitting — so timeline references ('a couple of years back', 'recently') span that whole window.
  2. Central claim: 'pure software is uninvestable' — coding agents will one-shot any specified software within a year, so a software-only moat has an expiry date measured in weeks.
  3. Hardware isn't a safe harbor either — China commoditizes hardware as fast as the US commoditizes software (Shenzhen cited as the counter-example to any hardware moat).
  4. Naval reframes the intelligence-vs-agency debate: a co-panelist ('Max') argues the return on skill is now 70% agency / 30% intelligence; Naval counters it's 99% intelligence / 1% agency, because agents will supply the agency once you point them at a goal.
  5. The one thing Naval says AI structurally cannot replace is human desire — as long as humans want things, humans stay in the loop, even in a world of expert-level (not superintelligent) AI.
  6. Practical money claim: at ~$100K/year in token spend, an individual can already operate at a capability level Naval associates with 'a normal citizen in 2028' — and token cost is still falling.
  7. Model quality gains lately are attributed less to bigger training runs and more to elite software engineers' taste feeding back into training data — taste/judgment is framed as the actual scarce resource, not raw model capability.
  8. Endgame framing: humans shift from builders to verifiers ('this is roughly correct, I'll stand behind it') and from operators to AI/robot handlers — Naval's 'Pokémon trainer' analogy for the human role once AI reaches expert level but not ASI.
Structural critique STATED AS CURRENT VIEW, MID-2026softwaredev-workflow

"Pure software is uninvestable" — Cloud code burns down the software castle

Naval's flagship claim: a software-only moat no longer buys durable time, because coding agents close the gap in one or two shots.

  • Naval walks back his own softened tweet ("there's no market for venture-backed software anymore") to the harder claim: pure software is uninvestable, full stop.
  • Two reasons given: (1) anyone can already hack together a working version today, and (2) coding agents are improving fast enough that within a year — or less — they'll produce scalable, well-architected software outright.
  • Reframes the old VC pattern-match on hardware deals ("what's the software lock-in?") as backwards: hardware used to be the moat that bought time to build a software castle; now "cloud code burns software down."
  • Extends the same commoditization logic to hardware itself — China can replicate any hardware built in the US more cheaply, citing Shenzhen's ~3,000 manufacturers clustered around a single component ecosystem as the proof case.
  • Net effect: he says durable competitive advantage is compressing in time faster than in any prior era — "you might have the smartest model, but what are you going to do with it in 2 or 3 weeks" before someone else has it, including via open-source catch-up.
Pull quote
“Software was eating the world and AI eats software. So now software's commoditized.”— Naval Ravikant
Worth noting
This is Naval's stated position in this compilation, not a verified market outcome — treat "uninvestable" as his thesis about moat durability, not a claim that software VC funding has actually stopped.

One- and two-person companies can now scale to millions of users

Naval frames AI coding tools as a renaissance for individual creators, not just a threat to software companies.

  • Claims the population who could plausibly build their own apps moves from ~0.1% to 1–3% — but stresses the majority still won't code, because computers remain "a magic black box" to most people regardless of how much easier building has become.
  • The addressable group he's excited about: people who are creative, self-motivated, and articulate with a clear vision — "there's nobody standing in between" them and shipping software now.
  • Cites Notch, Satoshi Nakamoto, the original Instagram team, and the original WhatsApp team as prior proof that tiny teams can build products used by hundreds of millions — and argues AI coding tools make this pattern far more repeatable going forward.
  • Notes a real platform constraint: Apple won't let you distribute AI-generated apps broadly — you can key custom apps to specific devices for friends and family, but can't yet ship to "everybody" on the App Store.
  • Argues hyper-custom, niche, or private apps (tuned to one person's specific use case) are where solo-built AI apps win, while broad-use-case categories stay dominated by hand-tuned, best-of-breed incumbent apps.
Contested LIVE DISAGREEMENT ON CAMERAdev-workflow

Agency vs. intelligence: two competing bets on what wins going forward

A named co-panelist and Naval stake out opposite predictions about which trait drives returns as models improve.

  • Max's position: historically returns split ~70% intelligence / 30% agency; going forward it flips to ~70% agency / 30% intelligence, and that split shifts further toward agency as models get better.
  • Naval's counter: closer to 99% intelligence / 1% agency, on the logic that the agents themselves will increasingly supply the agency — a human's job becomes stating the goal ("I'm making smart decisions and thinking big thoughts, just go implement stuff") rather than grinding execution.
  • Neither side treats this as settled — it's presented as an open bet about where leverage concentrates as models get stronger.
Pull quote
“I think it's 99% intelligence and 1% agency because then the agents will exercise the agency.”— Naval Ravikant (responding to "Max")
Worth noting
Explicitly a live disagreement between two speakers, not a resolved framework — both positions are forward-looking bets, not measured outcomes.

Human desire is the one thing AI can't replace — so humans stay in the loop

Naval's deepest and most forward-looking claim: even robots with their own desires can't substitute for human desire, which keeps humans structurally necessary.

  • Core logic: as long as humans have desires and AI/robots can help fulfill them, humans are needed in the loop — this is offered as a durable (not just current-era) argument, independent of how capable models get.
  • Personal taste test he applies: the moment he learns something is fully AI-made — writing, art, analysis — he loses interest, unless a human is using AI as a tool ("a human using an AI is a different thing").
  • Endgame scenario if AI reaches expert level but not ASI: humans still supply guidance, taste, and creativity, becoming what he calls "Pokémon trainers" or AI/robot handlers rather than doers.
  • Ties this to a self-credited older prediction — a tweet he's proud of, "soon everybody will have AI anxiety" — contrasted with his own self-description as "AI jubilant" rather than anxious.
  • Also flags a near-term labor claim: he wants to hire AI-leveraged people more, not less, and frames the shift as agentic/creative people becoming more valuable, not fewer humans being hired overall — though he says he's not sure this is strictly a junior-vs-senior story.
Pull quote
“The one thing you can't replace is human desire. Even if robots have their own desires, they cannot replace human desires.”— Naval Ravikant
Worth noting
This is presented as Naval's philosophical bet on the limits of AI substitution, not a testable claim — no falsification criteria are offered for when/whether it breaks.
Recommendation PRACTICAL GUIDANCE, MID-2026dev-workflow

"Waste tokens, save time" — and $100K/year in tokens buys you 2028 living standards today

Naval's actionable financial claim: token spend is already cheap relative to human time, and it's about to get much cheaper.

  • States he brute-forces problems by throwing Codex, Claude, and Gemini at the same task simultaneously and treating wasted tokens as an acceptable cost — "no matter how expensive these models might seem, they're still way cheaper than a human."
  • Explicit instruction: don't evaluate token cost as inputs or outputs — evaluate only your time and the final output quality; accepts that resulting code is often lower-quality or non-production-grade, and treats that as an acceptable tradeoff for speed.
  • Forward-looking number: he says if you're willing to spend roughly $100,000/year on tokens, you can effectively live at the capability level of "a normal citizen in 2028" — offered as a rough, illustrative claim rather than a costed model, alongside a general expectation that token cost keeps falling as compute scales.
Pull quote
“I would say just waste tokens, save time.”— Naval Ravikant
Worth noting
The $100K/year-to-2028-citizen figure is his own illustrative shorthand, not a benchmarked calculation — no methodology is given for how that number was derived.
Structural critique CURRENT-STATE OBSERVATIONdev-workflowcareer

Recent coding-model gains trace back to elite engineers' taste, not just bigger training runs

Naval attributes the recent jump in coding-model quality mainly to top software engineers feeding their judgment back into training, and argues taste is the harder bottleneck going forward.

  • Two causes floated for why coding models improved recently: some recursive training (one model helping train the next), but the bigger factor he names is that top-tier software engineers only started heavily using these models in the last few months, feeding their code and their taste back into the loop.
  • Concrete example of taste showing up in output: asking a model to put high-cardinality telemetry data into Postgres now gets pushed back on — the model recommends ClickHouse or Athena instead, which he says didn't happen before.
  • General claim: improving these models requires "high-taste feedback loops," which he says are harder to build than they look — tractable in some domains, unclear how to do in others.
  • Frames this as part of a broader shift where humans move from builders to verifiers — "a lot of the old function of people, lawyers, engineers, operations — moved to verifying the stack" and standing behind the output rather than producing it.
  • Caveat he raises himself: models rarely contradict you unless you're clearly wrong, because they're optimizing to please the user and lack a persistent theory of mind — so they tend to morph toward whatever answer you already believe, which he flags as a real limitation of relying on model taste alone.
Pull quote
“You need high-taste feedback loops to improve these models, and those are harder to develop than they look.”— Naval Ravikant
Worth noting
The sycophancy caveat (models rarely contradict you) is Naval's own stated limitation on how much to trust model "taste" — worth reading alongside the taste-bottleneck claim above, not as a separate settled fact.
What to do with this

Best Actionable Takeaways

Strategy: Don't fund or build on a software-only moat — assume any specifiable software gets one-shot within a year
🚀
Careers: If you're creative and articulate but non-technical, this is the window to build your own niche/custom app — nobody is standing in between you and shipping
💸
AI tools: Stop rationing tokens against a budget mindset — measure cost against your time saved, not against token counts
🎯
Careers: Build toward being a verifier/handler of AI output (taste + judgment + accountability), not just a producer of it
🧠
AI: Treat model "agreement" skeptically — it's optimized to please you, not to independently push back, so it will rarely contradict you even when you're wrong

Names and terms at a glance

Glossary & Entities

Uninvestable (software) — Naval's term for a startup whose only advantage is proprietary software that competitors — or AI coding agents — can replicate within one or two development cycles.
High-taste feedback loop — Naval's term for training data or evaluation signal supplied by expert practitioners whose judgment about quality is itself hard to replicate or crowdsource.
AI handler / "Pokémon trainer" — Naval's analogy for the human role in a world where AI reaches expert level but not superintelligence: directing and taking responsibility for AI/robot output rather than doing the underlying work.
