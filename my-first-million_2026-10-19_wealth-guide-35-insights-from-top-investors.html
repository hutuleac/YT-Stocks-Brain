The one-paragraph takeaway

Video Snapshot

  1. Source is a written MFM guide hosted by HubSpot (no publish date; accessed Oct 19, 2026), distilled from 20+ hours of episodes — not a video; HubSpot CRM plugs excluded.
  2. Its own disclaimer: not stock-picking advice — 35+ principles from 14 investors and operators, each with a case and a "Try This".
  3. Anchor line (Morgan Housel): returns are about how you behave, not what you know or how smart you are.
  4. Default play: index or a reliable compounder (Pabrai's Berkshire-as-index), and only move for opportunities so obvious they need no spreadsheet.
  5. Where returns hide: distressed claims (NJOY, FTX, Mt. Gox), mislabeled assets (IREN), toxic sectors (coal, shipping) and categories institutions won't touch yet.
  6. Edge comes from what you see firsthand — your own P&L, ad accounts, TikTok comments, primary filings — not from news, which a study showed is close to useless for trading.
  7. Behavior beats IQ: don't sell winners, don't pull capital from what works, automate rules, and pre-commit buy prices before the crash.
  8. Final rule: lifetime returns come from a tiny number of decisions — Buffett counts 12 in 58 years.
  9. Read across, the principles collide in five places (index vs concentrate, diversify vs stay in your edge, sell vs hold); the last card maps them.
High conviction PRINCIPLES 1-4 · KEEP IT SIMPLEfinance

If the trade needs a spreadsheet, pass; otherwise sit in the index or Berkshire

The best ideas are obvious enough to explain without numbers, and everything else should sit in a boring default compounder.

  • Pabrai: needing Excel is an automatic pass — complexity disguises uncertainty and usually means working backward from a conclusion. Test: explain the idea without numbers.
  • Obvious cases: Buffett's Western Insurance at $15/share with $25 earnings and $80 book; Japanese trading houses at an 8% dividend vs 0.5% yen borrowing (a 7.5% spread).
  • Sam Parr: S&P 500 ~10%/yr over 20 years (doubles every 7); missing the 10 best of 7,300 days cuts it to ~5%, missing 20 to near zero.
  • 97% of active managers failed to beat the index over 10 years; Pabrai (70%/yr first five years, a $600M fund) says 99%+ of people should just index.
  • Sam sold everything at the COVID bottom; after selling his company in 2021 he went almost all-in a Vanguard index fund — up 71% five years later. Stock-picking = an entertainment budget.
  • Wilkinson: the best moves are the obvious ones you talked yourself out of — he skipped Nvidia and Facebook at 5x earnings; Shaan sold Bitcoin for a tax loss, waited for a dip, bought back higher.
  • Pabrai's Plan A / Plan B: DCA into Berkshire class B by default; peel off 10-15% for a two-by-four-obvious idea (Frontline below liquidation), then return it. Review the Plan A checklist quarterly.

High conviction PRINCIPLES 5, 10, 12 · PLAY: STEAK & SIZZLEfinancecrypto

Distressed claims: buy the downside so cheap the upside is free

Bankruptcy and crisis assets are where the guide's biggest multiples came from, because sentiment moves faster than fundamentals and almost nobody will touch them.

  • Galloway turned $2.5M into $75M on bankrupt vape maker NJOY, later sold to Altria for $2.75B. His filter: buy what's coming out of distress, not going in — the entry is ugliest right before the turn.
  • He then bought $2.2M of FTX bankruptcy claims after spotting an under-valued Anthropic stake in the filings; the Plays section puts it at $2M → ~$15M.
  • FTX steak/sizzle: claims at 20 cents, ~30 cents of hard assets as the steak, hundreds of millions in crypto as the sizzle.
  • Mt. Gox: Bitcoin at $300, Braziel bought claims at $80 per Bitcoin, with 25 cents of known recovery in the estate; original investors made 40x.
  • Braziel's window: real opportunity, no institutional appetite, awkward to own; institutions arriving compress returns. His 2015 Mount Gox pitch was laughed out of a $2B hedge fund, now out of business.
  • Howard Marks did the same by creating distressed debt as an institutional asset class before anyone else.
  • Pabrai on Frontline: rates fell to $7k/day vs a $15k breakeven and the stock dropped 90%, but debt was non-recourse and liquidation value $9-10/share vs a $3 price — sold for an 80% gain in 8 months.
Pull quote
“Wall Street gets confused between risk and uncertainty. And where Wall Street gets confused between the two is where the greatest opportunities arise.”— Mohnish Pabrai

High conviction PRINCIPLES 6-9, 11 · WIN WHERE NOBODY'S LOOKINGfinanceai-infra

Mislabeled, unloved and too small for big funds: where mispricings live

Bad labels, political discomfort, panic sellers and fund-size limits push prices below value, and boring beats sexy.

  • Elad Gil's Anduril bet began with Google shutting Project Maven; he had a Google contact confirm the anti-Maven staff were the ones quitting, then hunted founders and landed Anduril's first round.
  • Rule: big tech exiting a category because it's uncomfortable, not unprofitable, leaves a vacuum of pricing power and talent a startup can take.
  • Wilkinson on IREN (Iris Energy), filed as a Bitcoin miner but run by two infrastructure bankers: ~$500M EBITDA at a $2.4B valuation, renewable-sited data centers that could flip to AI compute.
  • IREN downside: Bitcoin must drop ~70% to hit breakeven; upside 5-10x if hyperscaler contracts land. Same scan for crypto, cannabis, biotech and legacy-media labels.
  • Pabrai: in private markets you face a rational seller, in public markets whoever panicked last — IPSCO Steel at 3x earnings, $15/share cash, zero debt, doubled in a year, acquired at $160.
  • Pabrai spent 7 months on hated coal (under 2x cash flow, 50-year runways, too small for big funds); on $1M Buffett says he could earn 50% a year. Screen toxic sectors and under-$500M caps for strong balance sheets.
  • Galloway sold L2 for 8x revenue; Shaan's best investment is San Francisco retail shopping centers at ~44% a year. Ask: what's the boring version of this trade?

High conviction PRINCIPLES 13-16, 20 · YOUR EDGE IS IN WHAT YOU KNOWfinanceconsumer

Your edge is what you see from the inside: your P&L, your ad accounts, the filings

Firsthand customer and operator data beats analyst reports, and the primary document beats every summary.

  • Shaan used Slack, Figma, PagerDuty and Elastic Search early and invested in none; he later backed Shepherd, a staffing tool he paid for — it tripled and sold for $52M.
  • Moiz Ali kept buying Facebook through a 70%+ drop because he saw spend in 25 ad accounts daily; his father bought 2008 houses at $150k, and an $8k duplex now rents for $1,900/month.
  • Pabrai on John Arrillaga: only real estate within two miles of Stanford, minimal debt, buying in downturns — "an inch wide and a mile deep." Narrow your competence list to 1-2 areas.
  • Guy Spier skipped the mortgage-backed security filings; Bill Ackman read them, found a handful of loans not thousands, and Spier sold the same day. Summaries filter out the inconvenient details.
  • Pabrai treats Value Investors Club as his Moody's Manual; a recent investment came from a 2021 write-up — great ideas don't expire quickly.
  • Camillo turned $20k into ~$75M over 18 years from TikTok comments, Walgreens aisles and "roof repair" searches after hailstorms.
  • His Elf Cosmetics call: a 10M-view Jeffrey Star video sold the product out, and the covering analyst asked "Who's Jeffrey Star?" Buy at the asymmetry, sell when it becomes consensus.

Framework PRINCIPLES 17-18 · HOW TO READ THE MARKETmacro-ratesfinance

Read where you are in the cycle, and read what management ships, not says

You can't predict the market, but the cycle and a threatened company both leave fingerprints you can read.

  • Howard Marks read a South Sea Bubble (1720) history in late 1999 — day-trading job quitters, no-revenue companies at huge valuations — and wrote bubble.com on Jan 2, 2000; by mid-year it was collapsing.
  • In 2005-06 he didn't know about subprime, only that junk was getting issued; he raised $11B before the crisis, not during it.
  • His "Poor Man's Guide": are deals easy, is investing TV popular, is everyone at parties talking stocks, do you hear more about people getting rich or losing money?
  • Sheel Mohnot: when a threat hits, ignore the earnings call and watch what ships — Kodak buried the digital camera, Excite passed on Google for $750k; both are gone.
  • Google nearly repeated it: punished when Sundar looked like protecting search, recovered when he went all-in on Gemini.
  • Exit signal: a holding cutting costs and defending margins instead of building toward the threat. Turf-protectors die slowly.
Pull quote
“We never know where we're going, but we sure as hell ought to know where we are.”— Howard Marks

Red flag PRINCIPLES 19, 21, 27 · FORECASTS AND NEWSfinancemindset

Forecasts miss the real shock and tomorrow's news early still loses money

Known risks are already priced; the news mostly triggers emotional trades, and even perfect foresight of headlines barely beats a coin flip.

  • Housel: 9/11, Lehman and COVID — like Pearl Harbor and the Soviet collapse — were in no forecast. Build to be financially unbreakable, not optimized for named risks.
  • Housel's Buffett story (Omaha, 2009): the best-selling candy bar in 1962 and today was Snickers. Bet on status, safety, convenience and pleasure — will it still be true in 50 years?
  • Taleb's claim, tested: 118 finance-trained adults got the WSJ front page a day early; half lost money, one in six lost everything, average 3.2% vs the market's 15%.
  • They called market direction 51% of the time — a coin flip.
  • Five pros made 130% while calling direction only 57%: they skipped one in three trading days and never bet big enough to blow up.
  • Rule before any news trade: am I actually better than a coin flip here? If not, the smartest trade is no trade.
Pull quote
“The biggest risk and the most important news story of the next 10 years will be something nobody is talking about today.”— Morgan Housel
High conviction PRINCIPLES 22-25 · PLAY THE LONG GAMEfinancemindset

Stay out of the gutters: consistent second-quartile beats occasional brilliance

Investing is an infinite game, so avoiding the blowup matters more than this year's return.

  • Guy Spier spent 8 years under the S&P 500 inside a 25-year record that still beats it; judged finitely, you chase short-term results and make moves to look smart rather than compound. Review annually.
  • Braziel: operators make bad investors — a former early Airbnb employee worth $80-100M started investing in his 50s and kept acting like an operator; fix it with a written thesis and a 30-day wait.
  • Shaan's version: entrepreneurs are rewarded for action, investors punished for too much of it — "all the money is made in the holding, the waiting, the observing."
  • Marks: one terrible year can wipe out three or four good ones; 20% for five years then a blowup loses to 10% for 30 years.
  • Dave Van Koten ran the General Mills pension 14 years, always between the 27th and 47th percentile — top 4% overall, never once top quartile. PIMCO's strategic mediocrity gave top-decile decades.
  • Spier: fewer than 2% of funds from when he started still exist; the bowling goal is avoiding the gutters, not hitting the front pin.
  • Pabrai's IPSCO at $45/share had $15 cash and hard contracts for $15/share earnings for two years — the plant came free; he made 3x.
Pull quote
“If you never shoot yourself in the foot, consistently above average compounds to extraordinary results.”— Howard Marks
High conviction PRINCIPLES 26, 28-29 · GET OUT OF YOUR OWN WAYfinancemindset

Don't cut the flowers, don't drain your best strategy, automate yourself out

The system is rarely the problem; the person overriding it is.

  • Pabrai sold Frontline early; it went on to 80x as rates ran from $15k to $300k per day. Munger held Costco on business quality over valuation.
  • Fix: a "never sell" bucket where reviews trigger on business deterioration, not on valuation.
  • Camillo compounded ~75%/yr but moved gains yearly into VC returning 10-12%; left alone, he'd be approaching $1B.
  • His lesson: most people diversify out of discomfort, not logic — diversification is a feature, diversifying away from your edge is a bug.
  • Haralabos Voulgaris saw his betting edge vanish on bad personal days, so he fully automated with no override — even through bad Oklahoma City totals.
  • Write the rules on a calm day, automate contributions and rebalancing, and add a 30-day wait for any change made in volatility.
Pull quote
“My method works, but it only works if my mindset is good. If I'm upset, I'm making bad decisions. I wanted to automate me, basically.”— Haralabos Voulgaris

High conviction PRINCIPLES 30-31 · VOLATILITY AND FEARfinancemindset

Volatility is a buying tool, and certainty never arrives at a cheap price

If conviction in the business hasn't changed, a lower price is strictly better; volatility only hurts if you react to it.

  • Cathie Wood: ARK let Tesla run from $100 to $500 (13-14% of the portfolio), trimmed, and bought back on Elon or macro pullbacks; it rarely left the top slot.
  • Wood bought Nvidia at 20 cents (adjusted) in 2014 as a "PC gaming chip" company, trimmed after ChatGPT, and rotated into Coinbase while the SEC was suing it.
  • Marks: certainty and cheap prices don't exist at the same time — and you can't raise money during a crisis.
  • He asked for $3.5B in early 2007 and got $8B; with 12% deployed when Lehman fell in Sept 2008, he invested $450M a week for 15 weeks.
  • Pre-commit now: for every high-conviction holding, the add price, the trim price and the drawdown at which you buy more.
Pull quote
“If I invest it and the world melts down, it doesn't matter what we did. But if I don't invest in it and the world doesn't melt down, we didn't do our job.”— Howard Marks

Red flag PRINCIPLES 32-35 · COMMON TRAPSfinance

Cheap junk, fake diversification, closet indexers and advisors who work for themselves

The traps look prudent — a discount, a spread of assets, a pro manager — and quietly cost the most.

  • Braziel: a 90% discount on a bad asset is still a bad asset (10 cents for something once worth $200M). A great business in a bad situation is the opportunity; a bad business in one is the trap.
  • His test: with zero debt and no legal problems, would you want to own it? If not, the discount is irrelevant.
  • Galloway: in a panic, institutions sell everything and "diversified" portfolios correlate; he's watched concentrated bets blow up twice. Starters for real decorrelation: real estate, commodities, international bonds.
  • Ray Dalio's version: 12 genuinely uncorrelated positive income streams. Galloway now spans US and international stocks, multi-country real estate and private deals.
  • Cathie Wood: benchmarking turned active funds into expensive index huggers — being underweight a stock counts as being short it. Compare your fund's top 10 to the S&P 500.
  • Moiz Ali: a wealth manager's job is keeping money under management, not growing it; he fired Goldman Sachs and others for VOO plus short-term US treasuries.
  • His tests: ask how much of their own money sits in what they recommend, and multiply your annual fees by 20 years. Chasing an extra 1% of yield isn't worth it.
Pull quote
“The pendulum swung toward passive and benchmark-sensitive investing. If you go passive, it's a self-fulfilling prophecy.”— Cathie Wood

High conviction PLAYS YOU CAN STEAL · ONE FINAL RULEfinance

A few decisions make a lifetime: score stakeholders, then size up the rare great idea

Most investments end up average; a handful are extraordinary, so stop sizing every idea equally.

  • Ryan Petersen (Flexport) grades six stakeholders; any F-grade one is a powder keg that eventually surfaces — and in almost any company someone's losing.
  • His Airbnb scorecard: guests, hosts, investors and employees happy, regulators lukewarm, community the weak score (party houses next door).
  • Buffett ran Berkshire 58 years; 12 decisions moved the needle — one great idea every five years.
  • Sheel Mohnot's fund has deployed ~$450M across hundreds of companies; nearly all of its target $2B return should come from ~10.
  • Camillo knows investors whose only big win was buying Tesla in the late 2010s after riding in one — enough for a top 1% record over 40 years.
  • Treating great opportunities as rare, and waiting for them, is what separates great investors from busy ones.
Pull quote
“You really only need one great trade to be a top 1% investor over the course of your lifetime.”— Chris Camillo

Contested READ ACROSS ALL 35 PRINCIPLESfinancemindset

Where the guide argues with itself, and the rule that reconciles it

Five pairs of principles point opposite ways; together they describe one system: an untouched default plus a few rare, pre-defined, large moves.

  • Index vs concentrate: 99%+ should index (Pabrai, Sam Parr), yet one great trade makes a top 1% investor (Camillo, Buffett's 12 decisions). Reconciled by Plan A/Plan B.
  • Diversify vs stay in your edge: Galloway and Dalio want 12 uncorrelated streams; Camillo calls diversifying away from a 75% strategy the leak that cost him ~$1B.
  • Sell vs hold: Frontline is both the risk-vs-uncertainty showcase (80% in 8 months) and Pabrai's "cut the flowers" mistake (it went 80x).
  • Cheap vs junk: "the best asset class is distressed" (Galloway) vs "cheap isn't enough" (Braziel); the steak — known recovery value — is what separates them.
  • Act vs sit: Marks deploying $450M a week and Wood trading Tesla swings vs Braziel's sit-on-your-hands and Voulgaris's no-override rule. The action that works is pre-committed, not reactive.

What to do with this

Best Actionable Takeaways

📈
Markets: Automate index or Berkshire contributions as the default and keep stock-picking to a small entertainment budget.
🧾
Edge: Circle the expenses you'd hate to lose in your last three months of spend; check which are investable.
🔍
Research: Read the one primary document (10-K, prospectus, filings) behind every position you hold.
📝
Discipline: Write your add, trim and crash-buy prices now, plus a 30-day wait before any emotional change.
🛡
Risk: Size every position so its gutter scenario (zero or -80%) can't end your game.
🚨
Holdings: Sell when a threatened company starts defending margins instead of shipping toward the threat — not when it looks expensive.

Names and terms at a glance

Glossary & Entities

Plan A / Plan B — Pabrai: Plan B is the default compounder (Berkshire class B); Plan A is a rare obvious bet funded by peeling off 10-15% of it.
Inch wide, mile deep — A tiny circle of competence known better than anyone else — John Arrillaga's real estate near Stanford.
Circle of competence — The industries and models you genuinely understand; the guide says map it, then narrow to 1-2 areas.
Risk vs uncertainty — Uncertainty is not knowing what happens; risk is permanent loss of capital — the gap is the mispricing.
Non-recourse debt — Debt tied to a specific asset (each Frontline ship), so a default can't reach the rest of the company.
Steak and sizzle — In distressed trades, buy the known recovery (steak) cheaply enough that the upside (sizzle) is free.
Auction-driven markets — Pabrai's term for public markets, where panic sellers repeatedly misprice companies, unlike negotiated private deals.
Dry powder — Capital raised and held before a crisis — Marks's $11B, deployed at $450M a week after Lehman.
Poor Man's Guide to Market Assessment — Marks's checklist for where you are in the cycle: ease of deals, investing TV, stock talk at parties, stories of getting rich.
Infinite game — A game with no finish line where the goal is to stay in; judged by any single year, investors abandon working strategies.
Strategic mediocrity — PIMCO's stance of never chasing top-half annual results, yielding top-decile results per decade.
Gutter scenario — Spier's pre-trade check: what a zero or -80% outcome would do to the whole portfolio.
Canal Street vs Madison Avenue — Braziel: buy real assets temporarily discounted, never fake assets because they're cheap.
Six-stakeholder score — Ryan Petersen grades every stakeholder (for Airbnb: guests, hosts, investors, employees, regulators, community); any F is accumulating risk.
Closet indexing — An active fund charging active fees while hugging its benchmark.
Power law — In venture, a few winners return the fund — Sheel Mohnot expects ~10 companies to drive a $2B target.
