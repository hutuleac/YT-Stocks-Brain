The one-paragraph takeaway

Video Snapshot

  1. Bill Perkins (author of Die with Zero) made his money trading natural gas derivatives, started as a NYMEX floor clerk, moved to Houston in 1995 when deregulation hit.
  2. His core reframe: net worth peaks at a date, not a number. The body is a decaying asset, so the ability to convert money into experiences falls toward zero near the deathbed.
  3. Most people skip risk because of ego, not money. They can survive the job loss but not the status drop.
  4. Time buckets, not a bucket list. Experiences belong to specific life periods and don't transfer; life is Tetris, order decides the score.
  5. Money, health and time are the three tools; he admits he's currently "screwing over future Bill" on health (16-18% body fat, VO2 max mid-range).
  6. He paid $15M for Barnes's Sugar Shack, framing it as an arbitrage between market price and cultural worth, in a live-auction bidding war.
  7. Now runs two startups (SkyFi, Simax) and still trades, while planning a Japan stint and a "large garden" farm life.
Personal story NATURAL GAS DERIVATIVES · HOUSTON, 1995financecareer

From NYMEX clerk to a trader who sells insurance, not predictions

Perkins treats trading as writing insurance: don't blow up, and the edge compounds.

  • Path: NYMEX floor clerk, then OTC broker (six figures, hated it), then trader in Houston after 1995 deregulation. Analogy: broker = real estate agent; trader = the guy who buys and sells houses.
  • Typically short natural gas: the winter risk premium usually bled out, so most of his money came from being short over time.
  • Edge = temperament + informational edge, not arbitrage. Needs a stoic temperament: "your prefrontal cortex will not work while your amygdala is going crazy."
  • Swings of down 50% to up 250% on roughly $100M-$250M. Almost lost it all a couple of times; "the name of the game is to stay in the game."
  • Earned close to $1B in profit for the firm he worked at, but kept too little of his own money in the fund. Hindsight: should have concentrated when young.
  • Hindsight on career: would have gone into equities: far more capital, big funds charging 1 and 1.5 where "your maintenance becomes a profit center."
Pull quote
“We're kind of glorified insurance agents. We don't want to trade. We get induced to trade based on what the market is doing versus what we perceive for fair value.”— Bill Perkins
Worth noting
Perkins still runs the hedge fund and says his fund is "to get rich, not stay rich" (concentration risk), so his risk framing comes from someone who sells that product.
Framework DIE WITH ZERO · CORE THESISmindsethealth

Your net worth peak is a date, not a number

Money converts into fulfillment less and less as the body decays, so the age to spend matters more than the amount.

  • Started with "millionaire before 30," no cap number; later saw the answer is a date: a slope that picks up between 30 and 65.
  • Trigger was a stack of inputs, not one epiphany: the book Your Money or Your Life (money = hours of your life), a college friend refusing the "white picket fence," thinking about death.
  • Money, health and time are the three tools to allocate for maximum fulfillment; the purpose of life is "to be fulfilled according to your own values."
  • The well parable (from the book): people pump water their whole life, sip a little, and die thirsty.
  • Worry hierarchy: you can make more money later, not more time. "I fear wasting my life," not losing money.
  • Title logic: Die with Zero is also die with zero regrets; his regrets are all versions of "I didn't do my best."
Pull quote
“Your net worth peak is actually a date, not a number.”— Bill Perkins
Counterintuitive take PERSONAL PRACTICE · ONGOINGmindset

Ego, not money, is why people don't take risks; most preferences are programming

Crush your ego, audit your inherited wants, and own your part of every story.

  • People skip businesses and opportunities because they can't take the status drop, not the money risk; "they're just chicken" and often root quietly for you to fail.
  • Awareness isn't action; his analogy: alcoholics know they're alcoholics. Ego slips still happen monthly (his wife flags him as "an asshole" about once a month, usually when forcing to be right).
  • Most wants are programming: his example is fried crickets, repulsive only because he didn't grow up in Asia. Keep what works, replace what doesn't.
  • Method: ask of each stimulus-response pair "does it work for my goals?" rather than right/wrong.
  • Common trait of winners (hosts' view after ~1,000 episodes): internal locus of control. Perkins calls it maximum accountability.
  • Exercise (via Daniel Negreanu's self-help seminar, which Perkins also did): rewrite a story where you were wronged, with 100% ownership of your part.

“I'm the type of guy who will tell you like you got a booger hanging out of your nose and some people won't.”— Bill Perkins
Framework PLANNING METHODfamilyparentinghealth

Time buckets, not bucket lists: order the experiences of your life

Each life period has experiences that only fit there, so sequencing decides fulfillment.

  • You don't have one death, you have many: the college you, the single you, the young-kids you each "died." Experiences don't transfer between periods.
  • Analogy: life is Tetris: skiing 500 times with friends belongs early; push it late and you get neither.
  • Method: 3-5 year brackets, decide what belongs in each; life is discovery so you refine as you go. He and his wife plan around son Kai being under five, targeting living in Japan.
  • Balance is a pendulum, not an even pie: grind, vacation, party, spiritual work, in swings.
  • Shaan carves out three days a year (~1% of the year to plan the other 99%).
  • Health version: his doctor's Centurion Olympics - pick ~10 of ~50 activities for your last decade (e.g. wake surfing); VO2 max is the best indicator. He computes 80% of his are out of reach on today's trajectory.
  • Current gap: 16-18% body fat vs 8% four years ago, 11-month-old at home. Fix: a trainer who travels with him, "outsource accountability."
Pull quote
“Life is like Tetris. You kind of have to get the order right in order to get the high score and fulfillment.”— Bill Perkins
Recommendation READER STORIESfamilyrelationships

What readers actually change: inheritance, retirement dates, shared vacations

Giving early and spending on shared time beats a bequest at death.

  • Most common story: rethinking inheritance ("it shouldn't be a bequest, they're 60") and taking estate planning seriously.
  • Others moved retirement dates, or told parents to retire so their job is hanging out with grandchildren.
  • Sam's version: bought-in family on three options for in-laws (give now / spend on yourself / spend on something everyone enjoys); they bought a bigger vacation home.
  • Non-wealthy cases: a military member with a guaranteed pension asking why save a lump of cash; others just reallocate time (school drives, reading to kids).
  • Counterweight: fear of running out in retirement still exists; his answer is a large dose of "enjoy it now."
Personal story $15M AUCTION · SOTHEBY'Sfinancemindset

Sugar Shack: buying art as a cultural arbitrage

Perkins paid $15M for a painting he'd loved since childhood and calls it an arbitrage between market price and cultural worth.

  • Painting: Barnes's Sugar Shack (black joy in the segregated South); a copy of it featured on Marvin Gaye's After the Dance and in the Good Times credits. He first tried to buy the second copy from Eddie Murphy and was ignored.
  • Fellow collector John Arnold flagged the sale. First live auction he'd ever attended; estimate was in the hundreds of thousands, he expected to pay $3-5M.
  • Bidding turned into a "trading pit" between him and one art consultant for an unnamed buyer, ending at $15M total. The rival said "I'm not going to stop"; Perkins replied "then I'm going to make you pay."
  • Thesis: museums historically refused to collect African-American art; the stigma persists, creating a gap between what should be preserved and what the market pays. A collector's role is to signal what's worth preserving.
  • Result: his purchase reset the Barnes market; a lesser piece he wanted went from a few hundred thousand to ~$2M. Painting is now on loan to museums (kids vetoed hanging it at home).
  • Hosts' aside: Stallone bought Monet's Haystacks for ~$110K in the 1980s, sold for ~$3M, and it later sold for ~$130M.
Pull quote
“The role of the collector is to signal to future generations what is worth preserving.”— Bill Perkins
Worth noting
The "arbitrage" and market-reset claims are Perkins's own framing as the buyer, and he acknowledges he doesn't expect to close the gap in his lifetime.

Names and terms at a glance

Glossary & Entities

Die with Zero — Perkins's book thesis: use up your resources on experiences before death, ending with zero money and zero regrets.
Time bucket — A 3-5 year life bracket in which specific experiences are optimal and don't transfer to later periods.
Centurion Olympics — His doctor's exercise of choosing ~10 activities for your final decade and projecting the VO2 max needed to do them.
Future Bill — His shorthand for the later self whose health and time you borrow from when you neglect them now.
