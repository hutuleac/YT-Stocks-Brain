Framework HIS CORE IDEA-SOURCING METHODmindsetfinance

Bet on trends that run 10 years, then hunt the second-order winner

Building an important company takes ten years, so a two-year hype cycle or a trend that started five years ago is already too late.

  • Whiteboard exercise: list 20 trends you believe will run 10 years, then ask what will exist that doesn't today. Tim admits he skips the second step.
  • Bets that paid: internet advertising (DoubleClick), unstructured databases (MongoDB, a 20-year trend), nuclear (2.5 years ago), the creator economy, value-based care. Psychedelics is five years into a 10-year trend.
  • Second-order test: if Shopify wins, who supplies it? The picks-and-shovels sellers to data centers are doing very well.
  • Miss: in 2003 video cost ~$10 per thousand views to serve against ~$1 in ads. He saw the lines crossing by 2005-06 but didn't start YouTube, now worth ~$300B.
  • Longevity is a trend he believes in but has no product idea for. Same with e-commerce: 'it's solved' — delivery in 27 seconds, so there's nothing left to fix.
  • Business crush: if an idea still obsesses him after two weeks, he does it — no business model, because nobody knows the numbers yet.
  • Tim's version: he became Shopify's first adviser in 2008-09 at ~10 employees, betting only that e-commerce, mobile and broadband would keep growing.

Playbook DOUBLECLICK, BUSINESS INSIDER, GILT, MONGODBcareersoftware

Start narrow, obsess over product, decide faster than competitors

Every success started with one thing done well and expanded vertical by vertical; speed and product beat finance, marketing and exit planning.

  • DoubleClick: he joined as the 10th-12th employee and opened 25 countries in three years vs competitors' six, 20 of them before the first was profitable. Microsoft and Procter & Gamble followed, then the smaller players. IPO came 24 months in; independent today he says it would be worth ~$100B.
  • Business Insider began with three people covering only New York tech and grew to 600 journalists, six of them on defense. It tested four headlines for five minutes each and never ran an ad.
  • Gilt started with one women's-clothing sale a week, then added men's, kids, travel and home. Revenue hit $175M in year two and $500M in year four.
  • Early AlleyCorp model: he and Dwight Merriman put ~$500k each into six companies, built and launched each for ~a year, then raised from 20-25 VCs. Three became huge.
  • MongoDB had no revenue for 3.5 years — 'don't do that'. A database is like a pacemaker: nobody wants to go first. It did $40M after 8 years and is now worth ~$30B.
  • Uphill failure: a 'Shopify for healthcare' (HIPAA-compliant scheduling) took $6M and was shut down. He still doesn't know if it was execution or timing.

Sell the falling knife, trim a third at the markup, chase the 20 not the 2

Kevin sells when the market turns against a company, takes partial liquidity in big rounds, and keeps funds small enough to build companies rather than gather assets.

  • Gilt got commoditized once brands like Marc Jacobs built their own sites, Macy's fixed its site and Farfetch arrived. Theory had 20,000 items to clear; Gilt could buy 1,000.
  • Valued at $1B and hoping for $400M, he called it a falling knife and sold to Saks for $250M. Three years later they offered it back for $5M. He hasn't touched e-commerce since.
  • New rule: a small fund should sell about a third of a position in rounds four or five years in. Over the next year AlleyCorp will do that.
  • Personally he took money off the table via the QSBS small-business exemption: $10M tax-free.
  • Fund I will be at ~60% IRR at quarter-end, his family office too; none of their three funds is below 50%. There are 40-50 LPs (he's the largest) and 23-24 staff.
  • He won't raise a mega-fund: under 2-and-20, big funds focus on the 2% fee and AlleyCorp focuses on the 20% carry. It will grow only by adding verticals or geographies.
  • Public holdings: no fixed policy. He didn't sell MongoDB for the first year or two, then sold slowly as it rose.

High conviction VALAR ATOMICS: $20M → $6Benergyroboticsspace

Energy, robots and satellites beat the 165th soft drink

Deep tech has grown most at AlleyCorp because real 10-100x improvements in energy and robotics mean billions in contracts, while consumer apps are easy to clone.

  • Valar Atomics (small modular reactors), sourced by his deep-tech team, was backed at a $20M valuation under three years ago. It just raised from Sequoia at $6B.
  • Nuclear 'wasn't hot' then; fusion was. Not always a Trump fan, he says the administration is right on nuclear and psychedelics. France has run ~70% on nuclear for 50 years.
  • He met New York's governor three weeks ago about bringing nuclear to the state to cut energy costs and power data centers.
  • Tim's VC friend says early-stage venture is dead — he cloned Twitter in 12 hours by vibe coding. Kevin: with 164 soft drinks, the odds of a new one standing out are slim.
  • Robots: 100 to 10,000x more in 10 years. Winners are single-use and vertical (textile and car factories); restaurants are harder, which Travis Kalanick is industrializing.
  • Robot massage (an AlleyCorp investment) is '70% of the way': 17,000 unfilled massage jobs, and 25% of people prefer a robot. Portal moves satellites, a second-order bet on 10x more satellites.
  • An AI support agent noticed after 15 seconds that a caller struggled with English, switched to Spanish and solved her medical issue. 'It's coming so fast.'

Methylone: the gentler MDMA cousin that sold for $700M upfront

Pollan's book flipped Kevin at 54; the for-profit Transcend got methylone through trials and sold to Otsuka, and its public-benefit pledge now funds the field.

  • Michael Pollan's How to Change Your Mind (an NYT top-10 book of 2017) changed his mind. He became one of the biggest donors to the Yale Center for Psychedelic Research (John Krystal, Ben Kelmendi).
  • Each compound costs ~$200-250M to get through the FDA, too much for nonprofits. So in 2021 Kevin, Ben Kelmendi and Blake Mandel started Transcend. It holds a 20-year patent for PTSD, depression and anxiety but started with PTSD only.
  • Versus MDMA: dosable weekly (MDMA depletes serotonin; Shulgin said more than four times a year 'loses its magic'), less comedown, and a session length that fits healthcare.
  • Raised during the psychedelics 'desert period' two years ago. Industry mistakes they avoided: underestimating the cost of 4-6-hour supervised sessions, and bundling psychotherapy the FDA couldn't evaluate. 'The second mouse gets the cheese.'
  • Trials were 60% women: the top cause of PTSD is sexual assault, though the industry focused on veterans for political reasons.
  • As a public benefit corp, the original shareholders give 10% of gains. Expect $20M in the next 9 months as ~20 real grants (not 500), likely making them the field's largest donor for a year. Run by Nicholas, informed by PSFC.
  • Not even 0.5% of people with depression or PTSD have access, so cost is the bottleneck. He bets group therapy works as well or better, as AA shows.

Practice 8-10 WEEKS OFF A YEAR, NEVER BURNED OUTmindsetfamily

Three priorities, long vacations and curated rooms of smart people

Kevin protects work, family and fitness, cuts everything else, and builds trips and events that force good conversations.

  • Building DoubleClick, he kept only work, family (school runs) and fitness and cut the rest by ~80%: friends, culture, Sunday sports.
  • He tells CEOs to take 4-5 weeks of vacation and takes 8-10 weeks himself. He still works 2-3 hours a day, e.g. ski or bike in France, then 3pm-8:30pm New York meetings.
  • Four events: Deep Tech New York (450 people), Digital Health New York, DOC (longevity, Napa) and Odyssey (100 people, ideas only). He's taking 20 people to China to ask 'what does China do better?'
  • Trip design: 30-person Switzerland hike, 4-hour morning hikes, ~10 afternoon talks, assigned seating the first two nights only. His 8-person bike trip (Douro Valley) has each rider lead a 20-minute meal topic.
  • Ping pong: beat his school and region, lost at state level, then trained with Musa (14th at the 1992 Olympics). He still plays Yale varsity and his 28-year-old son.
Pull quote
“I've never burned out at all because I paced myself”— Kevin Ryan
Contrarian view HIS BILLBOARD MESSAGEpolicymacro-rates

Startups are fine; inequality and closed borders are the threat

Kevin worries less about tech than about mobility: tax the wealthy at 30%, shrink the deficit, retrain workers, and admit a million or two skilled immigrants.

  • Upward mobility has statistically diminished. With AI job losses coming, families who think their kids won't do better drift to the far right or far left.
  • Levers: wealthy paying 30% taxes, a smaller budget deficit to lower interest rates for everyone, and professional training and retraining.
  • Immigration: 'a million, 2 million' a year, not 100 million. AI PhDs earning $500k start companies that employ 5,000. Immigrants from Haiti, Nigeria and Kosovo 'do great'.
  • Yale is losing academics to Oxford, Cambridge and Canada. The Premier League is the best league because it recruits Norwegians, Egyptians and Ghanaians.
  • New York is booming — Europeans moving in, the Knicks, World Cup and US Open summer. Its two most valuable tech companies had immigrant founders, including Datadog (French).
  • He spends a lot of time abroad: the US is 'crushing it' in future industries, but China is doing a lot of great things.

Names and terms at a glance

Glossary & Entities

Second-order effect — The supplier, complement or behavior change a trend creates, beyond the trend's obvious winner.
Value-based care — A payment model where providers share in the savings when they cut cost while keeping outcomes.
QSBS — US qualified small business stock exemption: up to $10M of gains on qualifying startup shares tax-free.
2-and-20 — The standard VC fee: 2% of assets a year plus 20% of profits (carry).
Public benefit corporation — A for-profit company with a stated public mission written into its charter.
Methylone — An MDMA-related compound with shorter, gentler effects, developed by Transcend for PTSD.
