Personal story ANONYMOUS RETAIL INVESTOR · ~12-YEAR RECORDfinancemindset

The retired founder who beat the S&P 500 by making 2.9 decisions a year

A near-22% annual return came from almost never trading — deep study, written notes, and buying only when price finally cooperated.

  • Met by Sam Parr in the Hamptons; sold his company for eight figures, now invests in public stocks full-time.
  • Sam exported the brokerage account into Claude to count decisions (any buy or sell): 2.9 a year, max 6, zero in 3 of the last 10 years.
  • Annual return 21.8% vs 13.5% for the S&P 500 since 2014; beat the market 11 of 12 years, lagging only in 2022.
  • Longest idle stretch: 2022 to 2025. A $10M start would be roughly $60M now, depending on entry month.
  • ~15 stocks over a decade, give or take five — not obscure names: a large travel stock and a popular clothing brand 'like a Lululemon' (he wasn't one).
  • His process: study a few understandable companies very deeply, keep detailed notes, read annual reports, wait. One he researched for six months before COVID, judged too expensive, and bought the day it got cheap.

“I study a few things that I can understand and I study them very deeply. I keep very detailed notes and then I just wait for an opportunity.”— the investor, via Sam Parr

Framework FOUR PATTERNS FROM SAM'S GUEST LISTmindsetcareer

Few decisions, ignore the reversible ones, stay in your circle, write a premortem

Sam's read on the people he admires: they decide rarely, triage ruthlessly, say no outside their competence, and write the failure down in advance.

  • Few decisions: Buffett calls his strategy 'lethargy bordering on sloth'; Kevin Ryan, whose startup lab launched multi-billion-dollar companies, says he gets one good idea a year.
  • Reversible vs not: don't agonize over reversible calls. Shaan's default is 'I don't care' — everything is ruled out until it earns attention; Sam's neurotic default is the opposite ('the way you do one thing is the way you do everything').
  • Circle of competence: a friend passed on Sam's pitch not because it was bad but because 'I don't know anything about that industry.'
  • Write it down: premortems, journals, arguments for and against — and avoid resulting: judge the decision by its inputs, not its outcome.
  • Jack Dorsey wrote '140 reasons why Square will fail' before starting it — Shaan's example of humbling yourself after Twitter (Munger: 'tell me where I'm going to die so I don't go there').
  • Two leadership styles: Cornelius Vanderbilt, 'the Elon Musk of the 1800s', decided in secrecy and was proven right months later; many top leaders instead gather opinions — Sam finds people 100x more successful asking for his.
  • Dunning-Kruger: blue belts in jiu-jitsu feel like black belts and 20-year black belts call themselves beginners; the smartest admit what they don't know and how much luck mattered.
Counterintuitive take META'S AI PIVOT · ALEX WANGmindsetai-infra

Zuckerberg's rule: you're judged only by what you got right — and how right

Investing punishes mistakes; entrepreneurship forgives them if your one correct swing is big enough to wipe out the losses.

  • Shaan: the metaverse bet (~$60 billion, by his recollection 10x what was spent developing the iPhone) produced nothing iPhone-like; Zuckerberg then correctly pivoted to AI.
  • Zuckerberg's line, per Shaan: you don't get judged by your mistakes, only by the things you got right and how right. Contrast: Buffett's 'rule number one, don't lose money.'
  • Bezos' baseball analogy: the best hit yields four runs; in business one correct swing can yield a thousand runs.
  • Zuckerberg is on a victory lap as Meta's Muse model does well — Shaan calls it premature since the AI leader changes every ~6 months, though 'the stock agrees' so far.
  • Alex Wang: math-olympiad kid, founded Scale AI (data for AI labs), described as the youngest billionaire; Meta paid roughly $14-15B for a big stake largely to get him.
  • Why Wang, per Zuckerberg: strategic mind, clear communication, and conviction on talent density — 20, at most 200, extremely smart people in a flat team, not 2,000 pretty smart ones.
  • Wang on Zuckerberg: replies to a WhatsApp question with blog-post-length clarity, and texts ~20 smart people outside his board asking 'what are we getting wrong?' — partly to test who gives high signal.

Framework SHAAN PURI · BOOK: THE SCOREmindsetcareer

Most people, even very successful ones, can't name the game they're playing

Pick the game consciously, because the game decides what you want and who you have to become to win it.

  • Sam's executive coach called him harshly self-critical; Sam blames hanging out with 'literally the 30th richest person on earth'. Shaan's cure for comparison: get comfortable in your own game.
  • Sam asks advice-seekers 'where are you trying to be in 5 years?' — even driven people usually haven't thought about it.
  • Examples: LeBron James at 42, joining Philly to defy aging and catch Michael Jordan; Jaylen Brown, traded from the Celtics, playing a 'prove myself' game; most players just want fun and a good living.
  • Aaron Sorkin's story frame: intention and obstacle. Sam and Shaan's 20s game was 'make it' (become multi-millionaires); their 30s game became family plus success without the grind.
  • Successful people who keep climbing usually feel they won one game and need another 'to feel whole' — and would feel silly saying it out loud.
  • The Score: charades makes you expressive and collaborative, Mafia makes you a 'lying cheating bastard', Twister rewards flexibility, bodybuilding strength. Sam added it to Goodreads.
Pull quote
“If you decide to play a game the game decides what you want and who you need to be to get it.”— Shaan Puri, paraphrasing The Score
Principle MOTIVATION CORNERmindset

Get moving before you know the direction — and keep your sails up while losing

Action creates the plan; small wins reverse a downward spiral faster than strategy does.

  • Elon story (Shaan admits it may be myth): asked in 2008-09 what he'd do if Tesla and SpaceX both failed, he'd rent the cheapest apartment, buy a computer and start building within 24 hours — one night of devastation, no more.
  • Shaan's dad: it's about motion, not direction — changing direction once moving is easy, starting from a standstill is hard.
  • Napoleon, asked for his next plan: 'I act and the plan emerges from my action.'
  • Scott Galloway on the pod: keep your sails up when things are bad, so you're ready when the wind comes.
  • Their failing first company in Boulder: air mattresses, a garbage bag nailed to the wall instead of a trash can — a 'last play' instead of a next play mentality. A Flatirons hike and basic routine (trash can, clean room, good outfit) restored the ideas.
  • Motivation helps before success and after it, much less in the thick of it.
Pull quote
“The thing finds you. You just have to be building when it arrives.”— Elon Musk, as retold by Shaan Puri
Recommendation SAM PARR · BOOK: DAREhealthmindset

DARE got Sam on a plane sober for the first time in 15 years

Panic loses its grip when you invite it, rehearse the real setting in advance, and accept that the thing is going to happen.

  • Book DARE (on panic attacks): Dismiss ('so what'), Allow ('I'm open to it'), Run towards ('I want it to come faster'), Engage (move to a new activity once it peaks).
  • Sam had flown on Xanax for 15 years; read half the book, flew to Jackson Hole drug-free — and the plane's brakes failed on landing, forcing a go-around and a 45-minute diversion to Salt Lake City. He still stayed off it.
  • His claustrophobia work: exposure therapy, using Claude to find the shortest New York subway ride to start with; cruise ships remain his worst fear.
  • George St. Pierre, a fearful fighter who loved training, rehearsed fight day for months: same pre-fight meal, real UFC gloves, his outfit, the opponent's walkout music, octagon lights for heat.
  • Sam's application: rehearse a firing, negotiation or hard conversation in the actual room the day before; he now packs his suitcase as fear practice.
  • Shaan: the peak of enjoyment is the anticipation, not the event — so spending dread in advance blunts the real thing. On his skydive at 10,000 ft, acceptance ('I'm hooked to him, it's happening') neutralized ~50% of the fear.
Pull quote
“Instead of like being like I'm trapped in here with you, it was like you're trapped in here with me now.”— Sam Parr
Worth noting
Sam calls DARE 'a woo woo mind thing' that worked for him; a personal result, not a clinical one.
What to do with this

Best Actionable Takeaways

📉
Investing: Count your own decisions per year from your brokerage export — if it's far above ~3, question whether the extra trades earned anything.
📝
Decisions: Write a premortem before any irreversible call; spend no agonizing on the reversible ones.
🎯
Mindset: Name the game you're playing in one sentence, and check where you want to be in 5 years.
🎲
Strategy: Size bets so one correct swing can wipe out the misses — and keep enough chips on the table when it comes.
🧠
Health: Try DARE on a recurring fear, and rehearse dreaded events in the real setting beforehand.

Names and terms at a glance

Glossary & Entities

Premortem — Writing down every way a decision or company could fail before committing to it.
Resulting — Judging a decision by its outcome instead of the quality of its inputs and framework.
Circle of competence — The set of industries or topics you understand well enough to judge; good investors decline everything outside it.
Talent density — The ratio of exceptional people on a team; Wang's case for a few hundred at most on frontier AI.
DARE — Panic-attack method: Dismiss, Allow, Run towards, Engage.
Next play mentality — Sports idea of letting the last bad play not affect the next one.
