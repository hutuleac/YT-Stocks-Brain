Tim's Alzheimer's prevention stack

With no consensus treatment, Tim throws "the kitchen sink" at prevention because the disease process starts in the 30s and 40s.

  • Sauna a few times a week, 20 minutes at 170-180°F; hotter is not better and overcooking the head pushes the wrong way.
  • Norwegian 4x4 intervals (four minutes all-out, three off, four rounds); three times a week for six months reportedly produced durable hippocampal changes out to five years.
  • Zone 2 training, light lifts, and urolithin A (Timeline's Mitopure) for mitochondrial health.
  • 5 mg of over-the-counter lithium orotate; Tommy Wood and Rhonda Patrick are his research touchstones.
  • Environment: dental health, air purifiers, water, and xylitol gum and mouthwash; he tracks heart rate with a Morpheus monitor.
  • A blog post and podcast with the full detail and non-obvious choices are in draft.

Health data, supplements and being your own advocate

More biomarkers don't help unless they change the intervention, and "natural" products carry dosing variance that prescription drugs don't.

  • Quantified self 2.0 must go data, interpretation, action, then behavior change; wearables (he uses Oura) make data trivial, so the gap is the last mile.
  • Example: a creeping hemoglobin A1C trend; an oral glucose tolerance test helps, but the real problem is getting people to act while still in range.
  • Fancy Alzheimer's biomarkers don't change clinical intervention today; at best it is harvest now, decrypt later for retroactive analysis.
  • He was at the first Quantified Self meetup 18 years ago at Kevin Kelly's house; Gary Wolf's new book launched last week.
  • Examin-style tests of 10 melatonin products labeled 3 mg found some with zero and some with up to 20 mg.
  • His family's males die of heart attacks and he is a cholesterol hyperabsorber; he tested the best-studied, most innocuous drug first (ezetimibe, Zetia), turned out a hyper-responder, and skipped three others.
  • Natural is not safe by default: hemlock and arsenic are natural, and he would test multiple batches of any herbal product months apart.
  • Synthetics like LSD or MDMA beat ayahuasca on dose consistency; his first company was expensive because he did the standardizing most skip.

Psychedelics: caution, repeated exposure and what's new

Tim talks more people out of psychedelics than into them, yet credits them with surfacing hidden problems and removing his fear of death.

  • Treat them like spinal surgery: do the homework, measure twice and cut once; he would avoid a rented shaman in a canyon for a first experience.
  • Before them, huge unseen emotional icebergs ran his life, with 17 problems tracing to one or two roots; afterwards it felt like dropping boulders he didn't know he carried.
  • Swimming analogy: he feared water until his 30s, and Terry Laughlin's Total Immersion (dunk your head, glide, repeat) got him to over a mile in the ocean within months.
  • Fear extinction: terminal-cancer patients often lose fear of death after an ego-dissolution experience; he credits psychedelics for his own lack of it.
  • He has cut his frequency sharply as other tools arrived, such as accelerated TMS; too much leaves people stuck in an Alice in Wonderland state.
  • New: methylone, a shorter, gentler MDMA cousin; Transcend Therapeutics (co-founded by Kevin Ryan) aims to slot it into the health system.
  • Sub-perceptual doses of some psychedelics show anti-inflammatory effects, and his pet hypothesis is that chronic neuroinflammation downstream of infection explains many benefits; he funds Chuck Nichols' research.
  • Contraindicated with a family history of schizophrenia or borderline personality disorder; metabolic psychiatry (keto) is his most interesting alternative, citing Chris Palmer and Dom D'Agostino.

Getting unstuck: cost of inaction and the smallest next action

People stay stuck when the pain isn't painful enough, so Tim makes inaction vivid in writing and then shrinks the next step.

  • Fear-setting's neglected piece: three columns (1, 3, 10 years) listing the physical, financial and relational cost if nothing changes.
  • Change usually involves less pain and risk, and it is more reversible, than the status quo.
  • Tools beyond drugs: IFS (Dick Schwartz), CBT, DBT, and Atomic Habits; psychedelics can help you watch your life like a film.
  • Intellectual collector to builder: watch Casey Neistat on shipping on a schedule; take the smallest next action and rebuild identity around it.
  • For AI 12 months out, he'd do more defining and eliminating: the 80/20 Principle by Richard Koch and The Effective Executive by Peter Drucker.
  • Advice is easy to give; translating good intentions into behavior is the hard catalytic step; he has watched addicts and friends need rock bottom.
Pull quote
“the pain has to be painful enough.”— Tim Ferriss
Recommendation NO SOCIAL APPS · UPSIDE TIMEmindsethealth

Staying hopeful: social media fast, upside time and past crises

Remove the passive feed of rage, calendar deliberate optimism and risk study, and use history to rebuild confidence.

  • No social media apps on his phone for about 5 years; a one-to-two month fast is, he says, like twenty years of therapy.
  • Calendar upside time (hunting optimistic cases, with an LLM as the front end to YouTube search) and downside time (risks plus what can be done).
  • Platforms sell your attention to advertisers, and people linger on what upsets them more than on what cheers them.
  • He is reading Hard Times by Studs Terkel, an oral history of the Great Depression showing destitution alongside joy and a jingle writer's burst of creativity.
  • Mike Maples Jr.: teaching kids optimism is a parent's job, and optimism and action feed each other.
  • Metalearning (the 4-Hour Chef section of about 80 pages) builds adaptability, and he test-drives it on rock climbing and portrait drawing in Procreate.
  • Exercise to fix your state; handmade, imperfect work and human connection will likely become more valuable.
  • He says he has never been more at peace, and credits tools, not finances; a favorite gifted book is Awareness by Anthony de Mello.

Counterintuitive take FORMAT EXPERIMENT · ASK FOR IDEAScareerdev-workflow

Interview formats, getting current on AI, and what to build

Tim thinks interview podcasts are exposed to AI and wants audience ideas for lighter formats; for learning AI he says build, don't spend.

  • He calls most interview formats islands in the ocean with rising water; as a canary he cites prescriptive non-fiction book sales after ChatGPT 3.5.
  • The Random Show and Q&A are far less effort than researched guest episodes; he has done co-hosted interviews with Kevin Kelly and Danny Hillis.
  • Viewers can post format ideas to him on X with a format hashtag.
  • To get current on AI, skip the $5-10K: ask the tools for 10 one-to-three-hour projects (he is involved with Obo, built for learning) and compare Claude and ChatGPT answers.
  • Even slower models from six months ago are plenty; he has seen hackers do staggering things with older models.
  • A commenter says Claude Code is mind-bendingly good; Tim agrees on what you can build.

Recommendation POSTURE · FLEXIBILITY · TEETHhealth

Small body habits: posture, flexibility and white teeth

Cheap, repeatable habits beat gadgets: meditation for posture, end-range strength for flexibility, xylitol for teeth.

  • Tech neck: meditate (TM) 20 minutes twice a day in a stacked position; after weeks people complimented his posture.
  • Also a weekly massage ($10-20), self-massage tools for the pectoralis minor, a good chair, and a laptop stand (Roost) so the screen is at eye level.
  • Flexibility: build strength in end ranges per gymnastic strength training with Christopher Sommer, e.g. 20 pike pulses.
  • His teeth: no veneers or whitening strips; he credits xylitol gum and mouthwash bits twice a day, recommended by Tommy Wood, plus a Sonicare.
  • Hugh Jackman's rowing trick (2,000 m in 7 minutes) is elite-level and risky to copy.
Pull quote
