A memory bottleneck keeps the frontier labs neck and neck

No lab can buy 10x more compute than the others while memory is scarce, so capability gaps stay small until that constraint lifts.

  • Supply-chain bottlenecks rotate: packaging a year or two ago, memory now (largely Korean makers: SK Hynix, Samsung, plus Micron), maybe data centers or power next. Memory makers 'didn't quite believe the demand forecasts' and are now catching up via the fab cycle.
  • Result: an artificial ceiling on model size and inference for ~two years, so OpenAI, Anthropic and Google stay roughly close. Once it lifts, one could pull far ahead.
  • Everyone is constrained, including Google with its TPUs and Amazon with its Trainium chips. Months of giant-cloud training produce 'like a flat file', the way ~3-4 billion DNA base pairs specify a whole mind.
  • Revenue ramps keep compressing: per his team's chart (Jared), ADP took ~30 years to reach $1B, OpenAI and Anthropic about a year. Each is rumored near a ~$30B run rate.
  • That's ~0.1% of US GDP each and AI at least ~0.5% of GDP — before Azure, GCP or AWS AI revenue. At $100B in revenue each would be 1-2% of GDP.
  • Market structure: his Substack post about three years ago predicted an oligopoly aligned with the clouds, and that's 'roughly what happened'. Meta and xAI could still change it.

Meta's offers minted an IPO for a whole class of researchers; trillion-dollar buyers can pay $30B

The talent war and mega-cap balance sheets have created a new liquidity regime for both people and AI startups.

  • Meta's aggressive bids — rational given tens of billions in compute spend — forced rivals to match. That gave 50 to a few hundred researchers packages reportedly of tens to hundreds of millions each.
  • Unlike a single company's IPO, the class is spread across Silicon Valley. The only precedent he sees is early crypto holders around 2017. Some will chase big science or AI-for-science; Tim adds vices; and there are Austin's 'Dellionaires'.
  • Deals: xAI got an option to effectively buy Cursor, and Scale was 'sort of partially taken by Meta'.
  • 15 years ago the biggest market cap was ~$300B (Exxon or similar). Now several companies are worth trillions, so '1% of 3 trillion is 30 billion' buys almost anything.
  • The four kinds of buyer: labs or hyperscalers, big tech (Apple, Amazon, Oracle, Samsung, Tesla, SpaceX, Snowflake, Databricks, Stripe, Coinbase), vertical owners like Thomson Reuters, and competitors.
  • Underused option: merge with your rival instead of destroying pricing — X.com and PayPal did. Uber reportedly walked from a Lyft deal and spent more fighting than buying would have cost.

Most AI companies should consider selling soon; the durable few never should

Every tech cycle kills 90-99% of its companies; Elad is 'incredibly bullish' on AI and still thinks many founders are at their value peak now.

  • Precedent: Detroit's dozens of carmakers and hundreds of suppliers collapsed to a few. In the dot-com era ~1,500-2,000 companies went public (about 450 in 1999 and 450 in early 2000), and maybe 'a dozen, two dozen' survived.
  • Every company has a value-maximizing window, usually 6-12 months, before a headwind like commoditization, a lab competing with you, or a technology shift. The tell is the second derivative of growth starting to plateau.
  • Core labs (OpenAI, Anthropic, Google) look durable 'barring some accident or disaster'. For apps (Harvey for legal, Abridge for health, Decagon and Sierra for customer support), he applies four tests.
  • The tests: does your product get dramatically better when the model does? How deep and broad is it, and is it built into workflows? Do you hold proprietary data ('data moats in general are overstated')? Are you the system of record?
  • Adoption is about change management, not technology: the hard part is changing how people work.
  • Lyft's series C, which he passed on, turned on the winner-take-all vs oligopoly question — the same lens he uses now.

Market first, no science projects, and reduce every late-stage deal to one belief

Elad weights markets above teams, avoids science risk, and boils late-stage diligence down to the single insight that separates a 0.5x from a 10x.

  • Market first, team second '90% yes' — he has seen great teams crushed by bad markets. He avoids science projects; Chamath's SPACs 'saved hard tech' by returning money to those funds at the peak.
  • Late stage: every model shows 2-3x because funds underwrite to ~25% IRR. The real job is spotting the 0.5x or the 10x. If you need three beliefs it's too complicated; with none it makes no sense.
  • Examples: Coinbase as an index on crypto, Stripe as an index on e-commerce, Anduril on AI plus drones for defense (with a four-or-five-item defense checklist, including cost-plus vs hardware margins).
  • Heavy diligence anyway: multiple CFO meetings, customer calls, and a cash reconciliation audit he believes no other fund does. But he spares founders the '30 pages of questions that don't matter'.
  • SPVs started when he ran out of his own money following on in Stripe (around series C), then Instacart and Coinbase. He's 'terrified of losing other people's money' and tells fund scouts their picks are a track record, not free options.
  • Power law: a friend's analysis (perhaps Yuri Milner) found ~100 companies drove 90%+ of tech returns since the early 2000s and ~10 drove ~80%. His regrets are about not investing more in winners.
  • Venture changed: four-year vesting dates from the 1970s, when companies went public in four years. After Sarbanes-Oxley, growth investing moved private (DST and Yuri Milner were early); most companies grow on one core thing for a decade.

Why now, real TAM, and the distribution stories nobody tells

AI opened more markets at once than Elad has ever seen. Great companies win on product and aggressive distribution, which origin stories leave out.

  • 'Why now' shifts: regulatory (Samsara entered via required in-cab driver monitoring), technology (language models plug into all white-collar work and code), and incumbent shifts. A security startup now competes with HashiCorp, slowed by its sale to IBM.
  • Robotics differs: even the best model plugs into small existing hardware markets, so there's no instant runway the way language had.
  • Fake TAM: 'global e-commerce is $30 trillion' isn't your market. Real reframing is Coke deciding it competed for share of all liquids, not soda — hence Dasani.
  • Untold distribution: Google paid hundreds of millions a year to bundle its toolbar, Facebook bought ads on people's own names in Europe, ByteDance spent billions distributing TikTok, and Snowflake spent billions on sales. Cursor and WinZip spread by pure word of mouth.
  • Dead dogma: 'selling to law firms is awful' died because AI sells work product, not seats — Harvey sells labor-hour equivalents. 'Fraud will kill you in payments' was the post-PayPal version.
  • Market-limited vs founder-limited: YC says more founders means more giants; Elad stresses open markets. Every CEO now asks 'what's my AI story?', so an AI company without explosive growth is 'fundamentally broken'.
  • Vinod Khosla: your market entry can differ from your disruption — Instagram and Twitter started as toys, and SpaceX entered with launch then disrupted with Starlink. Fears about AI capex ROI are 'probably off'.

Pick board members like in-laws you'll see for a decade

Founders build boards reactively from whoever invested. Elad says write a job spec and accept a slightly lower price for a better board member.

  • Reid Hoffman: at best a board member is 'a co-founder you couldn't hire'. Elad: if your co-founder is your work spouse, board members are your in-laws — and an investor director can't be fired.
  • Naval: 'valuation is temporary but control is forever'. Board control can fire the CEO, so a worse price for a better person is often right.
  • Write a job spec for each seat as you would for any hire; treat the board as a portfolio that changes from early stage to public. Angels are a farm team — at Color, investor Sue Wagner (BlackRock co-founder; boards of Apple, BlackRock, Swiss Re) joined the board.
  • High Growth Handbook (scaling from 10 to 10,000 people, ~7 years old) is a dip-in tactical guide. His next book is the zero-to-one version: first five hires, first round, what to do when someone tries to buy you.
  • Origin myths are revisionist: Sarah didn't dream of accounting software at 3, and Pinterest didn't start with a 5-year-old's collections.

Learning by model and phone call; longevity kept boring

Elad learns through X, papers, 20-minute calls and multi-model research. On health he waits for 'real drugs', while Tim experiments carefully.

  • Inputs: X, papers (more biology now that AI labs publish less), and 20-minute calls with the smartest person on a topic (e.g. Noam Shazeer). He runs the same question through two or three models, demands primary sources, and uses Gemini for travel rankings.
  • An autism/ADHD deep dive suggested diagnoses rose mainly from diagnostic criteria and incentives, not prevalence, and that maternal age mattered more than paternal age in some data. He flags that his numbers are from memory.
  • For fun he uploads founder photos and asks models to cold-read personality from micro-features like crow's feet. He says 'it works pretty well', though he doesn't claim it's predictive.
  • Tim is using Claude Code to analyze 20 years of his own angel investing — who sent good deals, counterfactual holding periods. Elad mostly looks forward, Andreessen-style.
  • Elad's stack: sleep, exercise, eat well, vitamin D and creatine. He's curious about a rapamycin pulse and wants startups for neurosensory aging (eye drops for the weakening lens muscle). He funded a Stanford trial on the cosmetics of aging and calls most peptides cosmetic.
  • Tim (APOE3/3, family Alzheimer's): ketone esters, urolithin A, fasting per Dominic D'Agostino, watching obeticholic acid. He may combine rapamycin pulses with Norwegian 4x4 intervals and track hippocampal volume.
  • Tim on 'reboots': stellate ganglion block, ibogaine flood dosing (Nolan Williams's brain-age data, Howard Lotsof) — 'dangerous stuff'. He chose local over general anesthesia for surgery the day before, and calls bioelectric medicine 'one of the great next frontiers'.

Names and terms at a glance

Glossary & Entities

Personal IPO — Elad's term for a whole class of AI researchers suddenly getting IPO-scale wealth via pay packages.
Inference — Running a trained AI model to produce outputs, as opposed to training it.
SPV — Special purpose vehicle: a one-company investment fund pooling outside money into a single deal.
Second derivative of growth — Whether the growth rate itself is accelerating or flattening — an early warning for a peak.
System of record — The software holding a company's authoritative data, which makes it hard to rip out.
TAM — Total addressable market; 'fake TAM' inflates it with a market you don't actually serve.
Stellate ganglion block — An anesthetic injection near neck nerves explored as a nervous-system 'reset' for PTSD and similar conditions.
