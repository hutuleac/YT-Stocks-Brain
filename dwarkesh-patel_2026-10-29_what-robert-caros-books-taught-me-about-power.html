Video Snapshot

  2. Moses wrote the laws that governed him: one sentence in the 1933 Triborough Act let the authority refinance forever, and bond covenants put his powers out of reach of any legislature.

Moses wrote his power into the bonds

As the best bill drafter in Albany, Moses buried clauses that turned a two-bridge authority into a permanent, constitution-protected empire.

  • Public authorities were meant to repay bonds and dissolve; Moses added that Triborough could "from time to time" refinance with new bonds "for any other corporate purpose".
  • It could build connecting roads and facilities "not inconsistent" with the project, so anything linked to the bridge by a chain of roads was fair game.
  • In 1946 the city could borrow $150M; Triborough could borrow $360M.
  • The Constitution bars laws "impairing the obligation of contracts", so writing toll rights and surpluses into bond contracts made them irrevocable short of a constitutional convention.
  • By 1964 he had built 672 of 899 miles of New York State highways, plus the Triborough and Verrazzano bridges, Jones Beach, Lincoln Center and Shea Stadium.

Why no mayor or governor fired Moses

Law, a dozen posts, popularity, control of federal money and a YIMBY coalition made firing him unthinkable.

  • Commissioners could be removed only on "stated charges after a formal hearing", and he was "money honest", so there were never charges.
  • He held 12 posts at once, appointed by both the mayor and the governor, so no one person could remove him from them all.
  • In his first 4 months as parks commissioner he finished 1,700 park renovations; high schools voted him the most admired New Yorker, and projects finished within one election cycle.
  • With the best planners in the country, New York got 1/7 of national Depression work-relief money by 1936; mayors could build his projects or nothing. The Second Avenue Subway, by contrast, took 1972-2017 and $4.5B for 2 miles.
  • He threatened to resign so often that La Guardia's lawyer printed blank resignation forms, and newspaper-owner friends like the Sulzbergers would have blamed the mayor.
  • A YIMBY graft machine: discounted bonds for banks, jobs for unions, fees for lawyers, insurance commissions for party bosses.
  • Dwarkesh argues the 1970s fall of New York was structural (Detroit and St. Louis fell harder), not mostly Moses's fault.

Johnson's three tactics: leverage, agenda, brokering

Johnson took a job with one formal power (being recognized first) and made himself the second most powerful person in America.

  • Both of his predecessors as majority leader lost their seats; he got the job at 44 in a Senate averaging about 60 because nobody wanted it.
  • Leverage: he controlled floor scheduling, access to Speaker Sam Rayburn, Texas oil money, and committee seats moved from seniority to his steering committee. After Estes Kefauver crossed him in 1957, the coveted Foreign Relations seat went to John F. Kennedy.
  • Agenda: he made unanimous consent agreements the norm and kept bills off the floor until one was in place, so votes were settled in private ("all the action takes place offstage", per Paul Douglas).
  • Brokering: every committee assigned a staffer to report to him, aides tracked every senator, and he worked the cloakroom, so every trade ran through him.
  • 1957 Civil Rights Act: a 7-month deal traded an Idaho-Oregon dam for Western senators, jury trials for striking union workers, and Black citizens' right to serve on federal juries, while keeping the jury-trial clause that stopped a Southern filibuster.
Pull quote
“The real political genius of Lyndon Johnson was identifying and then gaining control over every possible source of leverage over his colleagues.”— Dwarkesh Patel

Idealist turned power-user vs opportunist turned reformer

Power changed Moses and revealed Johnson, Dwarkesh's sharpest contrast between the two.

  • In the 1910s Moses fought Tammany Hall patronage as a good-government reformer; his reforms died, and he learned that ideals are useless without power.
  • Caro: power began as "the servant of his dreams" but is "a drug that creates in the user a need for larger and larger doses".
  • Johnson showed no coherent ideology and molded himself to the Southern Dixiecrats; Herman Talmadge recalled "None indicated" on Johnson's sympathy for Black Americans.
  • As president he drove the 1964 Civil Rights Act and the 1965 Voting Rights Act.
  • Dwarkesh spent six years trying to book Caro: his agent, publisher, archives, even handwritten letters.

Names and terms at a glance

Glossary & Entities

Public authority — A government-created corporation that issues bonds to build a project and repays them from its revenue.
Bond covenant — Terms written into a bond contract; protected from later laws by the Constitution's contracts clause.
Unanimous consent agreement — A Senate deal that caps debate time and amendments on one bill.
YIMBY — "Yes in my backyard": a coalition that benefits from new construction.
