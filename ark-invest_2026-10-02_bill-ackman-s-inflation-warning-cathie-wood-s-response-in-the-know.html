High conviction RESPONSE TO BILL ACKMAN — RATES UP ON REAL GROWTHmacro-rates

Ackman's inflation spiral is a 1970s argument; Wood sees the 1980s instead

Rising rates reflect a stronger real economy, not runaway inflation — the same setup that preceded the 1980s productivity boom.

  • Ackman's tweet: rising interest costs keep feeding inflation in a vicious cycle — an argument Wood says was everywhere in the late 1970s.
  • Her template is the 1980s: productivity lifts real growth while inflation falls, helped then by the PC and software revolution.
  • Today's disinflation drivers: five platforms — AI, robotics, energy storage, blockchain and multiomic sequencing; deflation possible "especially if oil prices crack."
  • Rates since 1790 (built with Nick Grous's 2019 research): the 10-year went from below 1% in COVID to above 5% now — about the historical midpoint.
  • The real anomaly was the 1970s-90s spike: closing the gold window in 1971 after Vietnam and Great Society "guns and butter" spending; then the Fed overcorrected and "spoon fed" markets after 08-09 and COVID.
  • Five-year Treasury: the recent rise is mostly real yield; implied inflation sits steady a bit above 2% and she expects it to break down.
  • Bond market confusion: 40 years of decelerating nominal GDP and falling rates are now reversing — bad for bondholders, good news for growth.

High conviction WATCHING WHAT FED CHAIR WARSH WATCHESmacro-rates

The inflation gauges Warsh watches are already rolling over

Headline PCE still looks high, but private measures, the diffusion index and money velocity all point lower.

  • Headline PCE revised down from 3.7% to 3.4% year over year; core is a bit lower, but Warsh says he's fixated on headline — "a long way from 2%."
  • Truflation (a private measure Warsh also watches): headline up to ~2.7%, largely oil, while core keeps falling toward pre-COVID averages.
  • Fed diffusion index (share of categories rising 0.3% or more): the six-month line is reversing and should pull the annual one down.
  • Money growth ~5.7%, but a four-year view shows low-single-digit M growth not accommodating federal outlays, which are rising mostly on defense.
  • Velocity keeps falling; its closest match is labor participation, which peaked in the late 90s — boomers retiring and immigrants leaving keep it flat.
  • Yield curve couldn't regain 200-300bp steepness even with COVID stimulus; it reached ~75bp and is now in the 40s — Treasuries "sniffing out deflationary undercurrents" (TIPS don't show it yet).
Worth noting
Headline PCE at 3.4% is still well above the 2% target, and the Fed had been talking hawkish until this report.
High conviction FORECAST — 7-8% REAL GDP GROWTH, FIVE YEARS TO 2030ai-inframacro-rates

AI cost collapse: 99.99% a year cheaper, revenue exploding anyway

Falling prices plus exploding volume is "good deflation" — the microcosm Wood expects across the whole economy.

  • Wright's law in genome sequencing: every cumulative doubling cuts cost by 40%.
  • AI inference at fixed performance is getting cheaper by 99.99% per year — far steeper than sequencing.
  • Jevons paradox: OpenAI's annualized run rate went from $20B at end of last year to $70B, past Anthropic's reported $65B for July ("probably neck and neck").
  • Brett Winton's convergence chart: 15 technologies across five platforms; AI now links to blockchain through agentic payments, agentic commerce, Grok bot, Muse and OpenAI's bots.
  • Global real GDP grew ~0.6% a year in 1500-1600 (maybe refined to 0.5%); the industrial revolution took it to 3% — a fivefold jump.
  • ARK's forecast: 7.3% (7-8%) real GDP growth for the five years ending 2030, vs. the IMF's 3% (now ~2.5%); update in Big Ideas, out in January.
  • Productivity growth should reach 5-6%+, replaying the PC, software and wireless upswing of the 80s-90s; corporate profitability could hit all-time highs.

High conviction CALL — DOLLAR BACK TO ALL-TIME HIGHSmacro-ratesgeopolitics

American exceptionalism isn't done: the dollar heads back to its highs

Business-friendly policy and the US-led AI capex boom raise US returns on capital, pulling the dollar up as in the early 1980s.

  • Early-80s analog: tax cuts and deregulation lifted US return on invested capital and the dollar soared.
  • Bloomberg: 90% of global data center financing (by fixed-income metrics) is in the United States.
  • Effective corporate tax rate ~8%, lowest in the world, via 100% first-year depreciation on new factories and data centers — refunds get reinvested.
  • DXY (six currencies) sits near the high end of its range; the Fed's real trade-weighted dollar (26 countries) is near all-time highs.
  • Kalshi DXY market: DXY started near 98 and is now just above 100; bettors always priced above 100, starting at 103-105.
  • A strong dollar is "a powerful anti-inflationary force" since most commodities are priced in dollars.

Gold peaked with Warsh, oil goes to $30-35, Bitcoin breaks from gold

Disciplined money and technology shrink the two assets that thrived on monetary accommodation — while Bitcoin decouples.

  • Gold shows 15-20 years of bad monetary policy; Wood suspects Volcker and Greenspan quietly used the gold price as a policy indicator.
  • Gold peaked the day or day after Trump appointed Kevin Warsh Fed chair and has fallen since; she expects more declines as he imposes a price-rule discipline.
  • Oil has "levitated" for 20-25 years on easy money; 57% of oil goes to transportation, which is shifting to electric (China and Europe first, US following).
  • Oil to the $30-35 range, as in past crises — this time driven by a technology shift instead.
  • S&P-to-oil is still near a high even with oil at $90-100; S&P-to-gold signaled the 1966-1982 bear market and she expects it to reach all-time highs.
  • Bitcoin-to-gold has turned up; correlation since 2019 is 0.1 and recently negative, despite the "digital gold" label.
  • Metals-to-gold hasn't turned, held down by weak China data.

Mixed EMPLOYMENT FRIDAY — MANUFACTURING OUT, HOUSING STUCKmacro-ratesconsumerpolicy

Through the rolling recession: factories recover, housing and autos don't

Inverted curves no longer guarantee recession; the economy rotated through sector slumps without a full one.

  • Industrial-revolution curves were inverted >60% of the time by ~100bp; post-1913 inversions meant recession, but after the Fed's 22-24-fold hike in 16 months only housing, manufacturing, small business and lower-income consumers slumped.
  • Payrolls much weaker than expected, household employment +400k+, flat year over year; unemployment 4.2%, but 9.9% for 16-24 year olds.
  • Sentiment terrible (Michigan, now Conference Board); diesel ~$6.50; hourly earnings +0.1% (3% YoY), likely tech-job mix shift — she expects a labor shortage later.
  • Housing: existing sales "still in a recession," mortgages above 7%, new-home inventory near all-time highs, builders cutting prices; savings rate low.
  • Manufacturing is out of recession; non-defense capex ex-aircraft is booming after a long base since the tech-telecom bust.
  • Trade deficit widening (imports +2.8%, exports -2.2%) — the flip side is a capital surplus and more foreign direct investment.
  • Autos: China builds 30-35M a year but consumption fell from ~30M to ~19M; US sales below 16M in September — Uber/Lyft affordability means secular peak.

Names and terms at a glance

Glossary & Entities

PCE inflation — The Fed's preferred price index; headline includes food and energy, core excludes them.
Truflation — A private, high-frequency inflation index Wood says Warsh also watches.
Diffusion index — Share of inflation categories rising 0.3% or more, a breadth read on price pressure.
Velocity of money — How often money turns over in the economy; falling velocity mutes inflation from money growth.
Wright's law — Costs fall by a fixed percentage for every cumulative doubling of units produced.
Jevons paradox — Cheaper resources drive so much more usage that total spending rises.
Real vs. breakeven yield — A Treasury yield split into inflation-adjusted return and market-implied inflation (from TIPS).
Gold window — Dollar-to-gold convertibility for foreign governments, closed in 1971.
DXY — Dollar index against six major currencies; the Fed's broader real trade-weighted index uses 26.
Rolling recession — Sectors contract in turn (housing, manufacturing, small business) without a whole-economy recession.
Credit default swap (CDS) — Insurance against a borrower's default; rising CDS prices mean credit deteriorating.
