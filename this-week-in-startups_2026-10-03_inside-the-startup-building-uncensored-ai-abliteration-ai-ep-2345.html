Abliteration AI sells unrestricted models to the users frontier labs refuse

The wedge is professionals in cyber, biology research, synthetic data and defense who can't do their jobs on refusal-heavy frontier models.

  • Devin started it at the end of last year on a home GPU cluster, serving the site from his house. He met Jason via Founder University and Launch Festival, and Jason's rule is to 'say yes' to meeting founders.
  • Jason's framing: if OpenAI runs cyber attacks against Hugging Face (white hat or not), Hugging Face can't defend with OpenAI's latest model. Devin adds that big corporations inside the labs' special cyber programs still complain about refusals.
  • Use cases: testing whether you're exposed to a newly published CVE (which the big labs won't help with), and red-teaming AI agents, e.g. checking that no combination of commands gets a bank's voice agent to wire out your money. Startups resell this to big banks and critical infrastructure.
  • Customers began as cyber and red-teaming startups. In the last month Fortune 500 companies in almost every industry reached out, with CISOs asking to test their own systems 'before some outside AI comes in'.
  • Strategy: stay B2B and leave consumer image/video models to others. The goal is to be the trusted brand in a 'wild west' market through audits and relationships with law enforcement. The two co-founders came from the same big-tech company, got into the Speedrun accelerator, and will hire go-to-market and engineering roles soon.

Contested USER-LIABILITY MODEL — POST WHITE HOUSE ACCORDai-infrapolicy

Remove the refusal neurons, re-add policy as a separate model, and put liability on the user

Abliteration surgically edits the parameters behind refusals, then runs a tiny guardrail model in front that each customer configures. Devin calls it an 'inversion of control'.

  • Built on interpretability work: knowledge is localized in clusters of neurons (in a 27B or 32B-parameter model, say), so they find the parameters responsible for refusals and modify as few as possible to avoid hurting performance.
  • Two layers: an unrestricted knowledge model plus a small policy/safety model that runs in milliseconds, so customers can set stricter or looser guardrails per area without adding much latency.
  • Default policies cover CSAM and self-harm. Jason notes every platform since Usenet, Gopher, AOL and early Wikipedia landed on the same fix: show the suicide-hotline number.
  • Liability: the product isn't free, so Stripe signup leaves a paper trail. They provide 'information in, information out' with no agent layer, and Devin's analogy is that the hammer maker isn't liable for an assault. Jason adds that the hardware store and the paper bag aren't either.
  • Jason ties this to last week's White House accord: police yourselves, and courts, government action and civil suits remain the remedies for misuse.

The 'frontier' window is shrinking to months, and the GPU shortage is real

Devin says Chinese open models went from a year behind to about three months, which raises a commoditization question for highly valued labs.

  • Anthropic published a blog on how cyber-capable GLM 5.3 is, especially abliterated versions like Abliteration's. Devin says that's the source of 'some of the outrage in the industry'.
  • He dislikes the term 'frontier models' because it implies a fixed ranking where the window never closes. Rising commoditization risk means users may not tell models apart outside edge cases.
  • On lab hacking stories: 'if it bleeds, it leads'. Flashy hacks double as capability demos, while white-hat work gets ignored. Some of the stories are old incidents reported late.
  • Benchmarks like Exploit Gym reward models for how far they push an exploit (levels 1-4), which competes with a 'stop and tell someone' reward. Devin calls it an industry-wide engineering problem.
  • Devin's theory: labs with huge research headcounts produce people 'looking for things to do'. Jason likens it to driving a Model S at 170 mph on the autobahn: the car is fine until a tire blows.
  • Compute: they outgrew their capacity and users complained it was slow, and partners have since helped. Devin 'used to think that the GPU shortage was fake' until he hit it at scale.
  • Adoption: Devin cites an a16z report that a large share of businesses still haven't adopted AI. Jason sees it in his ~20-person venture firm, where the top 20% of people use 90% of the tokens and the bottom half use none.

Opinion ASK JASON — CALLER MATTHEW KERBUSsoftwarecareer

AI won't kill the billable hour; subscriptions and taste will reshape it

Jason disputes the 'everything goes outcome-based' view: PCs, cloud and the internet never ended hourly billing, and experts will deliver more for less instead.

  • The caller cites a This Week in AI debate, prompted by a Financial Times piece on the AI 'wrecking ball' hitting the billable hour, where a guest and Scott Stevenson predicted outcome-based pricing within a couple of years.
  • Jason: commodity legal work goes away (no attorney needed for a convertible note or a trademark), but a 10-hour research report becomes 1 hour, or stays billed at 10 for a better result. He expects subscriptions to replace the billable hour in many cases.
  • Precedent: design subscriptions used geographic arbitrage, with designers in South America or India at $1-5/hr vs US studios at $50-150/hr. 'Reasonably unlimited', but someone still counts hours.
  • You're paying for ownership: an AI can audit the books, but you want a human who will testify when the shareholder lawsuit comes. A $50,000-per-logo designer or a $50-100K Met Gala dress sells the inefficient experience on purpose.
  • Lon on Rick Rubin (License to Ill, Raising Hell): he isn't an engineer and doesn't touch the equipment, yet he's paid millions for taste. Jason's interior-designer example: $1M of furniture plus 20% markup and a $250,000 fee, $1,000 per square foot. He'd rather have eight hours of sleep and tonkatsu after skiing in Japan.
Pull quote
“I don't buy that the billable hour goes away. I just think humans move upstream and do more important work.”— Jason Calacanis

Speculative ASK JASON — CALLERS BRUNO & JOSHconsumercareer

AI micro-dramas as an IP test lab, and why solo founders still lose to teams

Micro-dramas from Asia went AI-native about six months ago. Jason sees them less as art than as a cheap way to test characters before bigger formats.

  • Format: 1-2 hours of content cut into 30-60 two-minute episodes, 5-6 free, then a subscription. It was live action until ~6 months ago. Now Seedance 2.5 plus agent-driven ComfyUI runs standardized production pipelines.
  • Caller Bruno (12 years in Hollywood, three Academy Award nominations) says ComfyUI is that pipeline: open source, runs locally or in the cloud, and Claude Code can build the workflow. Genres run from sexy dramas for women to medieval Chinese fantasy.
  • Lon pitched a micro-drama to Snap years ago and it passed. AI romance dramas now get hundreds of millions of views on TikTok and YouTube Shorts and in dedicated apps (TikTok's PineDrama, ReelShort), with subscription and ad monetization.
  • Jason: the next Spider-Man could start as a micro-drama, like 1960s Marvel, where Stan Lee and Jack Kirby ran a few issues and moved on if readers didn't bite. Today's AI video is 'junk food' (a Filet-O-Fish). A Toy Story moment is coming, and he bets Jeffrey Katzenberg gets there by pairing talent with AI plus existing tools.
  • Jason is courting ex-Marvel visual lead Andy Park, laid off along with his whole team, to start a company. Comic books are feast or famine: for every Robert Kirkman there are a thousand struggling artists.
  • Solo founders (caller Josh, annotated.com): investors still back three, then two, then one equally strong founder. Teams split the task-switching and carry less quit risk. Exceptions are proven founders like Mark Pincus, Elon Musk, Evan Williams or Travis Kalanick, or a solo founder growing 5-10% week over week who has removed product-market-fit risk.

Names and terms at a glance

Glossary & Entities

Abliteration — Editing the specific model parameters that encode refusals so an open-weight model answers everything, with minimal performance loss.
Inversion of control (guardrails) — Moving safety out of the model weights into a separate, customer-configured policy model.
Red teaming — Simulating a threat actor against your own systems or AI agents to find weaknesses first.
Exploit Gym — A benchmark that rewards models for how far (levels 1-4) they can take an exploit.
Micro-drama — Serialized two-minute episodes with cliffhangers, a few free, then subscription; now largely AI-generated.
ComfyUI — Open-source node-based pipeline tool for AI image/video generation, runnable locally or in the cloud.
