CNN's 'Randomonium' became the internet: lock onto the current thing and never let go

CNN's original plan of wall-to-wall coverage of whatever is most transfixing right now is exactly what social media now does, amplified by McLuhan's two laws.

  • Source: the little-read book Me and Ted Against the World by Reese Schonfeld, the lawyer who wired Ted Turner's first satellite deal. In ~1981 they pitched 24-hour news. Turner wanted 15 hours, assuming nobody watched overnight.
  • Schonfeld's 'Randomonium': at any moment there's one transfixing 'current thing'. Cover it continuously, put whatever fragments you get on air, and apologize later if wrong.
  • The breakthrough was the 1991 Gulf War, with live coverage from Baghdad. CNN spent 20 years chasing a repeat (Lewinsky, OJ) and then became 'something completely different'. The internet (X, YouTube, Twitch) reinvented Randomonium.
  • McLuhan's global village, which he meant as an observation rather than praise: media turns the world into a small town with no privacy. Humans can manage a Dunbar number of ~150 relationships, and the internet hands you 8 billion 'up in your grill'.
  • McLuhan's 'if it's on television, it's a television show': events get squeezed into sitcoms and dramas that end in a moral lesson. Seinfeld's rule, 'no learning, no hugging', stood out because it broke that format.
  • The internet version: 'if it's on the internet, it's a viral social media meme'. Even an alien invasion would become a meme, then a moral panic and a hunt for scapegoats. Thought experiment: Pearl Harbor in 1941 with Twitter.

Framework ~2.5-DAY HALF-LIFE PER CYCLEmindsetpolicy

Every outrage lasts ~2.5 days, and its truth and scale don't matter

A current thing needs outrage, two tribes and an underlying moral fight. Whether it happened, or how many people it hurt, barely changes the reaction.

  • Each viral explosion spikes and then decays with a half-life of about 2.5 days. It's never resolved, only replaced, and a week later nobody remembers it.
  • That's why Andreessen says politics can't be predicted: from April 20 to the November midterms there are 100-120 cycles, so the vote turns on the economy and whatever meme is live that day.
  • The recipe: activate outrage ('the news is called the news, not the importance'). Think wrestling for men, Real Housewives for women, The Apprentice in between. Add West Side Story tribes that attack values, then dox each other and try to get each other fired.
  • Truth doesn't matter, and ambiguity helps because it's easier to argue about. Scale doesn't matter either: one victim can outrage more than 10,000 (Stalin: 'a single death is a tragedy'). Orwell on the Spanish Civil War said the same about atrocities (nuns killed, later My Lai).
  • Jussie Smollett as a case study, now echoed in a Keanu Reeves Apple TV film where Jonah Hill's crisis lawyer staged the attack. The host adds the 'Astronomer CEO/HR' episode as a lighter example.
  • Viral videos start midway. The first one that mattered, Rodney King, began with the beating, and the Central Park birdwatcher clip showed no run-up. People only pull out phones once it's interesting, and trial evidence arrives a year later.
  • René Girard: scapegoating works because people forget it's a cycle. Cancellation hit people for things they 'literally never did'.

Online rage is a safety valve: political violence is at an all-time low, and calm centralized media was the anomaly

Andreessen argues every earlier medium fed physical violence, the past was far more violent than we remember, and 1990-2014 was an artificial lull.

  • Media and violence: posters drove the Spanish Civil War (the test bed for communism and Nazism), radio was the Nazi regime's medium (and FDR's), and TV fueled Vietnam and the riots of the '60s and '70s.
  • His 'moral defense' of social media is that virtual combat absorbs rage: 'if you're sitting at home scrolling social media getting mad, at least you're not out in the street hurting people.'
  • The past was worse than remembered. All in the Family (Norman Lear, Carroll O'Connor's Archie Bunker vs Rob Reiner's 'Meathead') and Family Ties (Michael J. Fox as Alex P. Keaton) both backfired as their conservative foils became the stars.
  • Going back: the 101st Airborne desegregating schools, the Red Scare, the World Wars, the Depression, the Civil War, the Napoleonic Wars and Gutenberg-era violence. Duels were normal for powerful men, and labor strikes met machine guns.
  • On depression: the rise tracks countries with benefits or school accommodations for diagnoses, which he calls a 'statistical artifact'. He concedes things have felt wilder since ~2014.
  • Centralized media peaked around 1970 (one paper per city, three networks, three newsmagazines, two wire services), creating suppressed volatility from the Cold War's end to ~2014.
  • The norm was fragmentation: 10-15 papers per city in the Revolution era. Ben Franklin started a paper to keep his press busy and ran ~15 pseudonymous 'alts' arguing with each other. The 1800 Jefferson-Adams election's slander 'is way beyond' today's.

“The fact that political violence is at like an all-time low in Western society is something that people really don't talk about.”— Marc Andreessen
Worth noting

Ops are everywhere, but calling something 'just an op' is cope

Movements often start as deliberate availability cascades, yet an op only matters if it resonates, and many become real.

  • Timur Kuran (preference falsification) and Cass Sunstein name the viral outrage cycle an availability cascade, and trace each one to an 'availability entrepreneur' who picks the specific victim or issue to focus on.
  • His example is Rosa Parks, a trained activist rather than a random passenger. 'It started as an op', but it unlocked profoundly positive change, so how it started is 'a footnote'.
  • His Machiavellian thought experiment: with a black budget, paid influencers and a bot army, the goal isn't paid posts forever but getting people to actually believe it, so ops aim to become organic. Real movements mix organic and staged parts, and 'that's just an op' is cope when the outcome is real.
  • The regulatory gap: paid shampoo influencers must disclose, and political donations are disclosed, but paying influencers to push a moral or political position is legal 'in the dark'.
  • AI policy: 'a lot of the AI doomers… is basically bought and paid for'. His example is a just-announced free 'influencer camp' fellowship for doomers, funded by MIRI 'and other donors'. 'Is it a conspiracy if it's in plain sight?'
  • Bot farms exist, and an influencer can have a bot army behind them without knowing it. Andreessen admits there are 'probably more ops than I've been willing to acknowledge', but they only work if the cause already resonates.
  • The host's lens is 'emergent collusion': aligned incentives look coordinated. Since joining a16z, people have attributed things to them that they aren't doing.

Legacy media is collapsing into private equity, the barbell is rising, and the first true internet president is still ahead

Trust and ratings for centralized media are in free fall, while short-form video and marathon podcasts both grow. Trump is a hybrid, and the pure internet candidate is coming.

  • Trust in centralized institutions has been falling since ~1970 ('WTF happened in 1971', 55 years) and hits record lows every survey. Cable news, newspapers and magazines are in 'full scale collapse', and non-adapters get 'run by private equity'.
  • Adapters: the New York Times has adapted, CBS acquired the Free Press, and Mark Halperin is the rare legacy-to-new-media bridge (his Trump Bible-verses interview). MTS mixes him with 21-year-old Twitch streamer Theo.
  • The barbell: short-form video (TikTok, Instagram Reels, X) on one end, 3-hour podcasts on the other. Lex Fridman runs up to 7 hours, Balaji did 10, and Rogan and Lex see high completion rates.
  • The rise of substance includes Substack, long-form essays on X, online courses and AI deep research in ChatGPT and Claude that writes '30 pages… basically a textbook'.
  • Practitioner media means people who do the thing explain it, like Andrej Karpathy on AI. Andreessen grew up on Charlie Rose at midnight and needed a DVR.
  • Deepfakes aren't new: he cites the Gulf of Tonkin ('a fish') and says Russiagate turned out to be 'quite an elaborate fake'. In 1960, Kennedy-Nixon radio listeners and TV viewers picked different winners.
  • Internet elections so far: 2008 (Obama fundraising), 2016 (Trump's social media), 2024 (podcasts). Trump is a hybrid who reads TV chyrons on mute and tweets to change the current thing. A 100% online president who ignores TV is coming, 'probably… 32'.

Names and terms at a glance

Glossary & Entities

Randomonium — Reese Schonfeld's founding CNN concept: lock onto the single most transfixing current event and cover it nonstop.
Global village — McLuhan's idea that electronic media turns the world into one small town with no privacy.
Dunbar's number — The ~150 stable relationships a human can maintain.
Availability cascade — Kuran and Sunstein's term for a belief gaining momentum because repetition makes it more mentally available.
Availability entrepreneur — The person who deliberately picks the specific story or victim that kicks off an availability cascade.
Preference falsification — Timur Kuran's term for people publicly stating views they don't privately hold.
Suppressed volatility — Andreessen's label for ~1990-2014, when centralized media muted political conflict.
Chyron — The text crawl at the bottom of a TV news screen.
