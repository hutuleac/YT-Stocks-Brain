Business model MIKE KORTES · NEXA COMP AND ECONOMICSfinancecareer

Paying loan officers 305 bps on a 275 bps loan: where NEXA says the money really comes from

Kortes says NEXA loses money on the loan itself and makes it on volume, payroll and control of large amounts of cash, which rivals can't copy because they don't know the technique.

  • Payout: on a 275 bps deal he pays out 305 bps, 100% plus revenue share. 'It's actually worse than 100% for me.'
  • His claimed edge: 'the more money I pay out in payroll, the more money I make'. Run through commercial banking and the tax code, that's 2.5% of every payroll dollar tax-free, and the same technique adds 57 bps per loan over the rest of the industry.
  • Why NEXA rates are lower: everyone sells the same Fannie/Freddie product, 'there's money in interest rate' more than in fees, and NEXA prices at wholesale because the loan officer gets the money. Retail shops charge more per loan.
  • P&L ledger workaround: if 300 bps is made and the loan officer is contractually owed 2%, the extra 1% goes into a NEXA ledger they can spend to grow their team but can't withdraw.
  • His caricature of a typical brokerage ('Dewey Cheatem & Howe'): loan officer gets 1%, company keeps 2% and covers ~0.5% in expenses, then marks up the rate so it really makes ~4%, undisclosed. NEXA says it discloses 'every penny we make'.
  • Whole brokerages can move over under a DBA, keeping their name, marketing and email. They shed licensing fees, audits, HR, complaints and liability; one person recently brought five brokers in.

Framework SERVICING · RETENTION SPECIALISTS · AI TRIGGERSfinancesoftware

Keep the loan, pay the originator to retain it, and let AI tell them when to call

Servicing gives NEXA's ecosystem the right to watch the borrower, and the originating loan officer gets paid monthly plus the data that leads to the next deal.

  • Legal structure: a separate company (named on air; the captions read 'Evoland') services loans for Fannie, Freddie and Ginnie. It hires the loan officers as 'retention specialists', so they're paid for a separate job, which regulators signed off on. It can't be done inside NEXA itself.
  • Kortes says the data is worth more than the monthly servicing income. Example: a trigger lead fires within minutes when another lender pulls the borrower's credit.
  • Late-payment example: a borrower who is 10 days late gets a soft credit pull. If revolving debt jumped ~$80,000 (say, a remodel), the loan officer pitches rolling it into a HELOC.
  • The rest of the industry routes these signals to underpaid call centers. Kortes says the loan officer 'has the highest retention rate in the business' if you give them the data and pay them.
  • AI layer: it reads social media for home-buying or selling intent ('80 some percent accuracy'), contacts borrowers, tells the loan officer what to say, and times their response. A slow loan officer loses the lead to another rep.
  • Rate-cut playbook: rates are high now and 'going to come down', so loan officers refinance their own borrowers and the loans come back into the same servicing portfolio.

Counterintuitive take KORTES'S OPERATING PHILOSOPHY · TAX CODE · PERKSfinancemindset

'Show me in the law': a seven-figure tax bill turned into a business model

Kortes runs NEXA by what regulators and his attorneys allow, not by industry custom. He says most 'can't do that' rules trace back to bankers protecting their margin.

  • Origin: a big 2018 led to a seven-figure IRS check in 2019. He spent most of 2020 studying the tax code 'to never write a seven figure check to the IRS again' and now reads 'in between the lines of the laws'.
  • His view of the tax code: it's built to reward creating jobs and opportunities, so he aims tax savings at the mortgage and real estate businesses.
  • Jets under Section 179: 'NEXA doesn't pay taxes. I don't pay taxes.' 'You can write a check to the IRS or you can write a check to a guy who owns a jet' — and get a jet.
  • Perks for anyone in the ecosystem, loan officers and BDMs included: jets at owner cost (~$3,000 an hour vs $7,000+ charter), turboprops at ~$900 an hour (Phoenix-Vegas in 1.5 hours either way), a yacht and resort-like homes. He flies the TBMs himself.
  • He doesn't watch competitors' talks at conferences, so he won't start mimicking them: 'the moment I start following somebody else, now I'm chasing.'
  • Regulators, auditors and attorneys back each structure. Bankers who cite compliance are really saying 'I can't make more money' doing it his way.

Structural critique NIL CHECKS · 30-MONTH CONTRACTS · WHY LOAN OFFICERS DON'T MOVEfinancecareer

Loan officers are paid like NIL athletes now; NEXA buys them out instead of locking them in

Rivals sign top producers to 30+ month contracts with clawbacks. NEXA pays off the buyout as a loan forgiven as they produce, so nobody is stuck.

  • Kortes's analogy: like D1 athletes earning NIL money, loan officers now get big checks to join specific companies.
  • Rival deals: leave at 29.5 months of a 30-month contract and 'you got to write the whole million dollars back'. NEXA writes the buyout (e.g. $500,000) as a loan forgiven as they produce.
  • Retention: he reads how much extra a top producer adds to the NEXA ledger and writes them a retention check.
  • Why people don't move: fear, not knowing, and rumors that giving away 100% is 'financially impossible'. His advice to his own loan officers is to keep rivals talking and asking questions.
  • Brad's evidence of the inertia: he invited all loan-officer listeners to a call and 'like 13 of you jumped on'. Weekly 'Why NEXA' call: Thursdays 11:00 Arizona time (whynexa.com).
  • Culture: 'they do say we run a cult over here at Nexa' ('cult and culture'). Loan officers get the CEO's cell phone, which Kortes says should be normal anywhere.

Recommendation 20-HOUR LICENSE · ~$8K A FILE · NO-LICENSE BDM ROLEcareerfinance

One loan a month beats a $90K job: the entry path, and a referral role that needs no license

Brad and Kortes pitch mortgage origination to anyone doing physical or hourly work: relationships matter more than loan knowledge now that AI does the technical part.

  • License: a 20-hour course (2.5 days over a weekend) with partner Locked In Learning, then NEXA University for the basics and lead generation.
  • Pay math: about $8K a file on average, so one loan a month is over $90,000 a year. Some NEXA loan officers routinely close 10 to 20 a month.
  • Kortes's career framing: the two highest-paid fields are professional athlete (you need a gift) and financial sales — 'we sell money'.
  • Leads: you can start on a team that hands you leads for a lower split, but he pushes self-producing: 'teach somebody how to fish', and don't get complacent on a '$15,000 check every Friday'.
  • BDM (business development marketer): no license, paid for referrals. Top performers are a grandma referring her granddaughter, a roofer, hair stylists, Uber drivers and realtors. It has been audited by state financial and real-estate departments.
  • Commission freedom: 'payations', meaning you stay on vacation as long as you want. Brad: nobody should have to ask another man for permission to spend more time with his family.

What to do with this

Best Actionable Takeaways

💸
Careers: Price a career switch in loans per month: at ~$8K a file, one closed loan a month is the $90K benchmark.
🔍
Careers: Ask any brokerage to disclose its full rate and fee take per loan before signing. Compare that, not the headline split.
📜
Careers: Read the clawback clause on any signing check. A 30-month lock-up with full repayment is the industry norm Kortes describes.
🏠
Personal finance: When rates fall, call your servicer or original loan officer before a rival does. They already have your data and get paid to keep you.
🧾
Business: Before accepting 'that's not allowed', ask where the law says so. Then get the answer from a regulator or your attorney, not a competitor.

Names and terms at a glance

Glossary & Entities

Basis point (bps) — One hundredth of a percent; 275 bps on a loan is 2.75% of the loan amount.
Loan servicing — Collecting payments and managing a loan after closing, paid monthly and carrying rights to borrower data.
Trigger lead — An alert that another lender just pulled a borrower's credit, sold to or seen by the servicer.
Soft pull — A credit check that doesn't affect the score or show to the borrower.
HELOC — Home equity line of credit: revolving credit secured by the home, usually at a lower rate than cards.
DBA — 'Doing business as': a brokerage keeps its brand while operating under NEXA's license and platform.
BDM — Business development marketer: an unlicensed referral role paid for bringing in borrowers.
Section 179 — US tax provision allowing immediate deduction of qualifying equipment purchases, such as aircraft used in business.
NIL — Name, image and likeness payments to college athletes; Kortes's analogy for loan-officer signing checks.
