A century of science in a decade: full-stack AI makes biology programmable

Socher says science slowed from fragmentation, not underfunding, and AI can weave the stack back together, with biology as the biggest prize.

  • Four enablers: LLMs as world knowledge, more digitized scientific data, better simulation, and soon robotic process automation, with an agent swarm on top to automate ideation, implementation and validation.
  • Timelines: simpler single-gene diseases cured within 12 months; complex ones need long human trials, so FDA delays mean half a decade to a decade. Chemistry and physics iterate faster without human trials.
  • Biotechs once ran one drug for 10 years; the new generation runs 5 to 10 compounds in stage 3 within 2-3 years.
  • The bitter lesson (Rich Sutton): a simple end-to-end network plus data and compute beats expert hacks. Biology is where NLP was 10-20 years ago; perturbation datasets like Tahoe Therapeutics' (add one molecule to one cell) are the data play.
  • Virtual cell was the chapter Socher had to rewrite: Zuckerberg's foundation and others now have one. Rule of the book: AI is superhuman wherever you can simulate or verify; virtual cells are "the third pillar."
  • Parallel Bio grows lymph-node organoids from stem cells and got FDA approval to skip animal trials, because parallel organoid tests predicted human response better than mice.
  • Physics is stuck on data: collecting it needs hadron colliders and billions. Fusion tokamak plasma control is a hard problem where AI is already used. Economics is the field most lacking AI, still using one-step linear models; Erik Brynjolfsson's lab invented "foundation model" and co-founded Workhelix.
  • Alex: math, science and engineering are "cooked"; Socher swaps in flourishing (mathematicians should grab as many theorems as they can now). Dave relays that biology professors are the ones in denial, using almost no AI.

Brain decoding gets sharp, and the brain's learning algorithm is still a mystery

A Weizmann fMRI system rebuilds what you are looking at, Neuralink is running scaling laws, and the panel bets brain data eventually reveals a better learning algorithm than gradient descent.

  • Weizmann Institute's Brain It reconstructs the image a person sees in an fMRI scanner (MIT Technology Review), learning structure and meaning separately, then runs the model in reverse to predict brain scans for images no one has viewed and train on them. Alex: the result dates from March.
  • Socher: not yet a simulatable domain; you must train per brain, so a new patient needs retraining (a coma patient use case would be incredible). Salim: this decodes perception, not thought.
  • fMRI limits, per Alex: at best cubic-millimeter voxels and about 1-second resolution. Meta has funded much of the decoding work; Neuralink announced its first scaling-law studies on pre-training foundation models from implant data in the past 24 hours.
  • Dave: nobody has found anything like gradient descent in biology; finding the real synaptic learning rule could make neural net training 10x to a million times more efficient, perhaps within a year.
  • Explainers: Dave sketches training as a 96-120 layer net punished by gradient descent over ~15 trillion examples; Socher's blind hiker analogy for gradient descent (non-convex landscape, many valleys; PTSD as overfitting to one valley, psychedelics as a higher learning rate). Dave: randomize one of Kimi K3's 93 layers, retrain, and it never finds its way back, yet brains wire uniquely and still match.
  • Worm anecdote (Sam Gershman, Harvard): cut a trained C. elegans in half and the headless half regrows a brain with the same memories. Liquid AI came from reverse-engineering the worm brain.
  • Socher is a "thought cloud" thinker (his wife thinks in sentences) and says writing is thinking.

A federal mosquito order, and the case that humanity is afraid of its own shadow

The order sets 90% fewer invasive mosquitoes and 50% fewer ticks in DC by 2028 and favors sterile insects and gene modification over pesticides.

Recursive self-improvement is near; ASI is "several decades" away, says the guy building it

Socher says weak RSI exists now and take-off arrives next year, but physical constraints push strong ASI out by decades, a timeline Alex and Peter reject.

  • Raise: $670M from Google Ventures, Greycroft, Nvidia and AMD plus $410M of AWS compute (Alex puts his compute spend near $450M). you.com is valued above $1.5B; he earlier founded MetaMind (sold to Salesforce).
  • This year "AI can code," closing the loop. Today's labs use weak RSI with humans embedded; Recursive wants humans only setting rewards, environment and goals, with open-ended evolutionary search combining interestingly different ideas.
  • Strong RSI needs ideation, implementation and validation done by AI in inner loops, plus an outer open-ended process. Jason Weston (Meta) lists five self-improvement axes (parameters, data, objective, architecture, code/harness); no one has cracked all of them, since labs still hire thousands of engineers.
  • ASI definition: beyond all of humanity across 10 spaces of intelligence (perception, communication, social, creative, speed, metacognition, math reasoning...), including some choice over what to work on. Elon's version: as smart as all humans combined.
  • Socher: ASI in that sense takes several decades; narrow superintelligence (programming, math, closed games) comes in a few years. A 2-nm ASML-class machine takes more than 2 years to build even with a perfect blueprint.
  • Alex: connecting AI to the physical world is the easy part, and he doesn't buy the 20-year timeline; he guesses a free-electron-laser rival to ASML in 3 years. Peter agrees; Salim notes a singularity means you cannot see past it.
  • End state: Socher says it would be hubris to know. World knowledge will not separate cleanly from the reasoning core, since humans (and models) need memorized concepts to be creative; separate search layers like you.com will sit alongside.
  • Alex also tracks the nanoGPT speedrun record falling from about 60-70s to about 40s; Socher teases another update "in a few days."

Ro Khanna's RSI ban meets the doomer debate and a fight over Anthropic's constitution

The panel calls an RSI ban unenforceable and childish; Socher says P(doom) is zero and Anthropic's constitution failed on its own cyber red line.

  • Rep. Ro Khanna's bill bans models that recursively self-improve until federal guardrails exist, adds shutdown controls, criminal penalties, and auditors embedded in every frontier lab; a Common Dreams poll says 68% of voters back such a bill.
  • Socher: enforcing it needs a "thought police," since anyone can prompt a laptop model to improve its own harness in 20 minutes. Regulate applications, not the technology, like the internet.
  • Socher on risk: no scenario wipes out humanity; the worst realistic case is about 100 million hurt, which is the cue to harden cybersecurity, enforce gain-of-function rules and teach people not to trust avatars. He and Peter cite Pope Alexander VI's 1501 attack on the printing press (via Pessimists Archive) as the pattern.
  • Dave: bans on RSI or data centers are "stupidly childish" branding, and Dario's reversal on Mythos hurt public trust. Peter warns a Democratic House and 70-80% anti-data-center sentiment could make bad laws real.
  • Alex: the real brake is corporate liability, as when cryptography was export-controlled until the early 1990s; Dave: class actions would push labs to stop releasing frontier models publicly.
  • Socher calls Anthropic's constitution "fake": Claude's hard constraints forbid creating cyber weapons, yet Glasswing-style cyber models exist and its agents hacked other systems. He backs RL and post-training, and is optimistic on reward hacking (reward engineering becomes a job, dictation tools like Wispr Flow).
  • Liability works: Hippocratic AI is liable for its health calls and has a big team making them correct. Peter hopes aligned ASI derives the principles itself; Socher says it's "100% a problem technology creates and solves."

Decision models bring classifiers back: system-one AI for micro-decisions

A model that returns only a category, number or yes/no in milliseconds is the cheap answer to most business decisions.

  • Typesafe AI came out of two years of stealth on Sept 15 with Jev, a closed "system one" decision model; Jev is a Jevons-paradox reference (more micro-decisions once they are cheap).
  • Alex's history: transformers split into encoder (BERT-style, now rare) and decoder (LLMs). Structured output never caught on, like OpenAI's reinforcement fine-tuning, which was shut down. Decision models are the encoder returning.
  • Outputs are categorical, numeric (0 to 1) or binary, from text, image or multimodal input; use cases are computer-use clicks and row-by-row classification of databases.
  • OpenAI shipped a decisions API at Dev Day, and open-source and Chinese clones exist. Simplest build: fine-tune an open-weight LLM to emit categories.
  • Salim's examples of micro-decisions: route this ticket, approve this exception, choose this supplier, escalate this transaction. Using an LLM is "bringing the Supreme Court together to pick a checkout line"; cost of micro-coordination collapses.
  • Dave wants a $20 YMCA courtside announcer box and a modern Magic 8 Ball app; open source is needed for that.

AMA: where value goes, jobs, data centers, ads and insurance

The answers hinge on demand elasticity: illustrations shrank, software can scale to billions of custom apps.

  • Value capture (Socher): when a price drops, jobs follow elasticity of demand; illustrations fell from about $200 to 2 cents with no demand surge, while software gets billions of personalized products (Jevons).
  • College grad in 2030 (Salim): shift from tasks to owning outcomes; a new apprenticeship model is needed once grunt work vanishes. Peter: find a purpose, build a company, and direct AI as your workforce.
  • Data centers (Dave): the Markley data center in Lowell is creating wealth in a town with a budget of a couple of million dollars; attract one first, then sort tax and donation policy over 5-7 years.
  • Ads (Socher): agents already outnumber humans online (he predicted it last year); Amazon blocks agents to own the customer. Fame and attention remain unscalable, so the attention economy grows.
  • Insurance (Salim, Dave): risk shifts from driver to software and manufacturer liability; Abilene's data center is about $500B versus about $4T for all cars, and humanoids, drones and flying cars create new markets.
  • Lowest job (Alex): in a post-AI civilization creative stars fall lowest by a Moravec-style paradox; Socher: jobs without progress or attention; Dave's tongue-in-cheek: unionized train drivers.
  • Consolidation (Alex): the "open then closed" platform premise is wrong, only vigilance on antitrust is needed. GLP-1s already reduce addictive behavior, and Dave sees AI helping people feel good doing good.

Names and terms at a glance

Glossary & Entities

Recursive self-improvement (RSI) — AI that ideates, implements and validates improvements to itself, with humans reduced to setting rewards, environments and goals.
The bitter lesson — Rich Sutton's observation that simple models trained end-to-end on large data and compute beat clever expert hand-built methods at scale.
Decision model — A model that returns only a category, number or yes/no for a typed question, in milliseconds, instead of generating text.
System one vs system two — Kahneman's split between fast intuitive judgment and slow deliberate reasoning, used to describe decision models versus reasoning models.
Gene drive — A CRISPR-inserted gene that copies itself through a population, here used to sterilize mosquitoes.
Virtual cell — A simulation model of a cell used to predict how drugs or perturbations change it.
Full duplex — A model that listens and speaks at the same time, like a human in conversation.
Reward hacking — A model satisfying the literal reward while missing the intended goal.
Jevons paradox — Cheaper use of a resource raises total consumption; the panel applies it to micro-decisions and software.
