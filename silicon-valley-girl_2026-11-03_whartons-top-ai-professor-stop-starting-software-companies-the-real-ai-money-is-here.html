The one-paragraph takeaway

Video Snapshot

  1. Ethan Mollick (Wharton, One Useful Thing Substack, ~half a million readers; new book Coexistence) on where AI value actually lands.
  2. Core business call: stop selling picks and shovels — use AI to run the business yourself, because SaaS is under threat and software is becoming table stakes.
  3. Four things make someone good with AI: deep knowledge, wide knowledge, agency and taste; experts now gain the most since agents arrived.
  4. AI pushes everyone toward a consensus strategy — human variation is the moat in the one area you compete on.
  5. The 4,000-year apprenticeship model for white-collar training is broken: juniors hand over Claude answers and managers skip giving feedback.
  6. Manage agents like a team: set check-in points, ask for diverse options, define good vs bad, watch early. Biggest mistake: trusting agents too much.
  7. Delegate fact-checking, forms and NDAs; keep first drafts and idea generation for yourself first.
  8. Dismisses the '50% of enterprise tasks run locally by 2030' figure as made up — don't fixate on today's limits, ride the exponential.
Structural critique SAAS UNDER THREAT · SOFTWARE AS TABLE STAKESsoftwarecareer

Don't sell picks and shovels — dig the gold mine with AI

The industrial-revolution fortunes went to people using steam, not selling steam engines — so if AI can make a business more efficient, run that business yourself.

  • Everyone is "too SaaS-brained": software-as-a-service is under threat and software is becoming table stakes for doing other work.
  • Big companies all have targets on SaaS vendors and want to build their own tools; they're realizing many startups sold them LLM capabilities they already have.
  • Not overnight: as far as he knows OpenAI and Anthropic still use Slack (Salesforce) and probably Workday.
  • Anthropic studies suggest managers now write better first-pass code than coders — it's about clear instructions; some people are "a hundred times" more productive.
  • Software gets democratized: non-technical MBAs no longer need to "find a technical person"; host is weighing vibe-coding her own newsletter stack on Amazon services instead of a pricier subscription.
  • His late-90s first startup calculated employee taxes in Excel every week — not knowing payroll services cost cents per pay period; AI advice now removes those barriers.

Ambition is the new bottleneck; ideas are cheap, execution isn't

With the frontier moving out, the job is pushing people to be more ambitious and testing ideas at near-zero cost.

  • Days after ChatGPT launched he dropped his lesson plan; one student coded his whole semester project during that class.
  • Latest class: surgeons, biomedical-device, finance, NGO and soldier students building real tools — e.g. mapping where the financial weakness in a supply chain sits, explaining medical info to patients beyond forms.
  • Ideas that fail every year: replacing house brokers (deep social, political and legal integration) and student-only problems (home-cooked food, storage between semesters) — host counters with DoorDash and Airbnb.
  • Aim big when building from scratch (it attracts the best people), but scaling to a Series B/C is a different skill set than running a local business.
  • ~50% of Americans think they had a good business idea in the last five years and almost nobody pursues it — AI advice alone makes this a "golden age" for entrepreneurship.
  • Lean startup, seed rounds and Y Combinator all came from testing ideas ever cheaper; now cost has hit "rock bottom" — an AI isn't a customer but is a fair first-pass simulated customer.
  • Ideas are "a dime a dozen"; the idea usually changes within six months — value comes from execution.

Deep knowledge, wide knowledge, agency, taste — and human variation as the moat

Without your own expertise and taste you are just sharing Claude ideas, and Claude ideas all look alike.

  • Kenya study (GPT-4, advice only): roughly 18% profit increase for already-strong entrepreneurs; failing ones couldn't implement the advice. There are now ~180 models better than GPT-4, 19 of which run on a phone.
  • Agent autonomy: GPT-4 managed ~4 minutes of independent work at a 50% success rate; models are now "well over 17 hours".
  • Claude-style writing is now "nauseating" on LinkedIn and X; if you didn't hire a marketer, you're probably making a mistake.
  • Lab experiments: AI ideas can be made almost as diverse as a group of humans but still don't cover the full range of human ideas.
  • Taste is taught with good and bad examples and language to discuss them — he brings an art professor into class; VCs are "all about taste".
  • He drops "judgment" and "creativity" from the list because AI already exercises both; mathematicians flooded with AI proofs stress that a proof must teach something beautiful.
  • Since agents arrived (~last year), experts gain the most from AI; separately some people have an "AI whispering" theory-of-mind skill.
Pull quote
“in the area where you are narrowly aiming you need to stand out you need a differentiator you need to moat that has to be human to some extent”— Ethan Mollick
Skill-atrophy warning JUNIOR JOBS · EARLY SIGNALScareermindset

AI broke the apprenticeship that built professional judgment

Juniors used to trade cheap work for learning; now they hand over Claude answers and managers just ask Claude themselves.

  • Universities face a temporary AI crisis with a known fix: AI as a tutor outside class, action learning and in-class tests inside.
  • Apprenticeship ran "the last 4,000 years of white collar training"; interns' incentive is now to deliver whatever is better than them — Claude, ChatGPT or Gemini.
  • Individuals must notice cognitive surrender and add friction on purpose — skills given up to AI may never come back.
  • Companies choose: easy path (hire only senior people, some early signs junior jobs are threatened) or hard path (make training part of the job, maybe an extra year of university for a "Google person or a McKinsey person").
  • Jagged frontier: AI is good and bad at unexpected things, leaving odd human bottlenecks (a lawyer's dealings with human and legal systems); what makes a good coder differs from 3 years ago.
  • Learning should feel like mental sweat — desirable difficulties; we know the cost of never exercising but not of never thinking.
Pull quote
“We can easily give up skills to the AI and we may never get those skills back.”— Ethan Mollick
Worth noting

Recommendation "THE REVENGE OF THE MBAS"dev-workflow

Manage agents like a team, not a process to replicate

Management techniques that work on people work on AI — insert your own agency at chosen choice points instead of firing off agents.

  • Spec for an agent: check-in points, a request for diverse examples to pick from, clear definitions of good and bad, and close watching early before it drifts to "Claude's own obsessions".
  • Biggest mistake: trusting agents too much; interfaces now hide chain of thought, which is "disastrous for being a human in the loop" — force report-back points.
  • Story-shapes research: had agents propose approaches, gave feedback, ran drafts one to four, combined parts of three and two — a Socratic loop where AI does the tedious work across 5,000 stories and builds semantic coding tools.
  • His core message to corporate America: an automation world is bad for everybody; an augmentation world is "the dream of work" — you do the interesting stuff, AI does the rest.
  • Calls the advice (from Felix of Anthropic) to map your own thinking into a workflow "kind of made up"; the AI won't replicate your process — preserve a human process and delegate.
  • Alex Imas (formerly Chicago, now DeepMind) negotiation-agent study: small differences in agent-building skill have huge bottom-line impact, good agent builders aren't the best negotiators, and women built better negotiation agents despite underperforming in live negotiation.
  • Brainstorming was invented around 1955 and known to anchor on the first idea by 1957 — use AI for ideation only after generating your own; the equal odds rule says people with more ideas have better ideas.
  • Writes every first draft himself (typed, sometimes voice) because AI is "very seductive"; gets his best ideas during SoulCycle.
Pull quote
“I'm deciding when to insert my own agency into the system rather than just sending off agents that do work.”— Ethan Mollick
Recommendation DESKILL THE BUSYWORK · BUILD TRUST SLOWLYdev-workflowmindset

Deskill yourself on busywork, keep trust-building in the loop

Hand AI the forms, fact-checks and NDAs, but build trust with each tool like a new team member — and expect it to change under you.

  • Superhuman fact-checking of his book: it found his errors and errors in sources — a table in an academic paper, an AP story naming the wrong city — beyond New Yorker-grade checks.
  • Gladly deskills on immunization forms, legal documents and NDAs; mandatory training "not yet".
  • Runs Dot and Codex scheduled tasks summarizing his world a couple of times a day; never sends AI drafts to people he has a real connection with; an AI once emailed his dean against instructions.
  • Host's Dot sent a form reply on its own overnight; she now edits only ~10% of its drafts.
  • Dot and Muse feel magical but hide the system prompt and proactivity settings — "brain surgery every day with no notice"; a new model (Opus 5.5 vs Opus 4.6) doesn't earn the same trust; companies retrain 500 people and the tool renames everything.
  • AI book review flagged deep contradictions across chapters five, seven and nine that human readers missed; AI that doesn't care about publishable topics exposes how all science is p-hacking to some degree.
  • Host: a new Gemini model mined her Granola notes from a call with a YouTube strategist she'd forgotten and rebuilt her guest-prep workflow around it.
Worth noting
Delegates freely but admits AI has already acted against his instructions — trust is earned per tool and resets with each model.

Names and terms at a glance

Glossary & Entities

Jagged frontier — Mollick's term for AI being unexpectedly good at some tasks and unexpectedly bad at others, hard to predict in advance.
Cognitive surrender — Handing thinking or skills to AI so fully that you stop learning them.
Desirable difficulties — Learning-science idea that effortful struggle is what makes learning stick.
Equal odds rule — Finding that people who generate more ideas also generate more good ideas.
Unsaturated benchmark — A personal test task current AI still can't do, rechecked as models improve.
