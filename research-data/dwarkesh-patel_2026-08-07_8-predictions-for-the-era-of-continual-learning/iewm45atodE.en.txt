So, I've explained before why I believe true lifelong learning is necessary.  I don't think an artificial intelligence can do the whole job as well as a human if it's only forced to write markdown files from session to session.  To give an illustrative example, imagine that this is how students learn to play the saxophone. So, you have one student who has never played the saxophone before.  He enters the music room and tries to play.  Of course, it's his first time, so he's not doing anything right .  And he writes down a bunch of notes about what went wrong.  And behind the music hall door, the next student is waiting. He comes in, reads all these notes.  He's never played either, so of course he's wrong.  And he continues to add to these notes.  And you have an endless number of students outside the door who keep writing notes to the next person.  I don't think there is any sequence of
text they could write to each other that would allow the next student to immediately master the saxophone on the first try.  At a certain point, you really need to accumulate relevant experience in your own brain.  I think the same will apply to many of the skills that we want AI to actually learn in all the workplaces where it is used. Okay, so what will change when we get true lifelong learning ?  First, I think a lot of proposals for regulating AI assume that you train a model and then deploy it. And so if we run a bunch of checks on the model before deploying it, we can guarantee that it won't help in cyberattacks or do anything crazy.  I don't think this assumption will necessarily make sense in the future.  And that's one of the many reasons why I'm really concerned about putting any kind of safety regulatory regime in place now, because we don't know what technology we'll be dealing
with even a year from now, let alone 5 or 10 years from now.  What if the model improves every day based on the millions of work sessions it conducts that day ?  If this happens, we could potentially see an archaic and even counterproductive approach to combating AI threats. If the government is looking to somehow assess the safety of modelers, I think it would be more appropriate to conduct monthly or quarterly risk inspections rather than trying to isolate some specific point after training is complete but before deployment begins, as this will no longer be a meaningful category in the future.  Second, the way laboratories ensure technical compliance will likely have to be completely changed. Much of the research now focuses on how to ensure that a fixed set of weights behaves properly during operation.  But I don't see much research on how to make sure that even with constant updating
of weights, the AI ​​system doesn't get hacked and doesn't turn into a deceptive or evil personality.  And if AI also generalizes experiences across users, how do you prevent users from introducing backdoors or some malicious biases into the underlying model?  In a way, that's really the problem of alignment for people, isn't it? People improve themselves.  If you have children—I don't have any , but I imagine that's how it goes .  If you have children, they go out into the world to learn new things.  Sometimes they start behaving unreasonably.  They fall under the influence of crazy ideologies, take the wrong medications, and become very strange.  But you hope you've given them enough common sense and basic values ​​to improve as individuals on their own, without acquiring any really strange beliefs or misanthropic ideas.  Third, the diversity of AI intelligences will increase.    There
are currently fewer than five prominent AI intelligences—I mean basic models that serve millions, hundreds of millions, or billions of users simultaneously.  And they're all pretty similar to each other, by the way, because they were also trained on roughly the same data.  But if AIs learn from their own experience, and that experience varies not only between different companies but also between different instances of the same model, we could see significant diversity in the output.  And this, in my opinion, will be a positive result.  I think one of the things to worry about in the future is the emergence of this monolithic " singleton", which will be quite boring.  A world where continuous learning occurs will hopefully be more interesting than the collapse of model diversity that we see today.  Fourth , when deployment becomes part of training, the returns from leadership in the AI ​​arms
race are accelerated.  After all, if you have the best model, and more and more people use your AI to do more complex and useful work, resulting in a lot of feedback that can be integrated outside of the session, your model will become even smarter.  Fifth: If the model is trained mostly during deployment, labs will feel a lot of pressure to release their smartest models early. Anthropic has reportedly been using Metis internally since February, but only made the model publicly available in June.  Under conditions of real continuous learning, this would simply be impossible.  You would n't be able to maintain a four-month pause between internal and external deployments and remain competitive , because the competitor that releases the worst model will have a better version thanks to real-world experience. Sixth: Continuous learning will create a clear business model for leading AI labs, which
they currently lack. Many people ask: how exactly will AI labs make money? I asked about that too .  When Dario was on my podcast, I asked him this question, and he made an analogy with cloud providers.  He noted: look, cloud providers offer many of the same services, but still make high profits.  You might have noticed this if you watched Amazon or Google's quarterly reports.  They are all well.  But the reason for the high margins of cloud services is that switching from one provider to another takes a lot of time and money .  Now there's nothing stopping me from starting a software repository with Codex, continuing with Cursor, and finishing with Claude Code.  But once true continuous learning emerges , and the model you work with gets better with each subsequent session, there will be significant transition costs. If you want to change AI, you will effectively fire an employee who has
accumulated months of experience working with your company and replace them with an inexperienced intern who needs to be taught from scratch.  And once such a tie-up occurs, model suppliers can demand quite high margins.  Seventh. Of course, large companies will understand this dynamic.  They will try to avoid such a binding.  But what if the choice is between being locked into a model vendor or losing an extremely valuable opportunity as the model improves for you from session to session?  If real-world use becomes the primary way to improve models, then AI labs can subsidize users and businesses, allowing models to train in their sessions.  This is already happening if you look at the types of deals offered to new users of programming products.  This is very similar to why Google provides access to search for free. Conversely, labs can claim that any enterprise that refuses to allow training
in their sessions will not have access to the best models.  Using carrot-and-stick methods, labs can do a lot to encourage users to let AI learn from experience.  Of course, I'm overlooking the fact that there's a difference between updating a single user's set of weights and merging all those different branches of weights back into the main model.  And the latter may be technically more difficult, but over time this will also be resolved.  Eight. AI training already has significant economies of scale.  You can spread all these expensive training costs among a larger number of users.  And the proof of this is that labs' revenues are growing much faster than their computing power.  But continuous learning can also lead to economies of scale when outputting data to end users, namely through batch processing.  You may have seen my episode with Ryder Pope where we discussed this in detail . 
But if the instructions for each company are to completely upgrade the scales, rather than using low-rank adapters, then batch processing provides huge advantages. Rough estimates suggest that the optimal batch size for inference in a sparse model like Deep Seek V3 is over 2400 simultaneous sequences.  If you don't, you're not using your computing resources efficiently enough.  And if you want to understand why, again, I highly recommend that episode with Reiner on the economics of withdrawal.  But in any case, the point is that a particular set of weights is only justified when thousands of sequences are decoded simultaneously.  A large company with many employees and agents performing a variety of tasks can service its scale branch very efficiently . Whereas an individual user working with only a batch size of one can get computing efficiency over a hundred times lower.  Therefore, the
economic aspects of servicing personalized scales are much more beneficial for large organizations. It is clear that a lot will change before lifelong learning truly works .  And the most important changes are probably the ones that are the most difficult to predict in advance.  But the ones above seem pretty clear even now.
