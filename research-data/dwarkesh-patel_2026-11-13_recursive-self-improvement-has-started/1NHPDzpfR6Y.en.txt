answer was Na'vi Stokes. Now, of course, it turns out that continual learning did turn out to be harder given the fact that the labs haven't cracked it yet. But are we so confident that it's that much harder that the nonline learning models a year or two smarter than the ones that are currently solving millennium prize problems won't be able to crack it? Even if they're amnesiacs like the current models are, these models are out here are solving millennium prize problems while still being these derpy savant interns that have no long-term memory. It's like watching somebody do cartwheels down the track and still keep pace with Usain Bolt. If they were actually allowed to run, I'm sure they could go even faster. But who's to say that they can't win this race while still cartwheeling? Now, all this being said, I think there is a serious chance that we don't solve continual learning within the
next few years and that models are not able to compensate for this missing capability through their other advantages. That's why I put a 25% probability that we won't automate Ilia level top human researchers until after 2032. One important objection people have is that we'll be bottlenecked by finding the right verifiable proxies. All the verifiable proxies that I've listed above assume the current paradigm, they would argue. But figuring out what objective to verify in the first place, choosing which hill to have GPT7 climb is itself an important part of AI research. This is what one might call research taste. And how are you going to train for that? Before 2018, almost nobody was saying that minimizing loss on next token prediction is going to be the most tractable path to AI progress. So nobody would have bothered to build nano GPT style speedrun environments where the agent swarm has
to minimize how much time it takes to get to a certain pre-training loss. Won't there be multiple new paradigms that we have to cross to get from here to super intelligence? And won't each new paradigm require some associated proxy that we don't know X anti? How will the automated researchers find these during RSI? I have two responses to this objection. First, the transformer was visible on in fact invented to beat the existing translation benchmarks. A similar story is true for reasoning models and the pre-existing coding and math benchmarks. Proxies made before a paradigm leap seem perfectly capable of capturing large step changes in performance. This is because AI learning algorithms are quite general. And second, it's not that difficult to come up with paradigm agnostic outer loop proxies. For example, how many millennium prize problems does your new model solve? And if you train your
model on data before 1905, how long does it take before it discovers general relativity? How fast does it master totally unseen video games? And so on. All in all, I think the other objections to the impact of RSI are much stronger, and I'd be very surprised if this ended up being the reason that RSI was not a big deal. The other big bottleneck that people point out is data. Isn't data one of the main inputs into AI progress? How will RSI happen without that scaling up at the same rate? My answer is that data will continue growing during RSI, but the AIs will be the ones compiling it. Producing better training data for both pre-training and post-training is itself an ML research problem and AIs are getting increasingly better at doing it themselves. Now, it is true that data is an extremely important input into AI progress. Together with Jerry Han, who's a student at Princeton, I actually
did some research into this. We found that for pre-training over the last 6 years more than three times the algorithmic progress has come from data improvements than model improvements. However, I don't think that people realize that these data improvements in pre-training are very much in line with normal AI research work. It's basically figuring out which techniques were filtering existing data or generating new synthetic data empirically lead to lower loss on a held out corpus. A similar thing seems to be increasingly true for RL. Deep Seek just published a paper where they talk about how they postrain their new model. They see that changes to the basic RL algorithm have not mattered nearly as much and that quote systematic improvements in the scale, diversity and verifiability of synthesized data and environments account for essentially all of the observed gains. End quote. And the way
in which these environments are made is itself getting more and more automated. They write quote we invest in large-scale automated pipelines for data synthesis and environment construction using difficulty and correctness as reward signals. We iteratively train the model to construct better tasks. End quote. Now I want to talk about the idea that AI progress will be bottlenecked by experiments. So earlier I made this extended analogy between the current acceleration in mathematics and what we should expect to happen in AI research. But there is one big difference between AI research and mathematics. In math, you can simply reason your way with pen and paper to new discoveries. Whereas in AI research, you need to run actual experiments to validate your ideas. And these take compute and time. And as the number of automated researchers grows, each gets access to a smaller and smaller slice
of the total experiment compute pie. My answer to this is that each agent will still get access to a ton of compute. Basically, Frontier Labs have been more than quadrupling their compute every single year. And if this trend continues, then by the end of 2027, OpenAI could almost give 10,000 agents, so the amount that were involved in solving the Millennium Prize problem, each enough compute to run a GPT3 size training run every single day. Keep in mind that the final model trained in the original transformer paper in 2017 took 3 days on 8 P100s which would cost about 50 bucks if he went to rent it today or about 110,000th of the training compute of GPT3. If the top human AI researchers like Nor Shazir and Iaskar had enough experiment compute to train a GPT3 size model every single day, they might make slower progress than they currently do. But it would be extremely surprising to me if they
failed to make meaningful progress altogether and just sat down and started complaining about how small their cluster was. Now, how productive do you think an AI lab would be if it had 10,000 great researchers, each with this much compute and each thinking 10 times as fast? You wouldn't want to write a check to this Neolab. The nature of research and experimentation at the labs today reflects that researcher time is extremely scarce relative to compute. top AI researchers individually get paid hundreds of millions of dollars a year. So the way labs use compute today tells us very little about how efficiently they could use it if compute rather than researcher time was the key bottleneck. There are two main ways in which the RSI agents worm will use experiment compute much more efficiently than researchers today use it. First, immediately they can trade labor for compute to save valuable researcher
time. Experiments today are and should be very wasteful with compute. So instead of painstakingly setting up and carefully monitoring tons of smallcale pre-training experiments, a human researcher today might instead kick off a couple of huge multi-agent RL runs in parallel. But if you're bottlenecked by experiment compute, you can just do more of the former. And when those automated researchers are doing an RL run, they could read every single rollout transcript and immediately pause and rebuild a whole environment if some task is broken. They could be doing crazy amounts of small scale ablations of their models to make them oh so slightly better. And when a team of agents decides to combine their compute together in order to do a much bigger run, they could spend many subjective decades designing the most informative poolled experiment. And once it's done running, they could spend further
subjective decades comprehensively analyzing it before designing a new experiment. The other big way that this huge agent swarm could remain productive despite the fact that each individual copy will have access to less compute is that these automated researchers could come up with a better science for learning from small scale experiments. Scaling laws themselves are a good example of this. The GBD4 paper says that they were able to predict the model's final loss using models trained with 10,000x less compute. One of the things that the smarter and faster automated company could be doing is trying to discover more scaling phenomenon like MUP which lets researchers anticipate the optimal hyperparameters for a larger run from a 100x smaller model. How many more similar discoveries like this are there? I remember chatting to an AI researcher who thought that learning from extremely small neural
networks could be incredibly informative. He put it this way. The gods above must work from the same principles as the ants below.
