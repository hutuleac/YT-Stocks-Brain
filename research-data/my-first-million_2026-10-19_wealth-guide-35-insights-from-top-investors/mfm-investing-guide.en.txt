Introduction
Quick disclaimer before we get into it: nothing in this guide is stock picking advice. We're not telling you what to buy, sell, or hold. We're two guys who find this stuff fascinating and spent a lot of time talking to people who are genuinely great at it.
What follows is 35+ principles from top investors and financial operators — Howard Marks, Mohnish Pabrai, Morgan Housel, Cathie Wood, and a dozen others. These are their frameworks, their mental models, their rules.
Some of these ideas will resonate immediately. Some will challenge how you currently think about money. All of them are worth sitting with.
What you do with them is entirely your call.
It's not about what you know. It's not about how smart you are. It's just about how you behave.
Morgan Housel

Keep It Simple
1. If You Need Excel, Skip It
Mohnish Pabrai
The best investment ideas are so obviously good that they don't require complex modeling. If you need a spreadsheet to convince yourself, that's a sign it won’t work.
Complexity is often a disguise for uncertainty. When the opportunity is real, the math is simple. When you find yourself building elaborate models to justify a position, you're usually working backward from a conclusion you've already reached. The truly great opportunities are obvious.
In Practice
When Warren Buffett founded Western Insurance, the stock price was $15/share while the company's earnings were $25/share and the book value was $80/share. No Excel needed — the opportunity was immediately obvious.
Similarly, when analyzing Japanese trading companies, Buffett discovered they had an 8% dividend yield while he could borrow in yen at just 0.5%. This 7.5% spread on billions of dollars required no complex analysis to recognize as exceptional.
Try This
For your next potential investment, try explaining it without using numbers or referencing a spreadsheet. If the fundamental value creation isn't clear without complex math, reconsider the opportunity.
"If you need Excel, it's an automatic pass. It means there's something complicated there which is not fitting in." — Mohnish Pabrai
2. The Easiest Path to Wealth
Sam Parr
Over the past 20 years, staying fully invested in the S&P 500 returns roughly 10% per year — your money doubles every 7 years. But if you missed just the 10 best days of those 7,300 days, returns drop to ~5%. Miss the best 20 days, and your gains drop to nearly zero. Over the last 10 years, 97% of active fund managers — Harvard MBAs with teams of analysts staring at Bloomberg terminals all day — still couldn't beat the index.
In Practice
At the bottom of the COVID crash, Sam sold everything. He was convinced the market was done. It ripped straight back up. After selling his company in 2021, he put almost everything into a basic Vanguard index fund. Five years later it's up 71%. He didn't have to do anything.
Mohnish Pabrai compounded at 70% annually for his first five years. Built a $600M fund. Has spent his entire career studying the greatest investors who ever lived. His conclusion: for the overwhelming majority of people (99%+), you're best off just buying an index.
Try This
Set up automatic contributions to a low-cost index fund so the decision to stay invested is already made. If you really want a stock-picking habit, treat it like an entertainment budget — a small amount you're okay losing — and keep the rest boring.
"It's not about timing the market. It's about time IN the market." — Sam Parr
3. Do the Obvious Thing
Andrew Wilkinson
The best investment moves are usually the obvious ones you talked yourself out of.
Clever conditions, waiting for a better entry, and tactical maneuvering almost always reduce returns compared to doing the simple thing. The problem is that the simple thing doesn't feel smart enough to act on. So people add conditions, wait for confirmation, and watch the obvious trade play out without them.
In Practice
Andrew knew Nvidia was an exceptional business. He saw Facebook trading at 5x earnings. He didn't buy either because the move felt too easy.
Shaan sold Bitcoin to harvest a tax loss, planned to buy right back, then convinced himself he could wait for a slightly lower entry. Bitcoin went straight up. He bought back higher.
Try This
When you catch yourself adding conditions to an obvious trade, like I'll wait for a dip, I'll find something smarter than the index, I'll buy after the next earnings… write down the simple move. Do it. Stop there.
"I want to do the thing that makes sense and is obvious, and then forget about it and stop trying to be clever." — Andrew Wilkinson
4. Plan A and Plan B
Mohnish Pabrai
Keep your capital working in a reliable compounder by default. Only move when something so obviously good comes along that it hits you in the face.
Plan A: The Obvious Opportunity "The best investments are ones that hit you in the head with a two-by-four. Where things don't make sense, right? That's when we wanna dive in."
Plan B: The Default Compounder "I would treat Berkshire Hathaway as the index... Dollar cost average into the Berkshire class B shares, and you keep doing that day in, day out."
By default, have your capital working in a reliable compounder like Berkshire Hathaway (Plan B), which combines safety with reasonable returns. Meanwhile, scan for rare, obvious opportunities (Plan A). When you find one, like Frontline trading below liquidation value during a shipping industry crisis, you "peel off 10-15% of what you have in Berkshire, put it into that, let it play out, then put it back into Berkshire."
Try this: Establish your default position (Plan B) with automatic investments, then create a clear checklist of criteria that would qualify an opportunity for Plan A funding. Review your Plan A checklist quarterly to ensure you're maintaining discipline.

Win Where Nobody's Looking
5. Distressed Assets = Hidden Gold Mines
Scott Galloway
The best time to buy is when everyone else is running away. When a company or asset becomes radioactive, most investors flee. That exodus suppresses the price far below actual value. The fundamentals don't change as fast as the sentiment does. The gap between those two things is where the opportunity lives.
In Practice
Scott turned $2.5M into $75M buying a bankrupt vaping company (NJOY) that later sold to Altria for $2.75B.
Then he did it again. He bought $2.2M worth of FTX bankruptcy claims after reading their filings and noticing something most people missed: FTX held a significant stake in Anthropic that wasn't being properly valued. Those claims are now worth significantly more.
Bankruptcy is messy, legal, and uncomfortable. Most investors won't touch it. Less competition means better prices.
Try This
You don't need to buy bankruptcy claims directly to apply this logic. Look for the category of investment that's currently the most avoided — companies or sectors coming out of distress, not going into it. The entry point is ugliest right before the turn.
“The best asset class is distressed, and it goes to a very simple notion — the sexier an investment, the lower the returns.”       — Scott Galloway
6. Run Toward What Big Tech Avoids
Elad Gil
When large tech companies abandon a category for political or reputational reasons, they create a vacuum. That vacuum is the opportunity. Big tech has pricing power, distribution, and talent. When they exit a category voluntarily, not because it's unprofitable, but because it's uncomfortable, those advantages disappear. The market opens up. A startup can now do what a company with infinite resources just walked away from.
IN PRACTICE
Elad Gil's investment in Anduril didn't start with a pitch from founder Palmer Luckey. It started with Google shutting down Project Maven. His reaction: if the big tech companies are running away from this entire category, what a wonderful opportunity for a startup.
He called a contact at Google, confirmed it was the anti-Maven people who quit and not the ones who wanted to work on it, and started looking for founders in the space. He found someone who led him to Anduril's first round.
TRY THIS
Make a list of sectors that large tech companies are actively avoiding right now. Not because the business is bad but because it's politically uncomfortable or reputationally risky. That's often where the next breakout company is quietly being built.
7. Look for AI Exposure in Mislabeled Companies
Andrew Wilkinson
Some of the best AI investments don't look like AI investments. They look like something investors already decided they don't want. Public market investors use simple category labels: Crypto. Cannabis. Legacy media. When a label is bad, most investors don't look deeper. That creates mispricings in companies that have real underlying value the label is hiding.
In Practice
Real AI upside exists in companies that get mentally filed under the wrong category, and trade cheap as a result. Like Iris Energy (IREN), which looks like a Bitcoin mining company so most investors tune out. Reality:
Two infrastructure bankers who spent years building massive data centers next to renewable energy
Currently doing ~$500M EBITDA at a $2.4B valuation
The real bet is that those data centers flip to AI compute — scarce infrastructure that hyperscalers are desperately hunting for
Downside is limited (Bitcoin needs to drop ~70% to hit breakeven). Upside is a potential 5-10x if hyperscaler contracts land.
Try This
Scan for companies labeled as crypto, cannabis, biotech, or legacy media that have underlying assets benefiting from AI. The bad label suppresses the price. The asset tells the real story.
"I love stuff that's miscategorized and misunderstood by public market investors." — Andrew Wilkinson
8. Public Markets Are the Best Place to Find Mispricings
Mohnish Pabrai
Public markets are the only place in the world where the same asset gets sold at wildly different prices depending on how scared people are that week. In private markets, you're negotiating with a rational seller who knows what they own. In public markets, you're buying from whoever panicked last. Those are fundamentally different transactions. The panic seller doesn't care what the business is worth. They just want out. That's your opportunity.
In Practice
Mohnish bought IPSCO Steel at 3x earnings with $15 per share in cash on the balance sheet and zero debt. The stock doubled in a year, then got acquired at $160 — more than 3x his entry.
He also spent 7 months studying the coal industry when everyone hated it. Found companies trading under 2x annual cash flow with 50-year runways. His reaction: where do I sign?
Try This
When a sector becomes toxic in the headlines — fossil fuels, regional banks, Chinese stocks — don't avoid it. Screen for companies in that space with strong balance sheets and low debt. The hatred is doing the pricing for you.
"In auction-driven markets, you repeatedly run into these things where companies get mispriced. There's companies emerging from bankruptcy. There's things that people just don't like."  — Mohnish Pabrai
9. The Small Portfolio Advantage
Mohnish Pabrai
As capital grows from $1M to $10M to $100M, investors must abandon these smaller, often more lucrative opportunities. While big funds need to deploy billions, individual investors can find opportunities in smaller, overlooked corners of the market. Buffett has said if he were managing only $1M, he could achieve 50% annual returns.
Your smaller portfolio size isn't a disadvantage — it's actually your greatest edge. You can exploit market inefficiencies in smaller companies, special situations, and niche markets that large funds must ignore.
In Practice
Mohnish spent 7 months studying the coal industry when everyone hated it. Found companies trading under 2x annual cash flow with 50-year runways. Big funds couldn't touch them. He could.
Try This
Identify market segments where large funds can't participate (companies under $500M market cap, special situations with limited capital deployment, or niche markets with low liquidity). Create a watchlist specifically for these types of opportunities.
10. Invent a Category
Thomas Braziel
The investors with the most extraordinary long-term returns got in before an asset class became institutional.
When institutions arrive, they bring capital that compresses returns. Being there before the capital arrives means you capture the entire tailwind. The window is always the same: real opportunity, no institutional appetite, awkward or embarrassing to own. That combination won't last. But while it exists, the returns are extraordinary.
In Practice
Howard Marks created distressed debt as an institutional asset class. The returns were staggering because nobody else was doing it yet.
Thomas Braziel carved out crypto distressed before any institution would touch it. His first Mount Gox pitch got laughed out of a $2B hedge fund in 2015. That fund is now out of business. Thomas made 40x on the trade.
Try This
Ask yourself: what's the current version of "institutional investors won't touch this" — not because it's a bad investment, but because it's unfamiliar or embarrassing to own? That's where the category-creating returns are hiding. Get in before it has a name.
"The best returns come before the category has a name." — Thomas Braziel
11. Boring > Sexy Every Time
Scott Galloway
The more exciting an investment sounds at a dinner party, the worse it probably performs. "Cool" sectors attract hype, competition, and inflated valuations. Boring sectors are ignored, underpriced, and quietly compounding. Your return on invested capital is almost always inversely correlated to how sexy the industry sounds.
In Practice
Scott built and sold L2, a digital benchmarking software company, for 8x revenue. Not glamorous. His biggest wins came from analytics, benchmarking, and infrastructure.
Meanwhile Shaan Puri’s best-performing investment isn't AI or tech. It's retail shopping centers in San Francisco, compounding at roughly 44% a year.
Try This
Next time you feel genuinely excited about an investment because it sounds impressive, pause. Then ask: what's the boring version of this trade? What's the retail shopping center equivalent right now — unloved, overlooked, but quietly compounding? That's probably the better bet.
"Your return on your invested capital is inversely correlated to how sexy and cool an industry sounds." — Scott Galloway
12. Risk ≠ Uncertainty
Mohnish Pabrai
Uncertainty means you don't know what's going to happen. Risk means you could lose your capital permanently. A business can have enormous uncertainty about its future and very little actual risk of permanent loss. When Wall Street sees uncertainty, it prices the stock like there's risk. That's the mispricing.
In Practice
When shipping rates collapsed to $7k per day (below the $15k breakeven cost), the Frontline stock dropped 90%. Wall Street saw a company bleeding cash with no clear recovery timeline — the definition of uncertainty. But Pabrai saw something different:
The debt was non-recourse (tied to individual ships)
The liquidation value was $9-10 per share when the stock was trading at $3
Despite the uncertain future, the risk of permanent capital loss was minimal
When Frontline's shipping rates eventually recovered, Pabrai sold for an 80% gain in 8 months.
Try This
When evaluating beaten-down stocks, separately assess uncertainty (unpredictability of future outcomes) and risk (probability of permanent capital loss). Look specifically for situations with high uncertainty but where tangible assets, balance sheet strength, or other factors limit actual risk.
"Wall Street gets confused between risk and uncertainty. And where Wall Street gets confused between the two is where the greatest opportunities arise." — Mohnish Pabrai

Your Edge Is In What You Know
13. Invest in Your Own P&L
Shaan Puri
The products you already use and love are one of the best signals you have as an investor. You're a real customer. You know the product works. You know people are willing to pay for it. When you actually use a product every day, you have information that no analyst report captures. That's your edge.
In Practice
Shaan was an early user of Slack, Figma, Pager Duty, and Elastic Search — all of them showed up on his P&L before they were household names. He never invested in any of them. When he finally applied the lesson, he approached the founder of Shepherd, a staffing tool he was already using and paying for, and asked to invest. The business tripled in a year and sold for $52M.
Try This
Pull up your last three months of business or personal expenses. Circle every line item you'd be upset to lose. Those are the companies you actually believe in. Find out if any are publicly traded, have a crowdfunding round open, or are raising from angels. That's your shortlist.
"I would have made 100x more money had I simply looked at our expenses and our P&L and just knocked on their door and said, hey, can I invest?" — Shaan Puri
14. Invest in What You Can See From The Inside
Moiz Ali
The best informational edge you can have as an investor isn't a Bloomberg terminal. It's a front-row seat to how a business actually performs in the real world. Analysts study companies from the outside. Operators and customers experience them from the inside.
In Practice
When Facebook stock got crushed and dropped over 70%, Moiz and his brother kept buying. Not because of any macro thesis. Because Moiz was logging into 25 ad accounts every day and watching businesses pour money into Facebook ads. The platform wasn't dying. The stock was just hated.
His father did the same thing in 2008 with real estate. He’s just a guy who knows the value of houses in his own neighborhood. When prices collapsed, he bought one for $150k. Then another. One duplex he bought for $8k now generates $1,900 a month in rent.
Try This
Look at the products and platforms your own business (or businesses you work closely with) genuinely can't live without. If any of those companies are publicly traded and temporarily beaten down, you have an informational edge most investors don't. Use it.
15. Go an Inch Wide, a Mile Deep
Mohnish Pabrai
You don't need to understand everything. You need to understand a few things better than almost anyone else. The investors who narrow their focus to a specific domain and go deep on it have a structural advantage over anyone who wanders in from the outside. Your circle of competence doesn't need to be large. It just needs to be real.
In Practice
Billionaire John Arrillaga was “an inch wide and a mile deep." He knew the complete history of every building he invested in: when it was built, current value, rental rates, ownership changes, and market trends. He maintained minimal debt and aggressively bought during downturns when others were forced to sell. He didn't diversify into other locations or asset classes, maintaining his focus for decades.
Try This
Map your circle of competence by listing industries, business models, and investment types where you have genuine expertise or insight. Then deliberately narrow this list to just 1-2 areas where you can develop unmatched depth.
"John Arrillaga didn't understand most things, but he only invested in real estate within two miles of the Stanford campus." — Mohnish Pabrai
16. Read The Primary Documents
Guy Spier
The most important facts about any investment are in the primary document. Summaries filter out inconvenient details. They're written to make something digestible, which usually means something gets left out. The original documents contain exactly the information that makes or breaks the thesis. Reading them is uncomfortable and time-consuming, which is why most people don't do it. And that's why it's an edge.
In Practice
Guy Spier found a company he loved and bought it without downloading the actual mortgage-backed security filings. Bill Ackman did. When Ackman showed him that each "security" contained only a handful of loans rather than thousands, the entire diversification thesis collapsed. Guy sold it the same day. The answer was sitting in a publicly available document he simply hadn't read.
Mohnish Pabrai uses Value Investors Club as his personal Moody's Manual — a curated collection of investment theses from serious investors, available for free. One of his recent investments came from a write-up posted in 2021. Four years old. Still relevant and actionable. Great ideas don't expire quickly.
Try This
For every significant position you hold or are considering, identify the one primary document that contains the most important facts — the 10-K, the bond prospectus, the original filings. If you haven't read it, you don't actually know what you own. Create an account on Value Investors Club and commit to reading at least one analysis per day.

How to Read the Market
17. Know Where You Are in the Cycle
Howard Marks
You can't predict where markets are going, but you can almost always figure out where you are in a predictable cycle. Every cycle has the same fingerprints. Deals get done too easily. Nobody seems worried. Everyone at the party wants to talk stocks…
In Practice
In late 1999, Howard Marks read a history of financial speculation and noticed that what people did during the South Sea Bubble of 1720 looked exactly like what was happening in tech: people quitting their jobs to day trade, companies with no revenue trading at enormous valuations. He wrote his memo bubble.com on January 2nd, 2000. By mid-year, the bubble started collapsing.
The same pattern in 2005-2006: Marks kept walking to his partner's office with the Wall Street Journal saying "look at this piece of junk that got issued yesterday." He didn't know about subprime mortgages specifically. He just knew the world was being reckless. So he raised $11B before the crisis hit, not during it. By the time Lehman collapsed, he had dry powder ready to deploy $450M a week.
Try This
Build your own version of Howard's "Poor Man's Guide to Market Assessment." Are deals getting done easily or with difficulty? Are investing TV shows popular? Is everyone at parties talking about stocks? Are you hearing more about people getting rich or losing money? The answers tell you where you are in the cycle — and what to do next.
"We never know where we're going, but we sure as hell ought to know where we are." — Howard Marks
18. Watch What They Do When It's Hard
Sheel Mohnot
When something threatens a company's core business, ignore what management says. Watch what they ship. That's the real signal. Any CEO can sound confident on an earnings call. What they actually build next tells you whether they're playing offense or protecting turf. Companies that protect turf die slowly. Companies that ship toward the threat survive.
In Practice
Kodak invented the digital camera and buried it. Excite passed on buying Google for $750k because they wanted users stuck on their homepage watching ads. Both are gone. Google almost made the same mistake with AI. When Sundar looked like he might protect search revenue instead of going all-in on Gemini, the stock got punished. When he didn't flinch, it recovered.
Try This
Next time something threatens a company you hold (a competitor, a new technology, a shift in behavior, etc.), ignore the earnings call. Watch what they actually ship. If they're cutting costs and defending margins instead of building toward the threat, that's your exit signal.
19. The Biggest Risks Aren't In The Forecasts
Morgan Housel
The risks everyone is talking about are already priced in. The ones that will actually hurt you don't have a name yet.
Markets are good at pricing known risks, but terrible at pricing unknown ones. The three biggest economic events of the last 25 years — 9/11, Lehman Brothers, COVID — weren't in any forecast. Neither was Pearl Harbor. Neither was the collapse of the Soviet Union. The thing that actually reshapes everything always arrives unannounced.
Try This
This means two things for investors:
Stop spending so much time on the forecasts. The thing the pundits are debating loudly right now probably isn't the thing that will actually matter.
Build a portfolio that can survive the unknown shock, not one that's optimized for the known risks. The goal isn't to predict what will happen. It's to be financially unbreakable when something unexpected does.
"The biggest risk and the most important news story of the next 10 years will be something nobody is talking about today." — Morgan Housel
20. Find the Information Wall Street Ignores
Chris Camillo
Wall Street runs on transaction data: credit card receipts, analyst reports, earnings models. The edge is in the information they won't look for. Chris Camillo turned $20k into ~$75M in returns over 18 years by doing something Wall Street analysts genuinely won't do: reading TikTok comments at 11pm, watching consumers in Walgreens, and tracking Google searches for "roof repair" after hailstorms.
In Practice
Chris called the analyst covering Elf Cosmetics after a Jeffrey Star YouTube video drove mass sellouts of their product. 10M views. The analyst said: "Who's Jeffrey Star?" That was all he needed to know. He had an edge the professional investor didn't have and couldn't have. It was beneath them to look.
Try This
Invest at the point of information asymmetry, when you know something important that most investors don't. Sell when the information becomes consensus, and the crowd catches up.
"You don't have to be smart. You have to be smart in a totally different way than others." — Chris Camillo
21. Bet On What Never Changes
Morgan Housel
Predicting what will change is hard. Almost nobody does it consistently. Identifying what will never change is easier and far more valuable. Human nature hasn't changed in thousands of years. The desire for status, safety, convenience, and pleasure is as strong today as it was a century ago. Companies built on those foundations don't depend on any particular technology or trend holding up. They just need people to keep being people.
In Practice
In 2009, Buffett was driving through Omaha during the economic wreckage. His friend asked how the world recovers from this. Buffett asked: "Do you know what the best-selling candy bar was in 1962?" His friend said no. Buffett said Snickers. "Do you know what it is today?" Snickers. Point made.
Try This
Before committing to any long-term investment, ask: is this built on something about human nature that will still be true in 50 years? If the answer depends on a specific technology staying dominant or a trend continuing, it's fragile. If it's built on something people have always wanted and always will, it's durable.

Play The Long Game
22. It's an Infinite Game
Guy Spier
Investing has no finish line. The moment you start playing it like it does, you start making the wrong decisions.
A finite game, like chess, or football, has rules, a time limit, and a winner. An infinite game has no fixed endpoint — just players who stay in or drop out. When you treat investing like a finite game, you optimize for the wrong timeframe. You chase short-term performance. You abandon good strategies during bad stretches. You make moves designed to look smart rather than compound well.
In Practice
Guy Spier spent 8 years underperforming the S&P 500 within a 25-year track record that still beats it overall. If he'd been playing a finite game, judged by any single year or three-year stretch — he would have changed his strategy, blown up his edge, or quit. Instead he kept asking one question: what keeps me compounding? Not: how do I beat the index this quarter?
Try This
When evaluating your investment performance, widen the time frame. If you're checking your portfolio monthly and adjusting based on what's up or down, you're playing a finite game inside an infinite one. Set a review cadence that matches the actual game — annually at minimum, with the question being "am I still compounding?" not "did I beat the market this quarter?"
23. Operators Make Terrible Investors
Thomas Braziel
The skills that make a great operator are almost perfectly inverted from the skills that make a great investor.
Operating rewards action, speed, and decisiveness. Every good operator is wired to do something. Investing rewards the exact opposite — inaction, patience, and the ability to sit on your hands while nothing happens. The instincts that built your business will quietly destroy your portfolio if you let them run unchecked.
In Practice
Thomas Braziel watched a former Airbnb early employee (worth $80-100M) start investing for the first time in his 50s. His instinct was to be active, decisive, confident. To move fast and fix things. Every trait that made him exceptional as an operator worked against him as an investor.
Try This
If you're coming from an operating background and starting to invest, build in a forced delay before any trade. Write down the thesis. Wait 30 days. If you still believe it, act. The discomfort of inaction is the price of admission.
"As an entrepreneur, you're rewarded for taking action. As an investor, you get punished for taking too much action. All the money is made in the holding, the waiting, the observing." — Shaan Puri
24. Consistently Good > Occasionally Great
Howard Marks
The best return you can earn this year is not the goal. The best return you can sustain for the longest period of time is.
One terrible year can wipe out three or four good ones. The math doesn't forgive blowups. Compounding only works if you stay in the game. A strategy that earns 20% for five years and then blows up is worse than one that earns 10% for 30 years without interruption. Most investors know this. Almost none act like it.
In Practice
Howard Marks' client Dave Van Koten ran the General Mills pension fund for 14 years. Every single year, the portfolio finished between the 27th and 47th percentile. Never great. Never terrible. After 14 straight years of second-quartile performance, he ranked in the top 4% of all managers. Never once in the top quartile. Still top 4% overall.
PIMCO, one of the world's largest bond funds, had an internal philosophy they called "strategic mediocrity." In any given year, they were never trying to be in the top half of their peers. But in every decade, they were consistently in the top decile, because the peers who beat them in any single year eventually got washed out.
Try This
Look at your worst investing year. Ask two questions. First: what one rule change would have prevented that loss? Second: did that year happen because you were chasing higher returns than average? If yes, the fix isn't finding a better strategy. It's finding one you can actually hold through the bad years without bailing.
"If you never shoot yourself in the foot, consistently above average compounds to extraordinary results." — Howard Marks
25. Don't Aim for the Pin. Stay Out of the Gutters
Guy Spier
Of all the funds that existed when Guy Spier started investing, fewer than 2% still exist. Most didn't quietly underperform. They imploded. The survivors weren't necessarily the best stock pickers. They were the ones who never made a bet big enough to end the game.
Guy uses a bowling analogy: beginners think the goal is to hit the front pin. Really the goal is to keep the ball off the gutters. Because if you stay in the lane long enough, you'll eventually hit something. The real threat is the catastrophic loss that removes you from the game before compounding has time to work.
In Practice
Mohnish Pabrai bought IPSCO Steel at $45 per share. There was $15 per share in cash on the balance sheet. No debt. Hard contracts guaranteeing $15 per share in earnings for each of the next two years. Even if the business collapsed in year three, he already owned the plant and equipment for free. He made 3x.
Try This
Before making any investment, ask: what's the gutter scenario? If this goes to zero or down 80%, what does that do to my overall portfolio? If the answer is "it wrecks me," resize the position. The goal isn't to avoid all losses — it's to make sure no single loss ends the game for you.
"Set myself up such that when I miss really badly, I'm still going to be okay. That is more important than actually hitting on the targets." — Guy Spier
06
Get Out of Your Own Way
26. Don't Cut Your Flowers and Water Your Weeds
Mohnish Pabrai
Most investors instinctively sell winners (cut their flowers) while holding onto losers (watering their weeds). Truly exceptional businesses have much more potential than investors initially realize. While valuation matters, the quality of the underlying business matters far more when deciding whether to sell.
In Practice
Pabrai made this mistake when he sold Frontline after getting quick profit. The stock continued climbing to an astonishing 80x return as shipping rates soared from $15k to $300k per day. By selling what appeared "expensive," he missed life-changing returns.
Charlie Munger demonstrated this principle through his unwavering commitment to Costco. He recognized that Costco's extraordinary business model, with its fanatical customer loyalty and disciplined cost structure, would create value for decades regardless of short-term valuation concerns.
Try This
Create a "never sell" category in your portfolio for businesses with durable competitive advantages, strong management, and long growth runways. Establish criteria that would trigger a review (not necessarily a sale) based on fundamental business deterioration rather than valuation metrics.
"This is one of the mistakes I've made through most of my career — I have repeatedly sold good businesses because they appeared expensive and I was always wrong." — Mohnish Pabrai
27. The News Won't Help You Beat the Market
Sam & Shaan
Markets process publicly available information almost instantly. By the time you've read the headline, the trade is already gone. What the news actually does is trigger emotional reactions — buying on good news, selling on bad. That's the trap. More news exposure usually means more emotional trading, which means worse returns.
In Practice
Nassim Taleb once said: if you gave an investor tomorrow's news 24 hours early, he'd go bust within a year. Researchers tested it. They gave 118 finance-trained adults the actual front page of the Wall Street Journal one day in advance. Half lost money. One in six lost everything. Average gain: 3.2% — while the market returned 15% that same period. Even with the news, people predicted market direction correctly only 51% of the time. A coin flip.
They then brought in five actual pros: hedge fund managers, top bank traders. They finished up 130% on average. But they weren't that much better at calling direction (57% vs 51%). Their real edge:
They skipped one in three trading days entirely
They never bet big enough to blow themselves up
Try This
Next time you want to make a trade based on a news event — earnings, a Fed decision, a headline — ask one question: am I actually better than a coin flip here? If not, the smartest trade is no trade.
"He who lives by the crystal ball will die eating shattered glass." — Ray Dalio
28. Never Abandon What's Working
Chris Camillo
The most invisible way to destroy your compounding is to pull capital out of your best strategy and redeploy it into something worse.
Most people diversify out of discomfort, not logic. When one strategy is working exceptionally well, the instinct is to spread the gains around — into new asset classes, new strategies, new ideas. It feels prudent. But capital migrating from a 75% return vehicle into a 10% return vehicle isn't prudent. It's expensive.
In Practice
For 18 years, Chris Camillo compounded at roughly 75% annually in public markets through his observational investing method. But every year, he pulled the gains out and reinvested them into early-stage venture capital, where he earned average VC returns of 10-12%. If he'd left the money compounding in his best strategy, his portfolio would be approaching $1B. Instead, every year he was taking capital from what worked and parking it in what didn't.
Try This
Rank every strategy generating returns for you by actual performance. Ask honestly: am I pulling capital from the top performer to fund something that returns less? If yes, that's the leak to fix first. Diversification is a feature. Diversifying away from your edge is a bug.
"I finally came to terms with the fact that pulling money out of what was working was a really bad decision." — Chris Camillo
29. Automate Yourself Out
Haralabos Voulgaris
A good strategy run by an emotional human will underperform the same strategy on autopilot. The system is rarely the problem. The person running it is.
Your judgment degrades when you're stressed, distracted, or emotionally off. The strategy doesn't. The gap between what your system says to do and what you actually do is where most returns get destroyed.
In Practice
Haralabos Voulgaris built one of the most profitable sports betting systems in history. Then he noticed that on days when his personal life was a mess, his edge disappeared. The model was still right. He was making it wrong. His solution was full automation. No human override. When the model got something consistently wrong — like Oklahoma City totals — his rule was simple: make no changes. Let it run. The discipline wasn't in making better picks. It was in not touching the picks.
Try This
Write down your investing rules on a calm day. Automate everything you can — contributions, rebalancing, all of it. Then add one rule: if markets are volatile and you want to make a change, wait 30 days first. The goal is to make your future emotional self irrelevant to the outcome.
"My method works, but it only works if my mindset is good. If I'm upset, I'm making bad decisions. I wanted to automate me, basically." — Haralabos Voulgaris
30. Volatility Is a Gift
Cathie Wood
Most investors treat volatility as a threat. The best investors treat it as a buying tool. Price swings in a fundamentally sound business are just the market temporarily mispricing something you already understand. If your conviction in the underlying business hasn't changed, a lower price is strictly better for you. The only way volatility hurts you is if you react to it.
In Practice
ARK owns some of the most volatile stocks in the market — Tesla, Coinbase, Palantir. When Tesla ran from $100 to $500, it became 13-14% of the portfolio. Cathie trimmed. When it pulled back due to Elon drama or macro fears, she bought back. The position rarely left the top slot.
She bought Nvidia at 20 cents (adjusted) in 2014 when everyone called it a PC gaming chip company. She held through years of indifference, trimmed on the way up after ChatGPT, and rotated into Coinbase while the SEC was actively suing it.
Try This
For any high-conviction position you hold, define in advance: at what price would I add more? At what price would I trim? Having that framework removes the emotional decision-making when volatility hits, and turns drawdowns into scheduled buying opportunities rather than panic events.
31. Buy When You Don't Want To
Howard Marks
The conditions that make buying feel most dangerous are the same conditions that make prices cheapest. You have to hold both of those things in your head at the same time.
Fear suppresses prices. Prices eventually recover. The investor who buys during fear captures the recovery. The one who waits for certainty buys at the top. Certainty and cheap prices don't exist at the same time.
In Practice
Marks raised $11B for distressed debt before the 2008 crisis, because you can't raise money during a crisis. When he tried to raise $3.5B in early 2007, clients were so eager they gave him $8B. By September 2008, when Lehman collapsed and the fund had 12% deployed, his logic was simple: "If I invest it and the world melts down, it doesn't matter what we did. But if I don't invest in it and the world doesn't melt down, we didn't do our job." He deployed $450M a week for 15 straight weeks.
Try This
Write down now, before the next crash: at what point would you be willing to buy more of what you already own? Name a specific price or drawdown percentage. Having that number in advance means the decision is already made when panic hits — and you won't have to feel brave to act on it.
07
Common Traps
32. Cheap Isn't Enough
Thomas Braziel
The biggest trap in value investing is confusing cheap with good. Something can be deeply discounted and still be worth nothing.
The discount is psychologically compelling. When you see something trading at 10 cents that used to be worth $200M, the brain screams opportunity. But the discount doesn't tell you what it's actually worth today. A bad asset at a 90% discount is still a bad asset.
In Practice
Thomas calls it fool's gold — it looks like a deal until you realize the underlying asset was never going to recover. His rule: you don't buy handbags on Canal Street because they're cheap. You buy them on Madison Avenue when they're discounted. The target is always a real asset temporarily mispriced — not a fake asset at any price. A great business in a bad situation is the opportunity. A bad business in a bad situation is a trap dressed up as one.
Try This
For any beaten-down investment, ask one question before anything else: if this company had zero debt and no legal problems, would I want to own it? If the answer is no, the discount is irrelevant. Move on.
"Don't buy handbags on Canal Street because those are all fake. You buy them on Madison Avenue when they're discounted." — Thomas Braziel
33. True Diversification Is Hard
Scott Galloway
Most portfolios that look diversified aren't. When institutions get scared, they sell everything at once. That's when you find out your diversified portfolio all moves in the same direction.
Diversification isn't just owning different things. It's owning things that don't react to the same events at the same time. US stocks, international stocks, real estate, and bonds all feel different on a normal day. In a genuine crisis, most of them correlate. Real diversification means finding income streams that are structurally uncorrelated — not just superficially different.
In Practice
Ray Dalio's version of this: the key is to find 12 positive income streams that are genuinely uncorrelated. Not 12 things that feel different. 12 things that move independently of each other. Scott has watched concentrated bets blow up in real time — twice. His current portfolio spans US and international stocks, real estate across multiple countries, and private investments. Not because it's more interesting. Because he's learned what actual diversification costs when you don't have it.
Try This
List every income-generating asset you own and ask: would these all go down at the same time in a market panic? If the answer is mostly yes, you're not actually diversified. Find one asset class that genuinely doesn't correlate with your others. Real estate, commodities, and international bonds are common starting points.
34. Your Fund Is Probably Just an Expensive Index
Cathie Wood
Most actively managed funds charge active fees while quietly hugging the index. You're paying for conviction you're not getting.
Once benchmarking became the standard, fund managers stopped thinking in absolute terms and started thinking in relative ones. The question shifted from "what's the best investment?" to "how far can I deviate from the benchmark without getting fired?" The answer to that second question is: not very far. So most active funds end up looking a lot like the index they're supposedly beating.
In Practice
Cathie watched this happen across the industry in real time. The entire asset management world converged on the same mega-cap positions — not because of independent conviction, but because the benchmark made it the path of least resistance. A fund that's "underweight" a stock they own plenty of is considered short that stock. The incentive isn't to find the future. It's to not deviate too far from what everyone else owns.
Try This
Look at your actively managed fund's top 10 holdings and compare them to the S&P 500. If they look nearly identical, you're paying active fees for passive performance. Either switch to a true low-cost index fund or find a manager who actually deviates from the benchmark with a clear thesis. There's no point paying for active management that isn't active.
"The pendulum swung toward passive and benchmark-sensitive investing. If you go passive, it's a self-fulfilling prophecy." — Cathie Wood
35. Advisors Work for Themselves
Moiz Ali
Your wealth manager's job is not to grow your money. It's to keep your money under management.
They earn fees on assets under management whether you win or lose. Every recommendation they make — every product they pitch, every reallocation they suggest — runs through that filter first. Their incentive is asset retention. Not your returns.
In Practice
Moiz went through Goldman Sachs and multiple other wealth management firms. Same conclusion every time. His test: "If I told my Goldman wealth manager that Bill Gates would give me a billion dollars tomorrow if I loaned him this pen, they'd say don't do that deal. Give us the pen instead." His solution after firing all of them: VOO and short-term US treasuries. No advisor. No fees. No products. The extra 1% yield chasing isn't worth the cost — financial or psychological.
Try This
Ask your wealth manager one question: how much of your own money is in the same investments you're recommending to me? If the answer is none, or it's different for my situation, you have your answer. Then look at what you're actually paying in fees annually. Multiply that by 20 years. That number will clarify things quickly.

Plays You Can Steal
Score Every Company Across 6 Stakeholders
Ryan Peterson's framework: A company where one stakeholder is losing is accumulating risk. That risk may not show up in the numbers today, but it's a powder keg.
Airbnb: Guests love it, homeowners love it, investors have done great, employees seem happy. Regulators are lukewarm. The community is the weak score — neighbors don't want party houses next door. That's a real vulnerability in the business model that sophisticated investors should weigh.
Go through almost any company and someone's usually losing. Any F-grade stakeholder is a risk that will eventually surface.
Buy the Steak, Get the Sizzle for Free
This is the core framework behind every good distressed trade.
In Mount Gox: Bitcoin was at $300, Thomas bought claims for $80 per Bitcoin.
The steak was the known 25 cents of recovery already sitting in the estate.
The sizzle was if Bitcoin went up or they found more.
Original investors made 40x.
In FTX: claims were trading at 20 cents.
The steak was ~30 cents in hard assets
The sizzle was hundreds of millions in crypto holdings that could appreciate.
Scott Galloway turned $2M into ~$15M.
The principle works because you're not betting on the upside — you're buying the downside protection so cheaply that the upside is essentially free.
One Final Rule
Almost all of your lifetime returns will come from a tiny number of decisions. Most investments will be average or worse. A small handful will be extraordinary. The mistake most people make is sizing every position equally, as if every idea has equal potential. They don't. The best opportunities are rare. Treating them as rare — and waiting for them — is what separates great investors from busy ones.
