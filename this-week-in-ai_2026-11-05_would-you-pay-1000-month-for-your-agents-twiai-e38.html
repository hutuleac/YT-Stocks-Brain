Flat-rate agent plans are breaking, and the panel says pay more

Always-on agents burn far more compute than subscription revenue covers, so labs are throttling and the panel expects higher prices.

  • Grokbot subscribers saw weekly usage meters hit 100%, some while bots sat idle; Musk said it was not a glitch and cost was unsustainable. Two weeks earlier on SpaceX's Q3 call he said he had never seen anything grow this fast.
  • Anthropic and OpenAI have also pulled back from all-you-can-eat plans; Goldman Sachs limited Claude Code and GitHub Copilot to engineers only after a productivity and risk review, cutting off bankers and traders.
  • Spend range: Garry Tan says $100-200K a year on tokens; Jason ~$40K a year for ~20 people (~$200 each) and claims $2M+ of software value; Nick runs 10-20 always-on agents and has spent $10-20K in a week; Peter Morales spent $100K in a couple of weeks building software.
  • Willingness to pay: Jason $1,000/month; Mark Jeffrey $1,000 (now $200-300); Nick ~$500 personal. Jason's ladder: $200 plan at half today's token allowance, plus a $500 tier.
  • Peter Morales's firm is moving staff to on-prem compute and self-hosted models, driven by token spend and the fear of a frontier price jump.
  • Jason's analogy: the Uber/Lyft price war ran close to a decade before Uber's funding outlasted Lyft. With Anthropic and OpenAI going public, he expects pressure for a path to profitability.
  • Jason's theory: Anthropic hasn't shipped its bot product because it is compute constrained and would hit the same all-you-can-eat wall.

Bittensor subnets as miner-funded compute that skips the debt

Mark Jeffrey argues Bittensor shifts data-center capex and risk onto worldwide miners, so compute and inference are cheaper than CoreWeave-style rentals.

  • Subnet 51 (Liam) sells raw machines pay-as-you-go with no KYC or contract; miners worldwide meet performance thresholds, pay for power and hardware and carry the risk. Ownership of a subnet is tied to the TAO token.
  • Subnet 53 (NGI) and Targon sit one layer up and provide inference; renting from Liam compounds the cost savings at each layer. Mark says NGI offers inference at a fraction of CoreWeave's cost.
  • Capacity: it could not run Grokbot today, but Mark says it scales elastically because rising usage lifts miner rewards and attracts more supply, with a warm-up lag.
  • Suppliers range from data centers to individuals' home machines. Mark runs Hermes agent on NGI inference and also uses Grokbot for ease of use.
  • Subnet 95 (actual computer) clusters home Macs for inference and lets owners rent spare capacity back to the network.
  • Mark built a subnet bubble chart (market cap, volume, 30-day change, weekly or monthly, USD or TAO) and opened it to the public.

Lenders pull back from neoclouds as AI stocks sell off

Debt markets cooled on GPU clouds right as chip and software names dropped; Nick Harris reads it as a mood swing, not a broken thesis.

  • Friday Oct 30: Intel, AMD, Dell and Nvidia fell and the semiconductor index dropped 7.2%; some outlets called it Black Friday for chip stocks.
  • WSJ that night: CoreWeave, Nebius and NScale had effectively been shut out of the bond market in recent weeks as lenders demanded tougher terms.
  • Musk replied to Chamath that debt markets had almost entirely shut off access for neoclouds and that the 200+ neoclouds face guppy-level death and consolidation.
  • Spreading squeeze: Oracle fell 8.8% on Monday, Meta is marketing $20B of bonds, Shopify sank on its first revenue miss since 2022.
  • Peter Morales: expect more efficiency work and local models, citing someone running GLM 5.3 locally. Nick Harris: debt lenders are risk-averse and a high risk-reward buildout invites this; some holders are taking profits.
  • Mark Jeffrey (via the All-In discussion): flags the mismatch between borrowing for data centers and the contracts CoreWeave earns from.

Colossus hits ~1.1M GPUs and the bill has to be recouped through pricing

Jason says Musk told him the xAI buildout is far bigger than assumed, so prices must rise and tiered pricing will follow.

  • Per Jason's conversation with Musk (and a tweet): over a million GPUs, 1.11M chips, power from ~300 MW to ~1 GW, capital cost from $13B to $36B.
  • Jason expects Google and Meta-style price discrimination: tweak value up for some users, give others free tiers and charge what each will pay, like an ad network.
  • Peter Morales: not all tokens are equal. Heavyweight reasoning models trained on Colossus-class systems are for hard problems such as formal-methods proofs; a booking agent can run partly on a phone.
  • Nick Harris: AI is improving as expected and breakthroughs should come soon; he cites Fields medalists saying they feel obsolete.

Photonic interconnect claims and the fight over who owns DRAM

Nick Harris says interconnect is the bottleneck lever; Altman's remark that home memory is wasted drew pushback and a case that memory demand never ends.

  • Nick Harris (Light Matter, CEO): the same gigawatt data center can train a model about 3x faster (a month instead of three) and serve about 2x the tokens per second, using photonic interconnect.
  • His thesis: computing is now very big chips plus ultra-fast low-latency links, because electronic scaling is ending. Light Matter has won an industry best-paper award two years running and published a Nature paper on a photonic computer; hardware ships next year.
  • Altman on the Stripe Cheeky Pike podcast with John Collison (posted Oct 29): it is globally terrible for everyone to hold the world's limited DRAM at home, which sits idle ~95% of the time; maximum utilization belongs in the cloud.
  • Mark Jeffrey rejects ceding sovereignty to the cloud. Peter Morales says 5% is outdated as agents now keep local machines busy.
  • A panelist's volumetric-memory argument: brains store in 3D connections while HBM is sheets of cells, so a population of AIs the size of humanity needs far more memory and DRAM stays short as AI scales; Jevons paradox applies.

What people built with agents in a few hours

Non-programmers now ship custom dashboards, scrapers and browser extensions, and the panel thinks the mainstream hasn't noticed.

  • Jason's Austin home search: agents mine YouTube, TikTok and Instagram for off-MLS listings, reverse-image-search them, build broker/architect/builder cards and email a weekly dossier to his wife; Grokbot even bought a domain and put the site behind a password. Under an hour of his work over 3-4 weeks.
  • Jason's Chrome extension "Unsponsored" removes sponsored Amazon listings: about 6 minutes and 30-40,000 output tokens.
  • Nick Harris: a live dashboard of Light Matter's manufacturing flow, from foundry to packaging to rack, by project, customer and due date. Jason's team builds dashboards in Lovable on a Notion backend.
  • Lon Harris's "bingebot" sends a weekly list of new streaming titles from his past top-10 lists; Nick grades Jason's AI usage a solid B (the 80s out of 100).
  • UX gap: the panel wants bots to share what they know about your taste instead of repeating it. Peter Morales has a bot write deployable code for any task he'd repeat more than once or twice.
  • Jason: the last 100 days eclipsed the first thousand days after ChatGPT, so the next 100 will eclipse them again.

AI proof credit fight and chatbots sourcing election answers from AI-built sites

Two trust stories: a disputed claim that OpenAI front-ran mathematicians, and a Washington Post review showing chatbots lean on three small AI-built voter sites.

  • Mark Jeffrey's cynical telling: mathematicians were nearing a human-written Navier-Stokes proof with an AI product when OpenAI announced its computer solved it; the allegation is that it benefited from their prompts. Mark says it was messy and "who knows if it's true."
  • Peter Morales relays Terence Tao: the age of heroes in mathematics is over. Nick Harris's counter: now superheroes. Domain experts' questions are their moat.
  • WaPo review of thousands of chatbot answers: ChatGPT, Copilot and others lean on Ballot Brief, Smarter.vote and Decode the Vote. ChatGPT cited Ballot Brief in 22% of election answers; Copilot in 75% (680,000+ referrals in 30 days).
  • Ballot Brief had errors: it credited a senator with backing a bill passed before they took office and merged two California Robert Garcias into one profile.
  • Gemini declined to answer 2024 election questions in the US and India, as shown by a Google announcement read on air.
  • Prediction markets: a panelist speculates large money could distort Polymarket and Kalshi but says they are just guessing; a fake survey firm got into poll aggregators.

Names and terms at a glance

Glossary & Entities

Neocloud — A GPU-focused cloud provider such as CoreWeave, Nebius or NScale, typically funded with debt.
Subnet (Bittensor) — A Bittensor network where miners compete for token rewards providing a service such as compute or inference.
Formal methods — Proving mathematically that software satisfies defined properties, used for mission-critical code.
Jevons paradox — When efficiency gains raise total consumption of a resource instead of lowering it.
